In Santiago del Estero, in Córdoba, and across much of the Gran Chaco shared by Argentina, Paraguay, and Bolivia, there is a recipe passed down without a recipe book: arrope de chañar. It is made by boiling the fruit of *Geoffroea decorticans* — a green-barked, thorny tree that withstands extreme drought — for hours, until it becomes a dark, thick, sweet syrup that rural families use as a home remedy for coughs, bronchitis, and winter colds. For decades it was just that: forest knowledge, passed from one generation to the next with no backing beyond accumulated experience. Today, preclinical trials published in peer-reviewed journals have confirmed that the syrup carries real, laboratory-measurable pharmacological activity. And as science puts a name to what these communities already knew, the forest that produces the raw material keeps shrinking year after year.

## A Syrup That Is Cooked, Not Manufactured

Making arrope is an artisanal, demanding process. According to accounts gathered from producers in the Atamisqui area of Santiago del Estero, the traditional preparation requires 15 to 20 hours of cooking over open firewood. First, fallen chañar fruit is collected and cured with ash to prevent it from being spoiled by insects; then it is boiled until the pulp separates from the pit, left to cool, mashed by hand, strained, and boiled again until it reaches the consistency of a thick syrup, similar to honey or molasses. The yield is low: 50 liters of juice produce only 6 or 7 liters of finished arrope. According to Slow Food International, which listed arrope de chañar in its Ark of Taste catalog of foods at risk of disappearing, similar ratios — roughly 3 liters of syrup for every 5 kilograms of fruit — hold across other arid regions where the tree grows, from central and northern Argentina to Chile's Atacama region.

The use is not limited to the fruit. The bark of the chañar tree, prepared as a decoction, is another traditional remedy for coughs and colds — a different preparation with the same goal: relieving respiratory symptoms with what the forest provides.

## What Science Confirmed, and What It Hasn't

It is worth being precise here, because there is a meaningful difference between what tradition has held for generations and what scientific evidence has actually demonstrated so far.

A research team from the Faculty of Biochemistry, Chemistry and Pharmacy at the National University of Tucumán — made up of Adrián Reynoso, Nancy Vera, María Eugenia Aristimuño, Adriana Daud, and Alicia Sánchez Riera — carried out preclinical trials using extracts of both raw chañar fruit and finished arrope, both sourced in Icaño, Santiago del Estero, specifically to check whether the artisanal cooking process preserved the properties of the raw fruit. The results, published in the *Journal of Ethnopharmacology* and the *Journal of Nutrition & Food Sciences*, were obtained by administering the extract to laboratory rats — a dose of 1,000 milligrams per kilogram of body weight over three to seven days — and tracking indicators of pain, inflammation, cough, and expectoration.

The raw fruit showed expectorant, antitussive, anti-inflammatory, and analgesic activity. The cooked arrope retained the first three properties but lost much of its anti-inflammatory effect during the prolonged boiling process — a relevant finding, since it shows that the traditional preparation is not pharmacologically neutral: cooking modifies, not merely concentrates, the fruit's active compounds. Reynoso explained that chañar's analgesic effect works at the level of the central nervous system, through a mechanism he compared to "an opioid-type analgesic, like morphine."

It is important not to overstate these findings. These are preclinical trials in animal models, not clinical studies in humans, and not doses equivalent to a household spoonful of arrope. What the research establishes is that there is a genuine pharmacological basis behind the traditional use — not a placebo effect or an unsupported folk claim — and that this basis merits continued study. The distinction matters: laboratory evidence of biological activity is one thing; clinical validation of a specific dose and therapeutic use is another. Chañar has the former. It does not yet have the latter.

## A Shrinking Forest

Chañar does not grow in pots or commercial plantations: it is part of the native forest of the Gran Chaco, one of the largest forested ecoregions in South America after the Amazon, and one of the fastest disappearing. Santiago del Estero is among the Argentine provinces with the highest deforestation rate in the country. In the first half of 2024 alone, 21,047 hectares were cleared there, according to a survey by Greenpeace Argentina that identified silvopastoral livestock ranching and the advance of the agricultural frontier — soy, cotton, and pastureland — as the leading causes of deforestation in the province.

That loss of forest is already translating into less fruit. Jessica Orellana, an arrope producer in the town of Tasigasta, describes the change with a simple comparison: a chañar tree that once yielded twenty fruits now yields barely five. The reason, she explains, is the loss of soil moisture and the general weakening of native vegetation as the forest becomes fragmented. Some producers now resort to gathering fallen fruit from urban yards and gardens, because trees in the forest itself are producing less and less.

Water is the other side of the same problem. In the Atamisqui area, household water supply can be limited to about 4,000 liters a month for a family of five and their livestock — a volume that barely lasts 25 days. Other producers, like Liliana Juárez, report that well drilling that historically reached 3 to 5 meters deep now must go down 7 or 8 meters, and that the water recovered is often saline. Chañar is a tree adapted to aridity, but the combination of deforestation and extreme water stress is eroding even the hardiest species of the Chaco forest.

## The Economy That Sustains the Recipe

Arrope de chañar survives today thanks to an organized rural economy, not a spontaneous one. The Unión de Trabajadores de la Tierra (Union of Land Workers, UTT) and the Asociación de Familias con Identidad Hortícola bring together producers across the region and sustain marketing spaces, such as the UTT's own store in Villa Atamisqui, where arrope has gone from being a strictly household food to a product sold by the jar, priced between 3,000 and 6,000 pesos depending on the area.

That organizing has also made visible work that was historically invisible: making arrope, alongside weaving and gathering wild herbs, was for generations the task of women classified simply as "housewives." Integration into cooperative spaces has, in recent years, given them explicit recognition as agricultural producers, even as the distribution of domestic labor within households remains unequal. Gabriela Pajón, who heads a cooperative representing 60 families, warned further that the recent dismantling of family-farming support policies has left producers without the institutional backing that once made it easier to obtain food-safety permits and sanitary registration to sell their products formally.

Chañar and its syrup condense, in a single fruit, a tension that runs through much of the Chaco forest: traditional knowledge that science is only now beginning to confirm through rigorous methods, sustained by a subsistence economy that endures with scarce resources, and threatened by a production model advancing over the very land that makes the remedy possible in the first place.
