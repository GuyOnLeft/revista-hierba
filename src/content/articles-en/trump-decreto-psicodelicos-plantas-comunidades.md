On April 18, 2026, President Donald Trump signed the executive order *Accelerating Medical Treatments for Serious Mental Illness*. The order directs the DEA, in coordination with the FDA, to establish a federal pathway for access to investigational psychedelics under the Right to Try Act; to reduce the restrictions that hamper research; and to initiate reviews of products upon completion of Phase 3 clinical trials for potential rescheduling.

Same-day commentary from Harvard Law School's Petrie-Flom Center, in a Q&A with I. Glenn Cohen and Mason Marks, observes that the order explicitly names **ibogaine** — twice — allocates 50 million dollars for state-federal collaboration on research, and creates FDA Commissioner's National Priority Vouchers for eligible psychedelic drugs.

What the order does not say is where these plants come from.

## The plants behind the order

The ibogaine named in the order is the active principle of *Tabernanthe iboga*, a West African shrub whose plant does not reach the Americas. But the regulatory category that the order reorganizes — Schedule I psychedelics — is populated, in its overwhelming majority, by plants and mushrooms whose medicinal knowledge was built by indigenous peoples of Latin America:

- **Peyote** (*Lophophora williamsii*) is a cactus of the Chihuahuan Desert and southern Texas, central to the spirituality of the Wixárika (Huichol) peoples of Mexico for at least five thousand years. Its active principle, mescaline, is on the U.S. federal Schedule I.
- **Ayahuasca** — the ritual preparation that combines the *Banisteriopsis caapi* vine with leaves of *Psychotria viridis* or other chacrunas — is the traditional medicine of approximately one hundred Amazonian indigenous peoples distributed across Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, Brazil, and Venezuela. Its psychoactive component, DMT, is on Schedule I.
- **Psilocybin mushrooms** (*Psilocybe* spp., among them *Psilocybe cubensis* and *Psilocybe mexicana*) sustain the healing practices of the Mazatec communities of Oaxaca — a tradition that María Sabina brought to global attention in the 1950s and for which she was, herself, persecuted by her own community when that attention turned into extractive tourism. Psilocybin is on Schedule I.

These plants are not raw materials. They are nodes of knowledge accumulated in concrete territories, sustained by concrete communities, against centuries of colonial pressure, forced evangelization, criminalization, and commodification.

## What the order does, and what it omits

The executive order, according to the Petrie-Flom reading, does three things technically:

First, it opens a Right to Try pathway for patients with serious illness to access investigational psychedelics ahead of full FDA approval. Mason Marks notes that ibogaine's inclusion is legally contestable — arguably, it has not "met basic safety requirements" as the Right to Try Act requires.

Second, it lowers federal barriers to research with Schedule I substances, aligning with a rescheduling process the authors note was already contemplated by statute, with a 90-day window after FDA approval.

Third, it allocates resources: 50 million dollars for state-federal collaboration on psychedelic research, and the creation of National Priority Vouchers to accelerate review of eligible drugs. Cohen observes that the order's language suggests the White House views these as "the appropriate drugs" for such priorities.

What the order does not address is equally significant. It does not mention the religious use of psychedelics — the peyote churches, the União do Vegetal and Santo Daime congregations that use ayahuasca as a sacrament, do not appear in the text. It does not address indigenous ceremonial use. It does not mention any non-therapeutic access pathway. The regulatory framework being built is strictly medical, strictly pharmaceutical, strictly individual.

## What is at stake in Latin America

This omission is not neutral. When a state regulates a plant without recognizing the tradition the plant comes from, the historically predictable effect is appropriation: the plant becomes a pharmaceutical input, the active principle gets patented, the supply chains are industrialized, and the community that for generations identified, cultivated, and learned the plant receives no recognition, no consultation, and no income.

For the Wixárika communities of Real de Catorce, in San Luis Potosí — where the peyote pilgrimage sustains Wixárika cosmology and where the plant itself is under pressure from mining and psychedelic tourism — a U.S. Right to Try pathway increases demand without increasing protection. For the Shipibo-Conibo, Asháninka, Kichwa, and Cofán communities that sustain the ceremonial practice of ayahuasca, the risk is structurally the same: a northern pharmaceutical industry that isolates DMT, synthesizes it, patents it, and leaves those who for centuries tended the plants in the role of informal suppliers to a market that excludes them.

The institutional response does not have to be binary. The medicinal regulation of psychedelics is, in many ways, a historical correction against decades of prohibition that blocked research. The problem is not that they are being regulated. The problem is that they are being regulated without recognizing the source of the knowledge.

International instruments such as the Nagoya Protocol, ILO Convention 169, and the Convention on Biological Diversity establish the principle of free, prior, and informed consent for any commercial use of genetic resources tied to traditional knowledge. The April 18 executive order mentions none of these frameworks. The FDA, the DEA, and the pharmaceutical laboratories that will benefit from the new pathway are not bound to consult them. That is the asymmetry.

## A question the region will have to ask

While Washington builds its framework, the South has to decide where it stands. Will Wixárika communities have a voice in how the cactuses that end up in U.S. pharmaceutical capsules are harvested? Will Amazonian indigenous federations be part of decisions about who can synthesize and commercialize ayahuasca derivatives? Will the governments of Mexico, Peru, Brazil, and Colombia demand benefit-sharing mechanisms for the communities that have been sustaining this knowledge for centuries?

Trump's order raises none of these questions. But the plants it regulates were not born in laboratories. They were born — and continue being born — in concrete indigenous territories. That is the unfinished work this week reopened.
