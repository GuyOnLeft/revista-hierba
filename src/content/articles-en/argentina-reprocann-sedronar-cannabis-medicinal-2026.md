In a small apartment in Greater Buenos Aires, a mother keeps a folder of medical records, court filings, and printouts of a government registration number that has not changed in over a year, even as the agency behind it has. Her daughter's epilepsy treatment depends on cannabis oil that Argentine law has permitted since 2017. What changed this year was not the medicine. It was who, inside the state, decides whether she gets to keep using it.

On January 26, 2026, Argentina's government published Decree 27/2026 in the Boletín Oficial, transferring administrative control of the National Medical Cannabis Program — best known by the acronym of its patient registry, REPROCANN — from the Health Ministry to SEDRONAR, the Secretaría de Políticas Integrales sobre Drogas de la Nación Argentina. The move did not touch Law 27,350, the 2017 statute that created a right to therapeutic cannabis access. But it did something that patient organizations and legal scholars argue matters just as much: it placed the daily governance of that right inside an agency built to manage addiction and narcotics control, not medicine.

For a country widely cited across Latin America as a reference point for patient-driven cannabis reform, the reorganization has become a live test of a basic question hovering over medical cannabis programs region-wide — whether legalizing therapeutic use is enough if the bureaucracy that administers it still treats patients as a public-safety category.

## What the Decree Actually Does

SEDRONAR long predates this cannabis assignment. Originally founded as the Secretaría de Programación para la Prevención de la Drogadicción y la Lucha contra el Narcotráfico, it lost its anti-narcotics enforcement functions to the Security Ministry in 2014 and has since focused on addiction prevention and treatment, running Argentina's 141 national helpline for problematic substance use and a research arm, the Observatorio Argentino de Drogas. It remains formally housed within the Health Ministry's structure, which the government has cited as evidence the move is a technical reorganization rather than a policy reversal, according to reporting by the trade outlet Cáñamo.

Under Decree 27/2026, SEDRONAR now handles registration, monitoring, and evaluation of REPROCANN, the registry that authorizes patients, home cultivators, "third-party cultivators" (formerly called "solidarity cultivators," under stricter 2025 rules), and civil associations to grow and use cannabis for documented medical conditions — epilepsy, autism spectrum disorder, chronic pain, and others. SEDRONAR is also tasked with research on cannabis-based treatments alongside what regulatory text describes as "non-conventional treatments," according to reporting by the outlet Agenda Cannabis.

The government has not published an extended public rationale beyond the decree text itself. According to multiple outlets that reviewed the measure, including Cáñamo and the independent outlet PelaGatos, officials have suggested — without elaborating publicly in detail — that consolidating oversight of psychoactive-substance-adjacent programs under one technical structure could, in principle, improve monitoring and evidentiary standards. What is verifiable is narrower: the decree amends the organizational chart set by Decree 50/2019 and does not repeal or modify Law 27,350.

## A Backlog That Predates — and Now Complicates — the Handover

The institutional change did not create Argentina's REPROCANN backlog, but it landed in the middle of one. According to reporting compiled by the outlet La Bulla and corroborated by coverage in Revista THC and the San Juan-based outlet Sanjuan8, more than 80,000 registration and renewal applications were pending as the transfer took effect. Average processing times had stretched from roughly 21 days in 2022 to more than 180 days by 2025, while approval rates fell from about 96% to below 50%, according to figures reported by La Bulla. Estimates of the total population affected — active users, expired permits, and first-time applicants — range from roughly 180,000 to 300,000 people depending on the outlet, a discrepancy that reflects how hard it has become to get a clean official count.

Courts have already begun weighing in. According to reporting reviewed by Sanjuan8, a federal court ordered the Health Ministry to pay 3,089,160 pesos in legal costs over what the ruling described as an unjustified delay that "forced the plaintiff to resort to courts" to obtain a registration the law already entitled her to. Separately, outlets including Cáñamo have reported that more than 1,500 judicial appeals had been filed nationally over REPROCANN delays even before the SEDRONAR transfer was finalized.

SEDRONAR has since rolled out a partial fix. Beginning in February 2026, the agency introduced an automated approval track for what it classifies as "low-risk" applications — cases where the prescribing physician's credentials are validated, the patient is over 21, there are no complex psychiatric conditions on file, and identity data matches the national RENAPER database — issuing certificates within 24 hours where those conditions are met, according to reporting by PelaGatos and Revista THC. Cases involving minors or mental health considerations remain under manual review, and the rollout has proceeded gradually rather than resolving the backlog outright.

## Medicine or Suspicion? The Argument Patient Groups Are Making

The sharpest public criticism has come from Valeria Salech, president of Mamá Cultiva Argentina, one of the country's most visible cannabis-patient advocacy organizations, founded by mothers of children with treatment-resistant epilepsy. Salech has described the SEDRONAR transfer as "una forma de exclusión encubierta" — a form of concealed exclusion — arguing that community organizations and family cultivators have for years filled gaps the formal health and pharmaceutical system did not, particularly for low-income families who cannot afford imported cannabis-derived medication, according to reporting on her remarks by La Bulla.

The underlying concern, echoed across the Argentine cannabis press, is definitional: Law 27,350 frames cannabis access as a medical right grounded in physician prescription and informed consent. SEDRONAR's institutional mandate, by contrast, is built around managing "problematic substance use" — a framework historically applied to addiction, not to patients following a doctor's treatment plan. Critics argue that folding REPROCANN into that structure risks recasting registered patients, even implicitly, as a population to be monitored for risk rather than treated for a condition. Requirements introduced in 2025 and still in force — mandatory specialized medical credentials for prescribing physicians and criminal background checks for third-party cultivators and NGO members — have reinforced that reading among advocacy groups, according to reporting by Sanjuan8 and Revista THC.

It is worth being precise about what is opinion here and what is documented. That SEDRONAR's mandate centers on addiction and substance-control policy is a matter of public record. That this reclassification will functionally worsen patient outcomes is, so far, an argument advanced by advocates and sympathetic outlets rather than a conclusion established by independent evaluation — no government or academic assessment of the transfer's real-world effects on patients has yet been published. The backlog numbers and processing-time figures, however, are independently corroborated across multiple Argentine outlets and are not in dispute.

## A Regional Signal, Not Just a Local One

Argentina's REPROCANN, launched in 2020, has functioned as a reference model elsewhere in Latin America precisely because it combined a health-based legal right with a relatively accessible administrative registry — a contrast to Brazil, where families relied on individual habeas corpus rulings for the same protection until recently, or Peru, where patients describe access to imported medical cannabis as prohibitively expensive. Housing that registry inside a drug-control secretariat, even one that no longer carries enforcement powers, will be watched by patient organizations elsewhere in the region as a test of how administrative architecture — not just legislative text — decides whether a medical cannabis right functions as one in practice.

The change also arrives amid broader health-budget austerity under President Javier Milei's government. SEDRONAR itself absorbed a roughly 30% cut to resources earmarked for addressing problematic substance use in 2026, according to reporting alongside coverage of Health Minister Mario Lugones's tenure. Whether an agency with tighter resources, built for a different mission, can also administer a medical rights registry efficiently is the question Argentine patients, courts, and advocacy groups are now testing — one renewal application at a time.
