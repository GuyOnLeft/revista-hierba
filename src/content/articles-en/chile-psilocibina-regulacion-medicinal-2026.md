In Chile, under Law 20,000 and Supreme Decree No. 405 of 1983, psilocybin — the psychoactive compound found in mushrooms of the genus *Psilocybe* — remains classified at the same level as trafficking-grade narcotics. At the same time, under the umbrella of that same Ministry of Health, a team of researchers is running the country's first approved clinical protocol using psychedelics: a trial studying psilocybin in patients with treatment-resistant depression, led by specialists from Universidad Adolfo Ibáñez and Pontificia Universidad Católica, with ANID funding, at Hospital Clínico San Borja Arriarán under physician Aurelio Riquelme.

The contradiction is exactly the one that has shaped medical cannabis patients' struggle across the region for years: a substance the state itself recognizes as a legitimate subject of clinical research remains, for any patient outside that trial, legally indistinguishable from a trafficking-grade drug.

## A Technical Team at Work Since 2023

The process that could change that picture has already been underway for nearly three years. In November 2023, then-Health Minister Ximena Aguilera tasked a Minsal technical commission with studying possible regulation of medicinal psilocybin use. That commission, coordinated by physician Sergio Sánchez — of Universidad de Chile and affiliated with the organization Latinoamérica Reforma — held national gatherings in January 2024 and January 2025, organized around four working groups, including one specifically focused on "access, quality and cultivation."

Minsal's roadmap aims to bypass the traditional legislative process in Congress, advancing instead through a decree with force of law signed directly by the President — amending Supreme Decree 405/1983 to allow psilocybin's use in pharmaceutical products. According to the technical team's own public statements, the goal was to present that formal proposal during June 2026, following a period of dialogue with civil society.

**A necessary clarification:** as of this writing, Revista Hierba could not independently confirm whether that June proposal was actually presented, or what stage its process is currently in. Available sources document Minsal's stated goal and timeline, but not a subsequent announcement confirming it was met. We will update this coverage as soon as official confirmation is available.

## What the Plan Proposes

Beyond the chosen legal mechanism, the technical proposal's content already has fairly defined contours. It calls for creating an advisory commission made up of user and health-professional representatives, and sets differentiated training requirements by role: psychologists, psychiatrists and physicians who want to prescribe or accompany psilocybin therapies would need to complete 40 hours of specific training, while those acting as therapeutic facilitators — a role designed specifically for this kind of treatment — would need to complete 900 hours. For an initial transition period, the plan also contemplates a "soft accreditation" path for people who already have experience facilitating psilocybin mushroom therapies, even without that formal framework.

Taken together, the design aims to position Chile as a regional reference point, following models already underway in countries like Australia and Canada, which in recent years have created specific regulatory pathways for the therapeutic use of psychedelic substances under clinical supervision.

## The Same Tension Cannabis Patients Already Know

For Revista Hierba readers, the pattern will feel familiar. In Argentina, Panama, Paraguay, Bolivia and other countries across the region, we have documented how the gap between a law that recognizes a substance's medicinal use and the real-world implementation of a safe access pathway can stretch on for years — sometimes decades — leaving patients in a limbo where the only real alternative is the informal market, with no quality control and no medical support.

With psilocybin, Chile is still at a stage earlier than that gap: there isn't even an approved regulatory framework yet to implement. But the chosen mechanism — bypassing Congress through a presidential decree — could, in theory, move faster than what has historically happened with medical cannabis laws in the region, which in several countries took years to move from legislative passage to operational regulation.

## Why It Matters Beyond Chile

If the proposal moves forward, Chile would become the first country in Latin America to formally regulate the therapeutic use of psilocybin, setting a precedent that patient movements in other countries — many of which have spent years fighting equivalent battles over cannabis — could invoke as a regional benchmark. It would also put front and center a question that coverage of medical cannabis has been raising for some time: why does a substance's scientific and clinical recognition — confirmed by the state itself through its own trials — take so long to translate into a real right of access for those not fortunate enough to be enrolled in a research protocol?

Until that question is answered, Chilean patients with treatment-resistant depression will remain in an uncomfortable position: eligible to take part in a state-funded clinical trial on psilocybin, but with no legal path to access that same substance outside of it.
