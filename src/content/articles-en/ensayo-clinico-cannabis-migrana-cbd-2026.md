For decades, people with migraine have quietly self-medicated with cannabis, hoping that some strain or oil might blunt an attack's pain, nausea, and light sensitivity. Until this year, no randomized controlled trial had tested whether that instinct has any basis in evidence. A study published in *Headache: The Journal of Head and Face Pain*, led by researchers at the University of California, San Diego, closes that gap — and its central finding cuts against a common assumption. A precise combination of THC and CBD, vaporized during an acute attack, outperformed placebo on multiple measures of pain relief. CBD alone — the cannabinoid most widely sold across Latin America as a legal, non-intoxicating wellness product — did not.

For a region where migraine is unusually common and specialist care is scarce, the distinction matters. It is also a useful case study in how this section tries to read cannabis research: not by repeating what a headline claims, but by asking what a study actually measured, in whom, and with how much certainty.

## A trial built to test what patients already do

The team, led by Nathaniel Schuster, screened 678 adults with migraine and enrolled 92 who met strict criteria — ages 21 to 65, with 2 to 23 migraine days per month. In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover design, each participant was assigned to treat up to four separate migraine attacks, each with a different vaporized cannabis-flower preparation supplied by the U.S. National Institute on Drug Abuse: 6% THC-dominant flower, 11% CBD-dominant flower, a combined 6% THC + 11% CBD flower, and a near-inert placebo (under 0.025% THC and 0.14% CBD). Treatment order was randomized, and at least a week separated each treated attack to avoid carryover effects. Participants, coordinators, investigators, and statisticians were all blinded; only the research pharmacists knew which flower was which.

In total, 247 migraine attacks were treated using a standardized vaporization protocol (four puffs at 180°C). The primary outcome was pain relief at two hours — a drop from moderate-or-severe pain to mild-or-none, the same benchmark long used to evaluate triptans, the standard acute migraine drugs. Secondary outcomes tracked full pain freedom and relief from the "most bothersome symptom" (nausea, light or sound sensitivity) at 2, 24, and 48 hours.

## The number that matters: CBD alone did not work

At two hours, the combined THC+CBD flower produced pain relief in 67% of treated attacks, versus 47% with placebo (odds ratio 2.85, p=0.016). It also produced full pain freedom in 35% of attacks versus 16% with placebo (OR 3.30, p=0.017), and relief of the most bothersome symptom in 60% versus 34% (OR 3.32, p=0.005). Those benefits held up: at 24 hours, sustained pain freedom was 28% versus 11% with placebo; at 48 hours, sustained symptom relief was 40% versus 18%.

THC-dominant flower alone also beat placebo on basic pain relief (69% versus 47%, OR 3.14, p=0.008), but not on the stricter measures of pain freedom or symptom freedom. CBD-dominant flower, tested alone, did not outperform placebo on any outcome, at any time point. The authors' interpretation is that CBD appears to make THC more tolerable — likely by dampening its psychoactive effects through a known interaction at cannabinoid receptors — without adding pain-relieving power of its own in this context.

Safety findings were reassuring but not trivial. No serious adverse events occurred. But intoxication was real and dose-dependent: on a 0-10 scale, participants rated the THC-dominant condition's subjective "high" at 3.5, versus 2.4 for the combination, 1.5 for CBD alone, and 0.6 for placebo. Roughly a third of participants reported some adverse event after THC-dominant flower — sedation, dizziness, throat irritation, increased appetite — compared with about 5% after placebo. When the researchers statistically adjusted for participants' awareness of feeling "high," the treatment effect held, suggesting the pain relief was not simply an expectancy or placebo artifact tied to intoxication.

## What this means, and doesn't, for Latin American patients

Migraine is not a marginal complaint in the region. A 2023 meta-analysis in *Cephalalgia*, led by Peruvian researcher Kevin Pacheco-Barrios and colleagues, pooled data from more than 53,000 people across Latin America and the Caribbean and found migraine prevalence around 15% — matching the global disease-burden ranking that places migraine among the top 20 most disabling conditions worldwide.

Access to care has not kept pace. A narrative review published in June 2026 in *Cephalalgia Reports* by the Latin American Headache Association (ASOLAC), covering Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Mexico, and Peru, describes what its authors call a paradox: even where health coverage nominally reaches 91–99% of the population, treatment gaps are severe. Neurologist-to-population ratios run as low as 1 per 47,000–58,000 people, concentrated in cities, leaving rural patients waiting years for a specialist visit. Newer preventive drugs, such as anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies, remain largely confined to private care. In Brazil, the review found, only 2.6% of patients eligible for preventive treatment actually receive it.

Into that gap, cannabis products — especially CBD oils and isolates marketed as legal wellness items rather than prescription medicines — have spread widely, often sold with vague claims about headache and stress relief and little guidance on dosing or expectations. The San Diego trial does not validate that market. If anything, it complicates it: it suggests that the CBD-only products most widely and legally available across the region are, for an acute migraine attack, pharmacologically indistinguishable from placebo, at least at the ratio tested. Whatever benefit patients experience from those combinations that do work — THC paired with CBD, in this case — comes bundled with genuine, dose-dependent intoxication, which matters for anyone who needs to drive, work, or care for children during an attack.

## The limits of this evidence

This was one trial, in the United States, using pharmaceutical-grade, NIDA-grown cannabis flower at fixed potencies (6% THC, 11% CBD) that do not necessarily match what is sold in dispensaries or pharmacies in Buenos Aires, Bogotá, or Mexico City. The 92 participants were not cannabis-naive, and a demanding protocol with a high screening-failure rate (678 screened to reach 92 enrolled) may limit how well the results generalize. The trial tested a single acute dose per attack, not daily preventive use, and it did not follow participants long enough to assess risks associated with repeated use, such as medication-overuse headache or cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome. The authors themselves call for multicenter trials and longer-term safety data before drawing firm clinical guidance. The study was funded by the Migraine Research Foundation and U.S. academic and government grants, with no cannabis-industry funding disclosed — a point worth noting given how much cannabis research globally is funded by companies with a stake in the outcome.

What the trial does offer, cautiously, is a template: rigorous, placebo-controlled evidence about which cannabinoid combinations do and do not help a specific, common condition — evidence that regional patients, physicians, and regulators can use to ask sharper questions than "does cannabis help migraine," starting instead with "which cannabis, in what ratio, and compared to what."
