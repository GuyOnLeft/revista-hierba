title: "Coca Leaf Stays on the UN's Most Restrictive Drug List, Despite the Science"
excerpt: "After a two-year review, the WHO kept coca leaf alongside heroin and fentanyl on the UN's Schedule I — even as its own technical report found no evidence of dependency and noted potential therapeutic properties."

In the Yungas of La Paz and the highlands around Cusco, the coca leaf has been chewed, brewed, and offered to the earth for at least four thousand years — long before anyone called it a narcotic. For two years, Bolivia and Colombia asked the World Health Organization to formally reconsider that classification, arguing that the plant's inclusion alongside heroin and fentanyl in Schedule I of the 1961 UN Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs was a historical error rooted in bad science. In December 2025, the WHO's Expert Committee on Drug Dependence answered: no. The leaf stays. What makes the decision notable is not that it happened, but how — the committee's own technical findings undercut much of its reasoning, and the process that produced it excluded the people most affected by it.

## Two governments, one petition

Bolivia formally requested the critical review in 2023, joined by Colombia, arguing that the 1961 scheduling of coca leaf was never based on rigorous pharmacological evidence. That claim has real historical footing: a 1950 United Nations commission of inquiry into coca chewing in the Andes — cited by rights groups including the Washington Office on Latin America (WOLA) — was conducted with what later reviewers described as openly racist assumptions about indigenous populations, and it treated a cultural and dietary practice as a medical pathology despite acknowledging it was "not toxicomania but a habit." That framing was carried, largely unexamined, into the 1961 Convention, where coca leaf was scheduled alongside cocaine and opium derivatives without a distinct evidentiary review of the raw plant.

The 2023 petition asked the WHO to finally conduct that review: to separate the leaf from the drug refined out of it, weigh its documented nutritional and traditional therapeutic uses, and assess whether Schedule I still made scientific sense sixty years later. The WHO agreed to open the critical review — a rare and significant procedural step — and convened its Expert Committee on Drug Dependence (ECDD), which met in October 2025 to evaluate coca leaf across pharmacology, toxicology, traditional medicine, and public health impact.

## What the committee's own evidence found

The technical report that emerged from that review, as described in Spanish-language coverage by La Tercera and Infobae, is more equivocal than the final recommendation suggests. The WHO acknowledged that there is no robust evidence that chewing or brewing coca leaf produces dependency, and it recognized "indicios de un potencial uso terapéutico" — indicators of potential therapeutic use, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antihypertensive, and antidiabetic effects associated with the leaf's alkaloid and flavonoid content.

That finding sits alongside decades of separate nutritional research. A widely cited 1975 analysis by Harvard researchers James Duke, David Aulik, and Timothy Plowman found coca leaf unusually dense in micronutrients relative to other Andean staples — with reported ranges (per 100 grams) of roughly 990 to 1,749 milligrams of calcium, 27 to 46 milligrams of iron, and significant vitamin A, B-complex, and C content, alongside notable protein and fiber. Andean communities have long used coca-leaf infusions to manage altitude sickness, or soroche, and to blunt hunger and fatigue during high-altitude labor. It is worth being precise about what the newer clinical literature actually shows here: peer-reviewed travel-medicine research on coca tea and altitude sickness has produced mixed results, with some studies finding no consistent reduction in acute mountain sickness symptoms compared to no treatment, even as anecdotal and some physiological exercise-response data point to real effects. The honest scientific picture is partial evidence of benefit, not a settled therapeutic claim — and Revista Hierba treats it as such.

## "Convertibility" becomes the deciding argument

Given that evidentiary picture, the ECDD's final justification for keeping coca leaf in Schedule I rested on a different and more contested principle: convertibility. The committee argued that because coca leaf can be processed into cocaine base and then cocaine hydrochloride, it meets the Single Convention's criterion for scheduling one controlled substance because another can be derived from it — comparing raw agricultural leaf to a chemical precursor. WHO officials also pointed to a documented 34 percent rise in global cocaine production in 2023, reaching record levels, as a public-health rationale for caution.

WOLA's analysis of the decision, part of its ongoing "Coca Chronicles" series, called this legal reasoning shaky on its own terms: convertibility, the organization notes, has historically been applied to chemical precursors rather than to a plant material with its own independent, non-narcotic uses. Because the WHO's recommendation was to maintain the status quo rather than propose a change, no vote was required or held when the UN Commission on Narcotic Drugs met for its 69th session in Vienna in March 2026 — under the Convention's procedural rules, only proposals to alter a substance's schedule go to a formal vote. The review that took two years and drew global attention closed, procedurally, in silence.

## Rights left off the table

The sharpest criticism of the process concerns who was allowed to shape it. According to WOLA's reporting, coca-specific pharmacological experts, indigenous representatives, and human rights observers were excluded from full participation in the ECDD's deliberations, and the committee's full annexes — which reportedly contained the review's social and rights-based discussion — were not made public. The UN Special Rapporteur on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, Francisco Cali Tzay, has separately argued that international drug scheduling of this kind conflicts with indigenous peoples' rights to self-determination, to their own natural resources, and to their traditional medicines — rights affirmed in UN resolutions in 2023 and 2024 that explicitly linked drug policy to indigenous rights frameworks, including free, prior, and informed consent in decisions that affect them.

For coca-growing communities in the Yungas, the Chapare, and Colombia's Cauca and Nariño departments, the practical effect of the WHO's decision is continuity rather than change: national frameworks in Bolivia and, to a lesser extent, Colombia already carve out legal space for traditional coca use and domestic markets, and those will remain unaffected in the near term. What the decision forecloses, for now, is the possibility those governments sought — an international acknowledgment that the leaf and the drug are not the same thing, and that sixty-year-old scheduling built on a discredited colonial-era study deserved to be corrected rather than reaffirmed by a narrower, procedural argument about chemistry.

Whether that acknowledgment ever arrives depends less on new pharmacology — much of what the ECDD needed was already in its own technical report — than on whether the UN's drug-control architecture can weigh indigenous rights and traditional knowledge as evidence in their own right, rather than as context to be set aside once the vote, or the absence of one, is settled.
