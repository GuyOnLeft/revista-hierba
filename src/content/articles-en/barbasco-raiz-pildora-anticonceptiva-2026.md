In the hill forests above Orizaba, Veracruz, there grows a wild yam with a tuber so heavy it can weigh more than forty kilograms, buried like a boulder under a curtain of vines. Local communities in Veracruz, Oaxaca, and Puebla knew it for generations by its bitterness and its toxicity to fish, using preparations from its roots — as many Latin American peoples have done with a range of unrelated species — to stun fish in streams without harming those who ate them. They called it barbasco. In the 1940s, that same root became the raw material for the first industrially viable progesterone, and a decade later, for the first oral contraceptive progestin ever synthesized. Nearly a century on, the story of barbasco is less a triumphant origin myth than a case study in how Latin American biodiversity and rural labor built a global industry that, once it no longer needed either, simply moved on.

## A Root With Two Names

Botanically, "barbasco" is not one species but a common name applied to several: in the yam family, the two that mattered most were *Dioscorea mexicana* — known locally as cabeza de negro — and *Dioscorea composita*, which researchers later found to contain roughly five times more of the compound everyone wanted: diosgenin, a plant steroid that can be chemically converted into progesterone and other hormones. Ethnobotanical surveys published in the Mexican journal Etnobiología describe how *Dioscorea* roots were extracted from Mexico's tropical forests on an industrial scale between 1943 and 1975, becoming the world's dominant source of diosgenin — but the underlying knowledge that these plants existed, where to find them, and how to dig up their enormous tubers came from campesino communities in Veracruz, Oaxaca, and Puebla long before any chemist arrived.

That chemist was Russell Marker, an American researcher who had developed a process — now called the Marker degradation — for converting plant steroids into progesterone cheaply. According to the science-history outlet Botany One, Marker traveled to Mexico in search of a Dioscorea species he had seen illustrated in a botanical text, and with the help of local plant collectors found wild cabeza de negro in the forests near Orizaba. He arranged for tons of the root to be harvested and shipped for processing. It is a detail historians of the period return to often: the names of the campesinos and collectors who actually led him to the plant and taught him to identify it rarely made it into the record, while Marker's own name became permanently attached to the discovery.

## From Jungle Root to Laboratory Steroid

Marker's work led to the founding of Syntex in Mexico City in 1944, the company that would turn Mexican yams into the raw material for a global steroid hormone industry. By 1949, researchers had identified *Dioscorea composita* as a far richer source of diosgenin than *D. mexicana*, and the trade shifted toward it. The payoff came on October 15, 1951, when Syntex chemists Luis Miramontes, then a 26-year-old Mexican chemistry student, together with Carl Djerassi and George Rosenkranz, synthesized norethisterone — the first orally active progestin, and the compound that would become a core ingredient of the first birth control pills approved in the United States in the 1960s.

For roughly two decades afterward, Mexico supplied the large majority of the world's raw material for steroid hormone production, according to historical accounts compiled by Botany One; by the late 1950s, tens of millions of Dioscorea plants were being harvested annually, and turning one kilogram of diosgenin into usable product required around twenty-five kilograms of dried root. It took an enormous, largely invisible rural workforce to keep that supply chain running.

## The Barbasqueros' Bargain

That workforce came to be known as the barbasqueros: more than 100,000 campesinos, according to Botany One's account, who harvested wild Dioscorea roots by hand, often as seasonal income between agricultural harvests in states like Oaxaca. Historian Gabriela Soto Laveaga documents their story in her book "Jungle Laboratories: Mexican Peasants, National Projects, and the Making of the Pill," published by Duke University Press, tracing how rural pickers, multinational pharmaceutical companies, and the Mexican state collided over who would control — and profit from — the barbasco trade. In 1975, amid the nationalist politics of the era, the Mexican government created a state company, Proquivemex, to take a larger share of the barbasco economy for the country and its harvesters.

It did not last. A companion analysis published in the journal Etnobiología concludes that, measured by the standards now used to evaluate non-timber forest products, the barbasco trade must be considered a failure: it generated, in the author's words, enormous wealth, but that wealth was distributed inequitably, and for the barbasqueros themselves the resource functioned mainly as survival income rather than a path out of poverty. By the 1970s, roughly 80% of the 7.6 million hectares of forest where barbasco had been harvested had already been converted to agriculture and pasture, according to Botany One. Then the ground shifted again: chemists found they could derive steroid precursors more cheaply from soybean oil and sisal waste, foreign pharmaceutical buyers lost interest in Mexican wild-harvested diosgenin, the Dioscorea research commission dissolved, and by the 1990s the barbasco economy that had sustained tens of thousands of families had effectively disappeared, with no conservation or livelihood transition plan left in its place.

## What Barbasco Still Has to Teach

Barbasco has not entirely vanished from science. A 2025 paper in the Mexican journal Milenaria, Ciencia y Arte, by researchers Ana María Huerta Olalde, Elena Hernández Granados, and Rafael Salgado Garciglia of the Universidad Michoacana de San Nicolás de Hidalgo, revisits *Dioscorea composita* beyond its contraceptive legacy, noting that diosgenin remains a precursor for anti-inflammatory drugs and other steroid-based medicines — and that intensive collection of wild roots has created real sustainability problems, prompting research into cultivation and biotechnological production methods that do not depend on stripping wild populations. Market analysts now estimate global demand for diosgenin at over a billion dollars annually, a figure driven as much by cosmetics and nutraceuticals as by pharmaceuticals, which only sharpens the sustainability question for the wild plant.

What barbasco offers, more than a forgotten chemical curiosity, is a clarifying case for how Latin America tends to enter global health narratives: as a source of raw biological material and labor, rarely as a beneficiary of the value that material eventually creates. The plant itself did nothing wrong — its steroidal saponins are exactly as useful today as they were in 1944. What collapsed was never the biology; it was an arrangement in which the forests and the people of Veracruz, Oaxaca, and Puebla carried the ecological and physical cost of a global pharmaceutical breakthrough while capturing almost none of its return. Any renewed interest in Dioscorea — for anti-inflammatory research, for sustainable diosgenin production, or simply for its place in Mexican scientific history — will be worth little if it repeats that same pattern.
