While Javier Milei's national government keeps tightening its grip on the Reprocann — the registry that authorizes home cultivation of medicinal cannabis — Buenos Aires province spent the last four weeks moving in the opposite direction, recognizing, organizing, and institutionally strengthening the cannabis and industrial hemp sector. Between June 16 and July 13, 2026, four developments unfolded that, taken together, sketch an actively developing provincial policy centered in La Plata, with branches reaching municipalities like San Vicente.

The contrast is not accidental. Since February 2025, when then-Security Minister Patricia Bullrich announced the government would revoke home-cultivation permits granted under the previous administration — more than 300,000, according to official figures cited at the time — Argentina's national medicinal cannabis program has been under sustained adjustment. In January 2026, Decree 27/2026 formalized another structural change: the Reprocann stopped reporting directly to the Ministry of Health and was moved under the orbit of the Secretariat of Comprehensive Drug Policy (Sedronar). The change did not alter Law 27,350, which remains in force, but it did change the program's management, monitoring, and approval criteria, which patient organizations report have become slower and more demanding.

## A month of provincial institution-building

Against that backdrop, Buenos Aires province chose a different path. On June 16, 2026, the provincial Ministry of Health formalized, through Resolution 4917/2026, the creation of the Advisory Consultative Council of the Provincial Therapeutic Cannabis Program — a permanent body of 15 to 30 members, drawing on specialists, universities, research centers, social organizations, and municipal representatives, tasked with advising the provincial state on medicinal cannabis policy. The provincial program underlying this council was created in 2025, in adherence to national Law 27,350. Members serve unpaid, two-year terms, with mandatory conflict-of-interest declarations and quarterly meetings.

Two days later, on June 18, the Provincial Institute of Associativism and Cooperativism (IPAC) and the Buenos Aires Cannabis Federation (FeCannBo) presented a joint initiative at the Casa de la Provincia aimed at productive development, health access, and labor formalization for the sector. The event launched the first Census of Buenos Aires Cannabis Cooperatives and announced the creation of a Provincial Council for Cannabis Regulation. Five cooperatives — Todo Orgánico, Brotar, Grupo Cannábico de Bahía Blanca, Sagrada Semilla, and Pelagatos Ltda. — shared their experiences on panels covering health, production, and labor. "We're here to listen to those driving the evolution of work, industry, local development, and regional economies. We're here to put ourselves at the disposal of the organized sector, with our institute's tools and the provincial state's resources, so cooperatives can develop their full potential," said IPAC president Gildo Onorato, according to the institute's own account of the event. Public health physician Daniel Gollán, a former Buenos Aires provincial health minister, also attended.

On July 11, FeCannBo took a further step and presented the 2026 Municipal and Buenos Aires Cannabis and Hemp Agenda, a document meant to bring municipalities, universities, hospitals, and cooperatives together around six pillars: production, transformation and distribution; access; training; labor; health; and research. The federation's secretary, Daniel Pereyra, presented the agenda as the foundation for "a sustainable and inclusive provincial public policy" that, he said, requires coordination among the state, universities, cooperatives, and sector organizations.

## San Vicente and industrial hemp

The sequence's most recent chapter came on July 13, when the municipality of San Vicente hosted the Buenos Aires Cannabis and Hemp Pre-Congress at the Quinta 17 de Octubre auditorium. Mayor Nicolás Mantegazza led the gathering, which brought together specialists, institutions, and actors linked to research, public health, and productive development in a district that has hosted a public-private medicinal cannabis "biofactory," developed with Conicet, since 2022.

At the event, the municipality signed a cooperation agreement with the Industrial Cluster for Cannabis Development (CIND), represented by Álvaro Pino, aimed at consolidating joint research, production, and tool-development efforts for the sector. The same ceremony recognized the career of pediatric neurologist Carlos Magdalena, of the Hospital de Niños Ricardo Gutiérrez, for his work promoting public cannabis policy and developing the field of endocannabinology "from a public-health, social, and community perspective," according to coverage by local outlet InfoBrandsen. The event's name — "pre-congress" — signals that organizers expect a larger-scale Buenos Aires congress later in the year, though no date had been confirmed as of this writing.

## Cooperativism as an answer to the national squeeze

What distinguishes this sequence from other provincial initiatives is that it does not stop at health: it also targets the labor and productive formalization of a sector that, for years, largely operated informally or under the imperfect umbrella of solidarity cultivation. The cooperative census launched by IPAC and FeCannBo is specifically meant to measure how many cooperatives and pre-cooperative groups currently exist in Buenos Aires's medicinal cannabis and industrial hemp value chain — a figure that, until now, no public agency had systematically tracked.

It is worth being clear about this push's limits. None of the bodies created so far — the health ministry's Advisory Consultative Council, the Provincial Council for Cannabis Regulation announced by IPAC — hold binding authority; they are consultative bodies whose recommendations do not obligate the provincial state to act. Nor has any specific funding been announced for the surveyed cooperatives beyond technical and institutional support. And none of these provincial initiatives can substitute for the Reprocann: the national registry remains, for the vast majority of Buenos Aires patients, the only legal path to home cultivation, regardless of its move under Sedronar's management.

Still, the accumulation of developments in under a month — a health advisory council, a cooperative census, a six-pillar agenda, and an industrial agreement — suggests something more than a string of ceremonial acts. It amounts, in practice, to the construction of provincial institutional infrastructure for a sector that the national government has treated with more wariness than openness since 2025. Whether that architecture translates into concrete access for patients and genuine income for Buenos Aires's cannabis cooperatives, or remains largely declarative, is a question the province's own calendar — with its still-undated "congress" — will have to answer in the months ahead.
