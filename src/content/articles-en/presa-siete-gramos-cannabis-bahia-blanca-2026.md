For half a decade, Argentina has run two legal systems that talk about the same plant in opposite languages: one that recognizes cannabis as medicine, and one that still prosecutes it as if it were cartel cocaine. That collision rarely has as concrete a name and face as it does in Bahía Blanca, where Mariela Agunín, 32, has spent more than two months in jail charged with drug trafficking for handing seven grams of marijuana to a friend in a public square — while her application for a medical cannabis authorization for that very substance sat, and still sits, stalled in the state bureaucracy.

## A Package in a Plaza, Two Competing Accounts

The case began on January 9, 2025, when police spotted two women exchanging a wrapped package at the corner of Chiclana and Alsina, in downtown Bahía Blanca. Inside was marijuana. It wasn't until April 15, 2026 that the investigation led to a raid — more than a dozen armed officers — on Agunín's home in the Villa Belgrano neighborhood, according to the local outlet Wips Digital. Her preventive detention was ordered on May 1.

Prosecutor Mauricio Del Cero, of prosecutorial unit UFIJ No. 19, argues the plaza exchange was not an isolated incident but part of a pattern of repeated sales between November 15, 2024 and January 9, 2025, backed by phone-message analysis, security-camera footage, and a recorded Mercado Pago transfer, according to reporting in the Bahía Blanca daily La Nueva. The charge — aggravated drug distribution for occurring in a public space — carries a sentence of six to twenty years.

Her defense attorney, Carlos Carnevale, disputes that framing. He argues the case rests on a single proven incident — the seven grams in the plaza — and that the phone-record analysis exceeded the window judicially authorized for surveillance, per the same outlet. He has sought to have the raid and detention nullified, requested her release, and asked in the alternative for house arrest on health grounds. On June 20, an appeals panel — judges Christian Yesari and Gustavo Barbieri — upheld the preventive detention, finding the phone evidence sufficient to sustain it, La Nueva reported.

## A State That Can't Decide If She's a Patient or a Trafficker

Agunín has no prior criminal record. She strings together work: selling fried pastries outside nightclubs, childcare, community bioconstruction, and dry-construction jobs with family members. According to La Izquierda Diario, she had recently completed a rope-access certification to work in the region's wind farms and was seeking to enroll as an agricultural technician.

Since 2023, she had an application pending with REPROCANN, the medical cannabis patient registry created by decree 883/2020 for people who cultivate or access cannabis under medical indication — in her case, for insomnia. Both La Izquierda Diario and the local site De la Bahía note the delay was not due to any irregularity on her part but to the state's own routine backlog in processing these authorizations. La Nueva reported that the authorization was only finalized after Agunín was already behind bars — meaning the same state apparatus that recognized her as a patient completed that recognition while the justice system was prosecuting her as a trafficker for the same plant.

This is not a call on the evidentiary dispute still being litigated between prosecution and defense — that is for Bahía Blanca's courts to resolve — but the underlying contradiction stands independent of how the case is ultimately decided: a person can simultaneously be a patient recognized by the health system and a trafficking suspect pursued by the justice system, over the same substance, in the same stretch of time.

## The Toll of Detention on Her Health

Pretrial detention has not been an abstraction for Agunín. By her own account, relayed by La Izquierda Diario, it meant an abrupt halt to the cannabis treatment she used for insomnia. Inside Saavedra women's prison, she began experiencing anxiety and panic episodes and needed psychiatric medication — clonazepam and quetiapine — that she had not required before her arrest.

Karina Romanelli, a spokesperson for the civil-society group Asociación Civil Cannabis Medicinal, publicly questioned the preventive detention in comments to De la Bahía, arguing there wasn't sufficient evidence to justify holding her and noting that Agunín "has episodes of anxiety and depression and needs her therapeutic treatment." That is the assessment of a civil-society organization focused on medical cannabis, not a forensic or judicial finding — but it points to a question the case itself leaves open: what happens to a patient's right to health when the criminal justice system treats her, in the meantime, as a trafficking suspect.

## A Pattern That Worries Advocates Beyond Bahía Blanca

La Izquierda Diario documented that Del Cero's office has a record of aggressively pursuing low-level cannabis cases, a pattern one Buenos Aires outlet reportedly captured with a mocking nickname for the prosecutor, according to that coverage. Setting the irony aside, the underlying pattern matters: Argentina belongs to a group of Latin American countries — Colombia among them — that combine relatively permissive medical-access frameworks with punitive narcotics statutes still applied harshly to users, growers, and small-scale sellers.

WOLA (the Washington Office on Latin America), which monitors regional drug policy, warned in an analysis of cannabis in Latin America that "the trend towards the criminalization of cannabis and its associated behaviors remains the norm" across much of the continent despite regulatory progress on medical use, and noted that punitive laws "disproportionately affect cannabis users, growers, and small-scale sellers." Agunín's case does not, on its own, prove that regional thesis — it is an active court proceeding, with a prosecution that maintains its own account of events — but it does illustrate, with a name and a face, the tension rights groups have flagged: a system that recognizes the right to treatment on paper while its penal machinery, still calibrated to pursue large-scale trafficking networks, struggles to distinguish who is actually standing in front of it.

With the appeals court ruling now on the record and the case still open, Agunín remains in Saavedra prison. Her defense is weighing further motions; the Asociación Civil Cannabis Medicinal continues to argue that her prolonged detention, compounded by her status as a patient, warrants urgent review.
