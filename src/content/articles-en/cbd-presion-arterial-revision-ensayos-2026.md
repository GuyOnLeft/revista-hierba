Hypertension kills more Latin Americans than nearly any other single risk factor, and much of that toll is preventable: according to the Pan American Health Organization, elevated blood pressure affects an estimated 20 to 40 percent of adults in the region and is linked to more than two million deaths a year, while the World Health Organization warned in a 2025 update that more than a billion people worldwide live with blood pressure that is poorly controlled or not controlled at all. Into that gap has grown a wellness market — drops, capsules and oils of cannabidiol (CBD) — that promises calm and, increasingly, lower blood pressure. A systematic review published June 19 in the *Journal of Cannabis Research* puts that promise through its most rigorous test yet, pooling four randomized controlled trials in 120 adults. The finding is real. It is also exactly the kind of preliminary result the romanticism trap exists for: interesting, mechanistically plausible, and nowhere near ready to replace a prescription.

## Four Trials, One Consistent Pattern

The review, authored by researchers Albert Roberts, Solon Fountzopoulos and Terun Desai, searched PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus and Medline for randomized, placebo-controlled trials testing oral CBD in adults, with or without diagnosed hypertension. They found four that met the criteria, using doses between 225 and 600 milligrams a day over periods ranging from a single acute dose, tracked for a couple of hours, to five weeks of continuous use. In all four, without exception, CBD produced statistically significant reductions in systolic blood pressure compared with placebo, with the effect most pronounced under stress or during sleep, and larger at higher acute doses — a dose-dependent pattern. Only two of the four trials also showed a significant drop in diastolic pressure. Side effects were generally mild to moderate — nausea, diarrhea, fatigue — with no serious cardiovascular events reported. One methodological nuance matters here: because dosing, formulations and outcome measures varied so much across the four trials, the authors themselves note they could not calculate a pooled effect size. This is a systematic review, not a meta-analysis with a single combined number — a distinction of rigor that should shape how much weight the finding is given.

## Not the First Time, and Not Yet a Green Light

This result did not come out of nowhere. In 2017, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial published in *JCI Insight* gave nine healthy male volunteers a single 600 mg dose of CBD and found that resting systolic blood pressure fell by roughly 6 mmHg, with a blunted rise in blood pressure during a stress test — a genuine mechanistic signal, though from a tiny sample. More recently, a meta-analysis published in July 2025 in the Brazilian journal *Revista Brasileira de Farmacognosia* pooled four trials with 104 participants and calculated a statistically significant combined effect for systolic blood pressure reduction after acute CBD dosing (standardized mean difference of -1.06; 95% confidence interval -2.01 to -0.11; p=0.03), but found no significant effect on diastolic pressure (SMD -1.17; 95% CI -2.55 to 0.20; p=0.09). Two independent reviews, roughly a year apart and produced by different teams, converge on the same bounded signal: acute, higher-dose oral CBD tends to lower systolic pressure, especially under stress, while its effect on diastolic pressure — and on chronic, unsupervised use — remains unproven.

## Why This Matters in Latin America

Regional context gives this finding a weight it would not carry elsewhere. The CBD market is growing in Latin America faster than its regulation: in Mexico, the health regulator Cofepris has issued repeated alerts against CBD products sold without sanitary authorization; in Brazil, Anvisa permits therapeutic CBD only by prescription; and even so, industry estimates project the regional CBD market will reach several billion dollars by 2026. Given that a large share of Latin Americans with hypertension go without adequate treatment, the temptation to reach for an over-the-counter CBD tincture instead of — or alongside — a prescribed antihypertensive is real and growing. That is precisely the scenario the review's authors are warning against, not endorsing: none of the four trials enrolled people with severe or poorly controlled hypertension, none ran longer than five weeks, and CBD is known to interact with the same liver enzymes (CYP3A4, CYP2C19) that metabolize many common antihypertensive and anticoagulant drugs, raising the risk of altered drug levels if the two are combined without medical supervision.

## What's Missing Before We'll Know for Sure

The authors themselves are explicit about what is needed: larger, longer trials — 12 months or more — with standardized doses and formulations, conducted in people with actual, poorly controlled hypertension rather than mostly healthy volunteers; comparisons across routes of administration (sublingual, inhaled) to better understand bioavailability; and studies establishing long-term liver safety. Until that exists, the evidence tier remains what it is: a small systematic review of heterogeneous trials — an encouraging signal, not a clinical recommendation. In a region where uncontrolled hypertension is one of the leading causes of preventable death, and where wellness marketing routinely outpaces the science behind it, that distinction is not an academic footnote.
