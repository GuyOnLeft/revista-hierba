Before there is a flower, an oil, or a patient who receives it, there is a seed. That agronomic truism is also the most fragile point in Argentina's entire medical cannabis supply chain: for much of the past year, the people who produce seeds, seedlings and cuttings — the genetic material any authorized crop depends on — were caught between two state agencies that kept passing responsibility back and forth. On June 30, the Regulatory Agency for the Hemp and Medicinal Cannabis Industry (ARICCAME) published Resolution 41/2026 in Argentina's Official Gazette, creating a special licensing regime for that specific link in the chain. It's a concrete relief for a sector that had spent months in limbo. But it deserves the same caution one should apply to any regulatory announcement in a country where, barely eleven months earlier, the executive branch itself had tried to dissolve by decree the very agency now signing the resolution.

ARICCAME's recent history isn't one of an institution that simply "streamlined its paperwork." It's the story of an agency that survived an attempt to eliminate it outright, and that now, while still disputing jurisdiction with another state body, is trying to bring order to one narrow slice of a much larger problem.

## A Year of Gridlock Between Two Agencies

The conflict didn't start with the new resolution — it had been building for a while. According to reporting by El Planteo, operators in the cannabis germplasm sector described a cross-blocking situation between the National Seed Institute (INASE) and ARICCAME, in which each agency routed responsibility to the other. INASE stopped processing cannabis-related authorizations and referred inquiries to ARICCAME, while ARICCAME maintained that jurisdiction over medical cannabis belonged to the Ministry of Health under Law 27,350, and that seeds should continue to be processed through INASE. The result, documented by the same outlet, was that germplasm companies and producers were left with annual authorizations in an unclear state, fearing they could lose investments already made in genetic lines developed over years.

That administrative standstill isn't a minor detail. Genetics — certified seeds, cuttings of stable varieties — is the foundation underpinning both research crops and the authorized projects that, further down the chain, eventually supply patients under the REPROCANN program or laboratories producing oils and extracts. When that first link jams, the effect is felt downstream, even if it takes months to become visible.

## What Resolution 41/2026 Allows — and What It Doesn't

The resolution, framed under Law 27,669, which governs Argentina's hemp and medical cannabis industry, creates what the text itself calls a "special adjustment regime" for granting licenses tied to the production and handling of propagation organs of *Cannabis sativa* L. for medicinal purposes — specifically defined as seeds, seedlings and cuttings.

It's important to understand the measure's limits, which the regulation itself takes pains to spell out. This is not a general license to produce cannabis, nor a market opening: it is a narrow mechanism aimed at natural or legal persons who already hold valid registry records — predating the resolution's publication — tied to propagating the plant. The application is filed through Argentina's remote-procedures platform, carries the status of a sworn statement, and requires, among other documents, a criminal background check and proof of tax registration. If granted, the license is valid for five years subject to annual validation, with a fee of 250,000 Argentine pesos, waived only for public agencies and national universities.

The resolution is equally explicit about what it excludes: it does not authorize the commercialization of flowers, inflorescences, floral biomass, or derivative products such as oils and extracts. If flowers appear as an inevitable byproduct of authorized cultivation, the rule requires they be destroyed rather than used for any purpose beyond obtaining propagation material. And, crucially for understanding the measure's real scope, the regime is transitional: it will only run until March 1, 2027, and any licenses it grants are complementary to — not a replacement for — authorizations still required by INASE, SENASA, the Ministry of Health, or the federal tax agency ARCA.

## An Agency That Survived Its Own Dissolution

To understand why this resolution matters beyond its technical text, it helps to go back to August 2025. On August 7 of that year, Argentina's Chamber of Deputies rejected, by a vote of 141 to 65, Emergency Decree 462/2025, which — among other state-shrinking measures — ordered the dissolution of both ARICCAME and INASE, alongside structural changes at agricultural and industrial research institutes INTA and INTI. On August 22, the Senate confirmed that rejection by a vote of 59 to 10, leaving the decree without effect. According to the decree's own official justification, the government sought to dissolve ARICCAME over what it called its "hierarchical and functional oversizing," splitting its functions between the national drug regulator ANMAT and the Ministry of Economy.

That backstory isn't incidental color: it explains why Resolution 41/2026 should be read as the product of an institution that, having barely survived an attempt to shut it down by decree, is still fighting another state agency over control of the same plant material. The measure resolves — at least temporarily, and for a limited universe of actors with pre-existing records — a conflict that, per El Planteo's coverage of what it called the "bureaucratic maze" between INASE and ARICCAME, had left sector operators without legal certainty for months. But it doesn't settle the underlying question of which agency ultimately holds jurisdiction over cannabis seeds in Argentina, nor does it offer any guarantee that the next budget fight or the next state-shrinking decree won't again put the agency now regularizing this activity back on the chopping block.

## The Trap of Reading This as a Victory

It would be a mistake — the kind of mistake enthusiastic cannabis-reform coverage often makes — to present this resolution as an unqualified sign of opening or "progress." It isn't. It is an administrative measure, time-limited, aimed at a very specific segment of actors who already held prior registrations, and one that explicitly does not touch either flower commercialization or patient access. Nor does it resolve the structural problem that caused it in the first place: the overlapping jurisdiction between INASE and ARICCAME, which had already produced, according to earlier reporting from the same outlet, episodes of operators being completely locked out of their accounts and warnings about lost investment in genetic lines.

What the resolution does show is something more modest, and in some ways more revealing: that medical cannabis regulatory policy in Argentina keeps moving at the pace of the country's broader institutional fragility. An agency that nearly disappeared by decree, that then spent months stuck in a jurisdictional dispute with another body, and that finally issued a transitional resolution with an expiration date is not the story of a mature regulatory system. It is the story of a sector — producers, researchers, and ultimately patients who depend on the genetic continuity of their treatments — still operating under the uncertainty of a state architecture that can be reconfigured overnight.

## What Comes Next

Resolution 41/2026 offers, at best, an administrative truce until March 2027. By then, ARICCAME — if it still exists in its current form — will have to decide whether to turn this special regime into permanent policy or let the sector slide back into the uncertainty that prompted the measure in the first place. In the meantime, the most honest takeaway from this episode isn't that of an industry advancing without friction, but of a productive base — the plant's own genetics — that in Argentina still depends on the stability of institutions the state itself put in doubt less than a year ago.
