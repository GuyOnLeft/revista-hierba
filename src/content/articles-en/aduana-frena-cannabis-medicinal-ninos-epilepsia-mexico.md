In Guadalajara, a mother spent eight years fighting for the right to give her son a medicine that had been legal, on paper, since he was thirteen. When Margarita Garfias finally won an injunction ordering Mexico's public health service to import a cannabis-derived drug for her son Carlos, who lives with refractory epilepsy, the ruling felt like the end of the fight. It wasn't. Getting the medication into the country still meant navigating customs agents, tax authorities, and armed checkpoints that had no clear mandate to let a legally authorized shipment through. Carlos received his first dose on March 13, 2025, at age 21 — thirteen years after his diagnosis, according to reporting by the Mexican outlet Expansión Política.

That gap — between what a Mexican court or federal permit authorizes and what actually clears the border — is now the subject of a legislative push in Jalisco. On July 22, 2026, state deputy Enrique Velázquez González, of the Hagamos party, announced an agreement seeking to compel federal authorities to guarantee access to cannabis-derived medications, flanked by activist Erika Sevilla and mothers of children with hard-to-control neurological conditions, including West syndrome, according to the outlet Página 24 Jalisco. The request is strikingly specific: it asks Mexico's Congress to review and harmonize the federal regulatory framework, and it asks the tax authority (SAT) and the National Guard to coordinate with health regulators so that judicially authorized CBD imports are recognized at ports of entry instead of being held or seized.

## Eight Years of Law, Six Import Permits

Mexico legalized medicinal cannabis in 2017 and issued implementing regulations in 2021, putting the federal health regulator, Cofepris, in charge of authorizing production, research, and imports of cannabis-derived products. On paper, this made Mexico one of the earlier movers on medical cannabis in Latin America. In practice, the agency has moved at a crawl: between 2017 and April 2024, Cofepris issued only six permits for the importation of cannabis-derived medications, Expansión Política reported, and as of that reporting no cannabis-derived drug had been approved for commercial sale inside Mexico. Families can buy creams, ointments, and food supplements over the counter, but not the pharmaceutical-grade products doctors actually prescribe for conditions like refractory epilepsy.

Imer Benjamín Flores Mendoza, a researcher at UNAM's Institute for Legal Research, put it plainly in comments carried by the university's Gaceta UNAM: what is missing is not a legal framework but political will. Mexico has no established minimum or maximum standards for cannabinoid dosing, he noted, and countries that recognized medical cannabis years after Mexico did have since built functioning medical industries around it — while Mexico has not.

## When Winning in Court Still Isn't Winning

For families without the resources or legal know-how to sue, the path Garfias took — filing an amparo, a constitutional injunction, against the health system — has become close to the only route to a legal prescription. Expansión Política found that at least twenty patients had amparos pending as of last year, with only two families having actually obtained medication through the courts by March 2025. A 2021 Supreme Court ruling ordering Mexico's social security institute, IMSS, to cover cannabis treatment for one plaintiff established a legal precedent that other families have since tried to invoke.

But a favorable ruling, it turns out, does not travel with the package. Customs officials and National Guard units posted at airports and border crossings have no standing protocol for recognizing an amparo or a Cofepris import permit when a shipment of CBD medication arrives, which is precisely the enforcement gap the Jalisco deputies are now asking the federal government to close. Families who lack the odds needed to win a court order in the first place are left with two options that Mexican reporting has repeatedly documented: pay, out of pocket, for irregular imports, or turn to unregulated cannabis oils sold informally — described in one interview as products "you can buy in the Metro" — with no guarantee of consistent dosing or safety.

## Jalisco's Push, and Who Is Behind It

The families driving this month's legislative effort are not new to the fight. Erika Sevilla has advocated for medical cannabis access in Jalisco since December 2016, when she began treating her daughter, Monserrat Josefina Gómez Sevilla, who lives with Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, a severe form of childhood epilepsy. Sevilla has told local media that her daughter went from roughly 300 seizures a day to a markedly calmer baseline after starting cannabidiol treatment — an improvement Sevilla has spent nearly a decade trying to make replicable for other families, including through Mexico's Fundación Mexicana para el Cannabis Medicinal.

The exhortos announced this week do not change federal law by themselves; as non-binding requests from a state legislature to federal authorities, they are a pressure tactic, not a guarantee. But they name a concrete, fixable failure — customs enforcement that ignores judicial and regulatory authorizations already on the books — rather than asking for a new law that would take years to pass.

## The Rights at Stake

Margarita Garfias, who founded the advocacy group Familias y Retos Extraordinarios after her son's case, has argued publicly that Mexico's failure to make cannabis-derived medication genuinely accessible violates Article 4 of the Mexican Constitution, which guarantees the right to health. She has estimated that roughly fifty Mexican families travel to Colombia to obtain medications unavailable at home — a workaround open only to those who can afford the trip.

That is the throughline connecting Jalisco's legislative push to a pattern seen across Latin America: laws that recognize a patient's right to cannabis-based treatment on paper, undercut by regulatory agencies, customs regimes, and health systems that were never built to enforce that right in practice. The children at the center of this fight are not abstractions in a policy debate. They are named patients, with named diagnoses, whose parents have had to become lobbyists, litigants, and, in Garfias's case, informal drug importers, simply to administer a medication a federal regulator has already approved them to receive. Whether the Mexican Congress acts on Jalisco's exhortos, or whether SAT and the National Guard adopt a protocol for recognizing existing import authorizations, will determine whether that burden keeps falling on parents instead of the state.
