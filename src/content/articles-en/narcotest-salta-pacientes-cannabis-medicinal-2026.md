Four people got into their cars in Salta on a June weekend the same way they had hundreds of times before. None of them was impaired at the moment they drove. But when the Municipality activated roadside narcotests for the first time, all four tested positive for THC in saliva. The outcome was automatic and identical to that of a drunk driver: vehicle impoundment, license retention, a fine, and the loss of twenty points from their driving record. One driver's car was eventually picked up by a family member.

The episode, documented by El Tribuno de Salta and Página/12 in the first weeks of June 2026, quickly outgrew the frame of a routine traffic violation. Because among the drivers caught up in these operations are, potentially, patients registered with REPROCANN — Argentina's National Cannabis Program Registry, created under National Law 27,350 — who use cannabis on medical advice to treat chronic pain, epilepsy, fibromyalgia, or the aftereffects of cancer treatment. For them, a positive narcotest is not evidence of driving under the influence. It is, simply, proof that they are following a legal treatment.

## Four positives, one structural problem

The program launched by the municipal Transit Secretariat uses an electronic device that analyzes a saliva sample and delivers a result within about five minutes, detecting cocaine, THC, and amphetamines through a cutoff threshold. Traffic Secretary Matías Assennato explained that the device "has a set threshold above which it begins registering as positive or negative," aiming to dispel concerns about false positives from passive smoke exposure.

But the threshold solves a technical problem while leaving a medical-legal one untouched: the device detects the presence of the substance, not the degree of impairment of the person driving. And that is precisely where traffic policy collides head-on with health policy.

## The body keeps the trace, not the proof

Unlike alcohol, whose elimination from the body follows a relatively predictable curve, THC can remain detectable in saliva for 48 to 72 hours after use in people who consume it regularly, according to specialists consulted by local outlets. That detection window bears no direct relationship to the psychoactive effect, which in most forms of use lasts only a few hours.

Attorney Martín Galván, a cannabis law specialist, was blunt about it: "a positive result doesn't always mean a person is unfit to drive," he said, also criticizing the municipality for rolling out the controls without prior dialogue with sector stakeholders, such as the Cannabis Producers Chamber. Attorney and activist Fernando Ruarte made a similar argument, contending that medical cannabis use cannot be equated with substances used purely recreationally, and that a patient should not have to choose between sustaining a treatment and keeping a driver's license.

The detail is not minor in a province where the debate over saliva testing already has history. Salta's Court of Appeals (Sala I del Tribunal de Impugnación) had previously warned, in a precedent cited by Diario Judicial, that this type of field test amounts to no more than "preliminary guidance" that "does not meet the certainty standard of laboratory evidence," and can produce false positives — in that case, from chewing coca leaf, an ancestral and everyday practice across much of northern Argentina. Provincial Road Safety Undersecretary Francisco Fleming himself acknowledged that same technical limitation, warning that the reagents cannot always distinguish traditional coca chewing (coqueo) from cocaine use — exposing a broader pattern: control technologies designed to catch illegal drug use keep ending up criminalizing legal, and even culturally rooted, practices that were never their intended target.

## A legal framework that doesn't talk to itself

Law 27,350 and its regulations recognize REPROCANN patients' right to carry up to 40 grams of dried flower or 30 milliliters of oil in any mode of transportation. On paper, it is a settled right. But the municipal traffic ordinance — which under Article 5.4.8 penalizes driving under substances that reduce fitness to drive — carries no exception or differentiated protocol for people holding that authorization. The result is a system where two equally valid rules pull in opposite directions, and where the burden of resolving the contradiction falls, in practice, on the patient stopped on the road.

City councilor Agustina Álvarez, who chairs the Council's Transit Commission, asked to know the model of device being used, its certification status, and the exact detection protocol before fully endorsing the measure. It is a minimal transparency demand that, even so, has yet to receive a complete public answer from the municipality.

## A partial retreat, and a question still open

On June 17, under technical and legal pressure, Mayor Emiliano Durand narrowed the scope of the controls: "It will now apply only in cases of accidents. The people involved will be given a blood-alcohol test and a test to see if they are intoxicated," he announced, stepping back from random roadside checks.

It is a meaningful retreat, but only a partial one. The decision reduces patients' everyday exposure to a surprise stop on the way to work or to pick up their kids from school. It does not, however, resolve the underlying question: what happens to a REPROCANN patient involved in even a minor collision, if testing positive for THC is still automatically equated with driving intoxicated, with no room for a doctor or forensic examiner to assess whether that person was actually fit to drive. That protocol gap — the absence of a clinical, not merely toxicological, standard for telling treatment apart from intoxication — is exactly what specialists and patients have been demanding since the controversy erupted, and neither the province nor the Salta municipality have resolved it yet.

Salta's case is not isolated within the region's broader landscape of access to cannabis-based healthcare. In Peru, where more than 55,000 patients are registered for medical use, only 34% manage to access products through formal channels, while the rest remain exposed to criminal complaints, seizures, and raids for sustaining treatments the law itself recognizes. The pattern repeats with local variations: forward-looking laws on paper, enforced by agencies — traffic, health, police — that don't coordinate with each other or update their protocols at the pace at which cannabis's legal status is changing. Until that gap closes, every patient will keep individually carrying the risk that a five-minute device will decide, on the side of the road, whether their treatment is medicine or a crime.
