In Latin America, cannabis reform has mostly arrived through familiar channels: a supreme court ruling in Mexico, a state monopoly built by legislation in Uruguay, a patient registry fought for in Argentine courts. In the Cayman Islands — a British Overseas Territory that shares the Caribbean's geography, its colonial legal inheritance, and much of the region's ambivalence about drug policy — reform is moving through a mechanism almost unheard of elsewhere in the hemisphere: a direct referendum, followed fifteen months later by a government commission asked to decide how much of that mandate to actually honor. On July 1, 2026, the commission answered. Its proposal is real, but it is also the narrowest version of reform on the table.

## A referendum that took fifteen months to become a proposal

Cayman lawmakers agreed in December 2022 to put cannabis decriminalization to a public vote, according to the territory's own legislative record cited by Wikipedia's summary of Cayman drug law. That vote finally happened in April 2025, held alongside the general election, asking Caymanians whether they supported "decriminalisation of the consumption and possession of small amounts of cannabis." The result was a clear but not overwhelming majority: 55.64 percent in favor, according to Cayman News Service's tally, with Marijuana Moment reporting the wider breakdown as roughly 56 percent yes to 36 percent no, with the remainder spoiled or rejected ballots. The referendum was non-binding, which meant the government could, in theory, have shelved it.

It did not shelve it, but it did not move quickly either. The question was referred to the territory's Law Reform Commission, an independent statutory body tasked with reviewing legislation and proposing reform. More than a year after voters went to the polls, the commission published its answer: a 69-page discussion paper titled "Cannabis Reform: Options for a Harm Minimisation Framework," open for public comment through September 1, 2026. As of publication, cannabis possession for recreational purposes remains fully illegal in the Cayman Islands — the paper is a set of options, not a law.

## Decriminalization, not legalization

The commission's paper is explicit about the distinction it is drawing, and it matters for what comes next. Decriminalization, as the paper defines it, means replacing criminal penalties for possession and consumption with administrative penalties, or removing penalties altogether, while production, distribution and sale remain illegal. Legalization means a regulated market — licensed cultivation, retail, taxation — comparable to how alcohol or tobacco are sold. After reviewing models from 16 other jurisdictions, the commission recommended the first path, not the second: decriminalizing personal possession and consumption while keeping commercial cultivation, importation, distribution and retail sale prohibited. That combination, the authors wrote, "would be most likely to reduce the negative impacts of prohibition while mitigating the risks of reform" — a formulation that reads less like a mandate honored than a mandate managed.

The specific parameters on the table: a possession threshold of 30 grams of dried cannabis or 150 grams fresh; home cultivation of up to four plants per household; consumption restricted to private residences; separate, stricter provisions for minors; and consideration of expunging historic convictions for minor possession. The paper's underlying reasoning is public-health-focused rather than libertarian: it argues that "cannabis prohibition has been generally ineffective in deterring its use," noting cannabis remains the world's most commonly used illicit drug regardless of legal status, and that prohibition itself "has the potential to exacerbate the negative health impacts of cannabis use" because unregulated users have no way to know the potency or purity of what they consume.

## The gap a decriminalize-only model leaves open

The proposal's internal tension is also its most cited criticism. If commercial cultivation, importation and retail all stay illegal, and only home growing of up to four plants is permitted, then anyone who does not grow their own cannabis — because they lack space, time, knowledge, or simply the inclination — has no legal way to obtain it. They remain exactly where the illegal market leaves them today: buying a product of unknown potency from an unregulated supplier, just without the criminal record for possessing it afterward. Among the public submissions Cayman News Service highlighted, one came from a physician who argued the commission should recommend full legalization rather than decriminalization alone, on the grounds that a decrim-only framework keeps consumers dependent on the same illicit supply chain the paper says it wants to undercut — undermining the report's own stated harm-reduction logic for anyone who cannot or does not cultivate at home.

That gap sits alongside an existing, if narrow, medical cannabis program. Cayman legalized cannabis extracts for prescribed medical use in May 2017, but flower itself remains prohibited even for patients: the only permitted forms are oils, capsules and vaporizer cartridges, according to medical cannabis clinics operating in the territory. More than 500 patients have used the program since 2017 for conditions including multiple sclerosis, Crohn's disease, chronic pain and chemotherapy-related symptoms, per those same clinics — a sign that even Cayman's medical framework has historically been built around caution and restricted product forms rather than broad access, which helps explain why the discussion paper's authors treated a decrim-only recreational model as the ambitious end of what is politically achievable, not the cautious one.

## London's shadow over Caribbean reform

There is a further constraint specific to Cayman's status that has no real equivalent in Latin America's own reform fights, where the sovereignty question is usually about domestic drug policy versus U.S. pressure or UN treaty obligations. As a British Overseas Territory, any bill that eventually emerges from this process will need to pass the Cayman Islands Legislative Assembly and then receive assent from a Governor appointed by London. That step is not a formality. In September 2022, Bermuda's Parliament passed the Cannabis Licensing Bill, a measure to legalize and regulate cannabis sales — and the UK's Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office instructed Bermuda's governor, Rena Lalgie, to withhold royal assent, according to the governor's own published statement, on the grounds that the bill as drafted was inconsistent with the UK and Bermuda's obligations under the 1961 Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs and the 1971 Convention on Psychotropic Substances. The bill never became law. That episode is the closest precedent for what Cayman is attempting, and it plausibly shapes the caution built into the July 2026 discussion paper: recommending decriminalization without a legal retail market is, among other things, a proposal calibrated to avoid the kind of confrontation with London that sank Bermuda's more ambitious law.

For a Latin American readership accustomed to reform fights framed as national governments asserting authority over their own drug policy — against the DEA's influence in Mexico, against the UN scheduling regime that Venezuela still invokes, against pressure from Washington more broadly — Cayman's story is a reminder that in parts of the Caribbean the equivalent fight is with a different capital. A referendum mandate can be diluted once by a domestic commission choosing the most cautious option on its own table, and diluted again, later, by a colonial veto it never had to justify to Caymanian voters at all. The 77,000 residents of the Cayman Islands, according to United Nations population data compiled by Worldometer, will spend the next several weeks submitting comments on a proposal that, even if adopted in full, would still leave them further from a regulated cannabis market than most of the Latin American mainland already is.
