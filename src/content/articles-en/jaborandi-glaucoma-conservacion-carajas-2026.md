In the mining heartland of southeastern Pará, where open pits carved out of the Serra dos Carajás supply nearly a fifth of the world's iron ore, a much smaller and far less visible resource is quietly propping up an entire category of modern medicine. Jaborandi, a knee-high Amazonian shrub with serrated, leathery leaves, is the only known natural source of pilocarpine, the alkaloid used in eye drops for glaucoma and in tablets prescribed for chronic dry mouth. There is no industrial synthetic substitute. If jaborandi disappears from the wild, so does a link in the supply chain for millions of prescriptions worldwide — a fact that has pushed Brazilian researchers to test an unusual conservation strategy in one of the most heavily mined landscapes in Latin America.

A study published June 3, 2026, in the journal PLOS One, led by Diego Escobar and Cecílio Caldeira Frois of the Instituto Tecnológico Vale (ITV), describes a four-year experiment to build what the authors call an "inter situ" collection of jaborandi inside the Carajás National Forest. The approach sits between two more familiar conservation strategies: ex situ collections, which store seeds or specimens away from their native habitat (in botanical gardens or seed banks), and in situ conservation, which protects wild populations where they already grow. Inter situ conservation instead replants genetically representative material from wild populations into new, semi-natural sites within the same broader ecosystem — in this case, forest areas that Vale's mining operations had disturbed and were actively restoring.

The method exists because jaborandi's biology leaves few other options. Its seeds are recalcitrant, meaning they lose viability within weeks and cannot be dried or frozen the way conventional seed banks require for long-term storage. "Jaborandi seeds lose viability rapidly, which complicates conservation through traditional means," Frois told the Brazilian science-communication agency Agência Bori, explaining why the team chose to grow living plants rather than bank dormant seeds. Jaborandi (formally Pilocarpus microphyllus, in the citrus family Rutaceae) is already listed as Vulnerable on Brazil's official Red List of Flora, a status researchers attribute to decades of unregulated leaf harvesting for the pharmaceutical trade combined with habitat loss.

## From Amazonian Sialogogue to European Pharmacy Shelf

Jaborandi's medical career did not begin in a laboratory. Portuguese chroniclers recorded its use in Brazil as early as 1587, and its name derives from Tupi-Guarani, roughly translating to "the plant that causes the mouth to drool" — a plain-spoken reference to the intense salivation the leaves induce when chewed or brewed, a use documented among Indigenous and traditional communities in the Amazon and the northeastern Brazilian interior for generations. That folk knowledge of jaborandi's sialogogue and sweat-inducing effects is what put it on the radar of Western medicine: in 1873, the Brazilian physician Symphronio Coutinho carried leaf samples to Europe, where French chemist Ernest Hardy and English chemist A.W. Gerrard independently isolated the alkaloid pilocarpine in 1875. Ophthalmologists adopted it the following year after discovering it could reliably lower intraocular pressure, making it, alongside physostigmine, one of the oldest continuously used drug classes in glaucoma treatment — a role it still plays as a low-cost option in many health systems, and as a rescue drug where laser or surgical options are unavailable. Later in the twentieth century, pilocarpine tablets (marketed as Salagen, among other brands) became a standard treatment for xerostomia, the severe dry mouth that follows head-and-neck radiotherapy or accompanies autoimmune conditions such as Sjögren's syndrome.

That two-century medical footprint sits on a narrow biological base. Roughly half of the pilocarpine sold globally still comes from leaves stripped from wild plants rather than cultivated stock, according to prior research on jaborandi's economic botany, and almost all of that wild harvest is concentrated in a handful of northern and northeastern Brazilian states, including the forests around Carajás, where cooperatives have gathered jaborandi leaves commercially since the 1980s. Concentrating so much of a global drug supply chain in one wild, overharvested species in one region is precisely the vulnerability the new study set out to address.

## Four Years, Four Populations, One Cooperative

Between 2020 and 2023, the ITV team collected seeds from four genetically distinct wild jaborandi populations during peak fruiting season in August and September, deliberately capping the harvest at no more than 20 percent of each plant's reproductive output to avoid depleting the source populations. Seeds were cold-stored for about 60 days, germinated in vermiculite under greenhouse conditions, and acclimated for five months at reduced sunlight before being transplanted into four separate ecological restoration plots inside the national forest, each at least a kilometer apart to prevent unwanted cross-pollination between distinct genetic lines.

The results were uneven but broadly encouraging, according to the paper. Three of the four transplanted populations exceeded the researchers' genetic-diversity target of 40 maternal seed lines, in some cases by more than double, and by 2023 those three populations had each established more than 500 living individuals, for a combined total of roughly 2,400 surviving plants. First-year field survival varied considerably by population and harvest year, ranging from as low as 20 percent to 100 percent; the fourth population lagged behind the others throughout the study and will need continued intervention, the authors note. Long-term success — whether these replanted populations will reproduce naturally and sustain themselves once researchers step back — remains an open question, since the transplanted jaborandi has not yet reached full reproductive maturity.

A cooperative called CoEx-Carajás, made up of families with generations of experience managing and harvesting jaborandi inside the forest, was involved from the project's planning stages, contributing practical knowledge of harvest timing, plant behavior and terrain that shaped where and how the new populations were established. The involvement reads less as symbolic outreach than as a genuine input into the study's field methodology — the kind of local ecological expertise that pharmacological researchers have historically undervalued when working with extractivist communities.

## A Conservation Project Funded by the Miner Next Door

The study's institutional setting is worth stating plainly rather than glossing over. The Instituto Tecnológico Vale is the corporate research arm of Vale, the mining company that operates the Carajás iron complex, and the project was funded through an internal Vale grant. The restoration plots where jaborandi was replanted are areas the company disturbed through mining and is now required, under Brazilian environmental law, to rehabilitate. That does not make the science less rigorous — the paper went through PLOS One's peer review, and its authors are candid about the study's limitations, including the underperforming fourth population and the unresolved question of long-term reproductive viability. But it does mean the fate of a medicinally important wild species is, in this instance, entangled with the restoration obligations of the same industry reshaping the landscape around it. Readers evaluating the promise of "inter situ" conservation as a model for other Amazonian plants with recalcitrant seeds should weigh that context alongside the encouraging survival numbers.

For a magazine that scrutinizes the science behind healing plants as closely as its pharmacology, jaborandi is a useful reminder that a medicine's supply chain is rarely just chemistry. It is Tupi-Guarani knowledge documented four centuries ago, a French chemist's 1875 isolation of a single alkaloid, a Brazilian cooperative's decades of sustainable-harvest practice, and now a mining company's research institute — all converging on a shrub that most glaucoma patients using its derivative eye drops have never heard of.
