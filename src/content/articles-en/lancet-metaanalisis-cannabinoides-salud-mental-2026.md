The largest randomized-controlled-trial-only review of cannabinoids for mental health and substance use disorders ever assembled has reached a conclusion that cuts against much of the momentum behind medical cannabis expansion: for most of the conditions it examined, the evidence does not support routine prescribing. The review, led by researchers at the University of Sydney's Matilda Centre and published in The Lancet Psychiatry on March 16, 2026, screened 5,774 studies and narrowed them to 54 randomized controlled trials meeting its inclusion criteria — a combined 2,477 participants, drawn from trials published between 1980 and May 2025.

For a field that has expanded largely on the strength of observational data, patient testimony and regulatory approvals granted without gold-standard trial evidence, a review built exclusively on RCTs functions as a stress test. The results are uneven, and in one case, alarming.

## Where the Evidence Falls Short

Across six conditions — anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, psychotic disorders, obsessive-compulsive disorder, anorexia nervosa and opioid use disorder — the meta-analysis found no statistically significant benefit from cannabinoids compared with placebo. For depression, the review could not evaluate benefit or harm at all: it found zero randomized controlled trials testing cannabinoids against the condition, a striking gap given how frequently cannabis is used, prescribed or self-administered specifically to manage depressive symptoms.

The most concerning finding concerns cocaine use disorder. Rather than showing no effect, the review found cannabinoids significantly increased cocaine cravings relative to placebo — the opposite of the harm-reduction effect some clinicians and patients have assumed. It is the kind of result that reorders clinical caution rather than confirming it: a substance sometimes recommended as a substitute or bridge therapy in substance-use treatment showed a measurable adverse signal for one of the exact populations it is sometimes used to help.

## Where the Evidence Points the Other Way

The picture is not uniformly negative. For four conditions, the review found signals of benefit, though every one of them came with a "very low" or "low" certainty rating under the evidence-grading standard the authors used. A combination of CBD and THC reduced both withdrawal symptoms and weekly cannabis use among people with cannabis use disorder — a somewhat circular but clinically relevant finding, since cannabis use disorder itself is a growing referral category. Cannabinoids also showed reduced tic severity in Tourette's syndrome and reduced autistic traits in autism spectrum disorder, both at very low certainty. For insomnia, the review found cannabinoids increased total sleep time at moderate certainty — but that finding lost statistical significance once trials rated at high risk of bias were excluded, meaning the apparent benefit may be an artifact of weaker studies rather than a real effect.

On safety, the review found that for roughly every seven patients treated with cannabinoids, one experienced an adverse event that did not occur in the placebo group — though rates of serious adverse events, the kind requiring hospitalization or causing lasting harm, did not differ significantly between cannabinoid and placebo arms.

## A Field Built on Thin Trials

The methodological detail that gives the results their weight is not just what was found, but how it was found. Of the 54 trials included, 24 were rated at high risk of bias — nearly half the evidence base the field's strongest positive claims are frequently built on. The median trial size was just 31.5 participants, far below what is typically needed to detect anything but a large effect with confidence. And roughly one in five trials disclosed conflicts of interest, a detail the authors flag as relevant to how cautiously the positive findings, in particular, should be read.

The authors' policy recommendation follows directly from that math: larger, better-designed trials are needed before cannabinoids can be prescribed for most mental health conditions with confidence, and regulators should apply particular scrutiny to markets where the clinicians writing prescriptions have a direct financial stake in patients continuing treatment — a dynamic increasingly present as medical cannabis systems commercialize.

## Reading Certainty Ratings Correctly

The "very low," "low" and "moderate" certainty ratings attached to each finding are not a stylistic hedge — they come from GRADE, the evidence-grading framework used across evidence-based medicine to describe how much a finding's certainty could change as better research arrives. A "very low certainty" finding of benefit, in practice, means the true effect could turn out to be null, harmful, or considerably larger than measured — the studies simply are not strong enough yet to narrow that range. That distinction matters enormously for how the four positive findings in this review should be communicated to patients: a doctor citing "reduced tic severity in Tourette's syndrome" without noting the very-low-certainty label is describing the evidence more confidently than the evidence itself supports.

The gap between a single positive trial and clinical guidance built to withstand scrutiny is exactly what this review was designed to expose. Observational studies and patient-reported outcomes — the evidence base much of the current medical cannabis expansion has relied on — are useful for generating hypotheses and documenting real-world experience, but they cannot control for placebo effect, expectation bias or the tendency of patients who feel cannabis is helping them to be the ones who stay in treatment and report back, while those for whom it does not work quietly drop out of view. Randomized controlled trials exist specifically to close that gap, and this review's central finding is how few of them exist for cannabis compared with how widely the treatment is already prescribed.

## The Latin American Stakes

None of the trials reviewed originated in Latin America, and the review itself makes no claims about regional prescribing patterns. But the findings land at a moment when several Latin American countries are expanding, not narrowing, the conditions for which cannabis-based treatment is authorized. Argentina's Ley 27.350 has broadened its recognized indications since 2017; Brazil's ANVISA has approved a growing catalogue of cannabis-derived products through an accelerated regulatory track; and across the region, patient associations and prescribing physicians frequently cite anxiety, PTSD and related conditions among the reasons cannabis-based treatment is sought — precisely the categories where this review found no significant benefit over placebo.

That gap between regulatory expansion and trial-grade evidence is not unique to Latin America; the same pattern has driven prescribing in the United States, Canada and parts of Europe. But it carries particular weight in health systems where private insurance coverage is inconsistent and many patients pay for cannabis-based treatment out of pocket — meaning the cost of prescribing on the strength of weak evidence falls, disproportionately, on the patients themselves. The review does not argue cannabis-based treatment should be withdrawn from patients currently using it. It argues that the next generation of trials needs to be built to the standard the current evidence base has failed to meet — and that, until then, regulators and prescribers should be candid with patients about exactly how thin that evidence still is for most of the conditions cannabis is prescribed to treat.
