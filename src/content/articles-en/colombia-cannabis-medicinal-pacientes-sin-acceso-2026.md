title: "Ten Years of Legal Medical Cannabis in Colombia: Barely a Thousand Patients Access It Through the Health System"
excerpt: "Colombia has regulated medical cannabis since 2015 and exports it worldwide, yet only a handful of patients access it through the health system. A new decree promises change while home cultivation remains a legal gray zone."
tag: "Rights"
---

Colombia legalized medicinal cannabis in 2015, opening a regulatory path that was supposed to make it one of the region's leading medical markets. A decade later, the country has issued more than 6,000 cultivation and production licenses, built an export industry that reaches Europe and North America, and become a magnet for foreign investment. What it has not done, according to Colombian doctors, patients and the country's own Constitutional Court, is guarantee that the medicine actually reaches the people who need it.

Estimates cited by the investigative desk of El Tiempo put the number of patients accessing cannabis-based treatment through Colombia's formal health system at little more than a thousand — compared with more than 300,000 patients in countries with mature medical cannabis programs. The gap between a decade of regulation and a patient base that small is the starting point for understanding why, in 2026, Colombia is trying once again to fix medicinal cannabis access with a new decree, while the barriers that kept patients out in the first place — cost, insurer refusals, and legal ambiguity around home cultivation — remain largely unresolved.

## A market built for export, not for patients

Colombia's cannabis licensing system exploded early and then collapsed. According to figures reported by El Tiempo, licenses issued by the Colombian Agricultural Institute, the Justice Ministry and the national drug regulator INVIMA peaked at 928 in 2020 before falling to 57 in 2023 and just 16 in the first seven months of 2024. Export revenue tells a similar story of underperformance relative to early projections: Colombia exported $10.8 million in cannabis products in 2023 and just over $3 million in the first half of 2024, a fraction of the nearly $800 million industry analysts had once forecast for 2025.

"Because the domestic market was never developed, the industry is in decline," Julián Wilches, co-founder of the Colombian cannabis firm Clever Leaves, told El Tiempo. Sandra Carrillo, president of the Colombian Association of Medicinal Cannabis (Asomedccan), was blunter about what that has meant for patients: "The situation in Colombia has worsened. The pharmacies that had INVIMA approval have been disappearing." Colombia's cannabis industry, in other words, was designed to sell abroad — and largely failed even at that — while the domestic patients the 2015 regulation was meant to serve were left with a shrinking number of places to actually fill a prescription.

## What the Constitutional Court ordered

The clearest evidence that this is a rights problem, not just a market failure, came from Colombia's Constitutional Court. In a ruling published in February 2024, the Court's Second Review Chamber sided with two patients — a 55-year-old woman with chronic polyarticular pain and a 63-year-old woman with fibromyalgia and insomnia — whose insurer, Famisanar, had refused to authorize cannabis-based preparations their physicians had prescribed. Famisanar argued the treatments fell outside the mandatory health benefits plan and were not properly regulated.

The Court rejected that reasoning outright, ruling that cannabis-based medications are financed through the same capitation payment mechanism that funds other covered treatments, and that denying them on cost or bureaucratic grounds amounted to "a violation of the fundamental guarantee to dignified life." It ordered Famisanar to authorize and continue supplying the prescribed preparations, and directed the Ministry of Health and the Institute for Health Technology Evaluation (IETS) to build clearer protocols so future patients would not have to sue their way into treatment. A 2025 narrative review published in the Revista Colombiana de Ciencias Químico-Farmacéuticas found the pattern behind that lawsuit was structural: researchers identified low purchasing power, physician distrust and stigma as barriers on the patient side, and slow authorization procedures and limited medication availability as barriers on the health-system side.

## The decree that promises to open the door — again

Colombia's government answered with Decreto 1138 de 2025, issued by the Ministry of Health on October 27, 2025. For the first time, the decree recognizes dried cannabis flower as a finished medical product that can be sold directly in authorized pharmacies under prescription, alongside extracts, oils, capsules and ointments that were already permitted. The Health Ministry described it as positioning Colombia "at the forefront of responsible medical cannabis access, with a social and productive approach," and paired the flower authorization with incentives meant to bring small cultivators, farmer associations and ethnic communities into a market long dominated by a handful of large exporters.

The decree assigned INVIMA a deadline of March 2026 to publish the technical regulations that will actually make the flower provision usable: manufacturing standards, cannabinoid testing thresholds, prescription and dispensing rules, and a traceability system linked to the Justice Ministry's seed-to-sale registry. As of this writing, patient advocates and industry groups are still watching to see whether that regulatory architecture, once finalized, meaningfully lowers the price and improves the availability of treatment for patients like the ones who had to sue Famisanar — or whether it repeats the export-first pattern that left the domestic market underdeveloped for the better part of a decade.

## The gray zone that criminalizes the people the law was meant to protect

For patients who cannot wait on pharmacy shelves that keep disappearing or insurers that fight every prescription, Colombian law technically allows another path: home cultivation of up to 20 plants for personal medical use, without a license. In practice, cannabis policy researchers describe that provision as functioning inside a legal gray zone. The absence of clear operational guidance for police and prosecutors on how to distinguish authorized medical self-cultivation from unlicensed trafficking has produced what one analysis called "ambiguous interpretations of the law and unequal application by authorities" — leaving patients who grow their own medicine exposed to seizure, prosecution or the burden of proving in court that a garden of cannabis plants is treatment rather than crime.

That is the quieter, harder-to-quantify cost of Colombia's decade-long implementation gap: it is not only that too few patients can afford or access cannabis medicine through pharmacies and insurers, but that the patients who respond to that failure by growing their own plants remain, in effect, unprotected by the very regulation that was supposed to legalize their treatment. A country can build a legal cannabis export industry — Colombia has — while leaving the sick person with a home garden and a doctor's prescription still one police encounter away from a courtroom.

Whether Decreto 1138 and INVIMA's pending technical rules close that gap, or simply relocate it, will depend on decisions still being written into regulation as this article goes to press. For the patients who have spent a decade watching Colombia's cannabis industry grow abroad while pharmacies at home ran out of product, that answer cannot come soon enough.
