excerpt: "A scientometric review of 183 studies found that only two of five medicinal plants sold in Brazilian markets — cumaru and chambá — have human clinical trials; the other three remain untested in people despite decades of lab research."

---

In the public markets of Fortaleza, Recife and Belém, the same five plants show up on nearly every herbalist's stall: bark of cumaru for a wheezing chest, a spoonful of chambá syrup for a child's cough, jucá pods steeped for a stomach ache, a sprig of orégano de monte crushed into an infusion for infection, and the tart fruit of the caja tree offered as a tonic. For generations, this has been how millions of Brazilians treat everyday ailments, often because the public health system is slow, distant, or simply absent from their neighborhood. What has been harder to answer, until now, is a more uncomfortable question: how much of that knowledge actually holds up when it is tested the way medicine is supposed to be tested — and how much is still, honestly, an open question?

A scientometric review published in March 2026 in the journal *Diversity* by researchers at the Federal University of Ceará, Brazil's Embrapa Tropical Agroindustry, and Mexico's Center for Food and Development Research set out to answer exactly that, for five of the region's most-cited medicinal species: *Amburana cearensis* (cumaru or imburana), *Justicia pectoralis* (chambá or anador), *Libidibia ferrea* (jucá or pau-ferro), *Lippia origanoides* (orégano de monte, sometimes called salvia brava) and *Spondias mombin* (jobo, caja or taperebá). The result is less a triumphant vindication of traditional medicine than a sober map of where the evidence is strong, where it is thin, and where — after more than three decades of laboratory work — it simply does not exist yet.

## A field built mostly on Web of Science, not on wards

The review's authors combed the Web of Science Core Collection for every indexed study on the five species between 1991 and 2024, using bibliometric software to trace who published what, where, and how often it was cited. They found 183 publications in total. Brazil accounted for more than 60% of them, and Brazilian institutions and authors dominated the field's productivity and citation counts — a reminder that this is, first and foremost, homegrown science, not knowledge extracted and validated somewhere else. The Journal of Ethnopharmacology was the leading outlet, followed by Industrial Crops and Products, which the authors read as a sign that ethnobotanical documentation and applied product development are closely linked in this literature.

But the same analysis exposed a lopsided structure. Keyword networks were dominated overwhelmingly by two words: "antioxidant" and "antimicrobial," almost always tied to "in vitro" — meaning results generated in a test tube or a petri dish, not in a human body. Traditional-use descriptions, the review notes, increasingly function as a starting point for choosing which compound to screen next, rather than as evidence in their own right. That is a defensible use of ethnobotanical knowledge. It is also, bluntly, not the same thing as proof that a remedy works.

## Two plants have clinical trials. Three do not.

The starkest finding in the review is procedural rather than chemical: of the five species, only two have ever been tested in a randomized controlled trial in humans.

*Amburana cearensis* is the more mature case. A double-blind, placebo-controlled trial published in the Brazilian Journal of Pharmaceutical Sciences gave adults with mild persistent asthma a standardized cumaru syrup as an add-on to their usual treatment for fifteen days. Some 61.9% of the treated group reported meaningful improvement in their asthma-related quality of life, compared with 9.5% in the placebo group, and lung-function measurements moved in the same direction. No serious adverse events were recorded. It is a real result — but the review is careful to flag what it does not show: the trial tested cumaru only as a complement to standard asthma treatment, only in mild cases, only over two weeks, and without a standardized measure of how much active compound — coumarin, chiefly — each dose actually contained.

*Justicia pectoralis* has a similar, smaller signal. A randomized trial with 114 children, split between a chambá syrup group and a placebo group, found that the syrup cut cough frequency and severity and improved nasal congestion and sleep within 48 hours, with no serious adverse events. Again: real, but short — two days of follow-up is not enough to say anything about repeated or long-term use in children, and the coumarin content of the syrup used was not tightly quantified either.

For the other three plants, the review states plainly: no human clinical study has been identified for any of them, despite decades of laboratory evidence. *Lippia origanoides* essential oil has shown activity against drug-resistant fungal infections and even anticonvulsant effects in rodent models of induced seizures — striking results that have never been tested in a person. *Libidibia ferrea* has a dense body of antimicrobial and antioxidant data and is even being explored against tumor cells in mice, but has never been dosed in a controlled human trial. *Spondias mombin* has the deepest literature of the five, going back to a widely cited 1991 study on its antiviral compounds, yet it also carries a specific caution the review flags directly: some of its preclinical signals point toward reproductive effects, meaning pregnant patients in particular should not assume traditional use equals safety.

## The plant itself may not survive to be studied

There is a further irony sitting underneath the cumaru story that the review does not dwell on but that matters for anyone thinking about where this medicine actually comes from: *Amburana cearensis* is listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List. A 2015 study in the conservation journal *Oryx*, based on fieldwork in northwestern Argentina, documented population decline driven largely by logging for its valuable, aromatic hardwood — the same tree prized in carpentry is the one steeped for asthma and bronchitis. Where logging bans are enforced, as in Argentina's Jujuy province, tree populations skew older and larger; where selective cutting is permitted, as in neighboring Salta, few trees survive long enough to reach reproductive size. A remedy with the best clinical evidence of the five is, at the same time, a tree whose long-term supply is not guaranteed.

## What the gap actually means for patients

None of this should be read as license to dismiss traditional plant medicine, and the review's authors are explicit that it should not be read that way either. Ethnobotanical persistence — the fact that a plant has been used, safely, across generations and communities — is real evidence of something, even if it is not proof of a specific pharmacological claim. What the review argues for instead is closing what its authors call the field's "evidence imbalance": more chemical standardization so a dose of cumaru syrup means the same thing in São Paulo as it does in Fortaleza, and more controlled human trials, particularly for jucá, orégano de monte and caja, before any of the five plants can reasonably be recommended by a health system rather than a neighbor.

For the many Latin Americans who reach for these plants precisely because formal healthcare is out of reach, that distinction is not academic. A syrup that works as a complement to prescribed asthma treatment is a genuinely useful tool. A syrup marketed as equivalent to that treatment, on the strength of tradition alone, is a different and riskier claim — and right now, for three of these five plants, the clinical evidence to settle that question simply is not there yet.
