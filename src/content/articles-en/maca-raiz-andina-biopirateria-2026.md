On the Bombón Plateau, a windswept expanse of the central Peruvian Andes straddling the Junín and Pasco regions, farmers have spent two millennia coaxing a small, radish-shaped root out of ground that freezes most nights of the year. *Lepidium meyenii* — maca — grows exclusively between roughly 4,000 and 4,500 meters above sea level, in "the harshest and coldest areas" where other crops fail, as the Spanish chronicler Father Bernabé Cobo already noted in 1653. It is, by most accounts, the highest-altitude food crop on Earth.

That combination of extremity and endurance is precisely what turned maca into a target. Over the past three decades, the same plant that Andean communities have dried, boiled into a sweet porridge called *mazamorra*, and offered to their deities for abundant harvests and fertility has also been patented abroad, smuggled out as contraband seed, and cultivated at industrial scale by a country that never grew it before 2012. In May 2026, Peru's Congress approved the ratification of a global treaty designed to make that harder to do again — and in July, at the World Intellectual Property Organization's General Assembly in Geneva, Peru became the first country in Latin America and the Caribbean to formally join it.

## A Crop Born Above the Frost Line

Maca's cultivation dates back an estimated 1,300 to 2,000 years, with archaeobotanical evidence pointing to domestication near San Blas, in what is today the district of Ondores, Junín, according to a peer-reviewed ethnobiological and ethnopharmacological review published in the *Journal of Ethnopharmacology*. The earliest written account comes from the chronicler Pedro Cieza de León, who in 1553 described natives of the Bombón province subsisting on roots that could survive where little else could.

Traditional preparation was — and largely remains — simple: the hypocotyls are sun-dried for weeks, which allows them to be stored for years, then boiled in water or milk before eating. Andean communities used maca as a staple food, an energizer for travelers and llama herders crossing the punas, and, according to ethnographic accounts, in preparations linked to fertility and in ritual offerings tied to good harvests. By the 1980s, however, the crop had fallen so far out of active cultivation — an estimated 50 hectares nationwide — that Peruvian researchers considered it functionally endangered.

## From "Andean Viagra" to a Patent Fight

Maca's global rediscovery in the late 1990s came with a distortion attached. Peru's own government, under President Alberto Fujimori, promoted the root to Japanese buyers as an "Andean viagra," a marketing shorthand for erectile-dysfunction relief that stuck in international media and persists today, despite never being an accurate description of either the plant's traditional use or the scientific evidence behind it.

The commercial interest that followed produced the opposite of protection. In 2001, the New Jersey-based company Pure World Botanicals obtained U.S. Patent No. 6,267,995 covering an extraction method for maca's libido-associated compounds, marketed as MacaPure; Biotics Research Corporation had already secured a separate 2000 patent combining maca with deer antler for testosterone augmentation. Peruvian farmers and indigenous organizations denounced both patents, not to seek exclusive rights of their own, but to have the plant returned to the public domain, according to a report at the time by the advocacy network GMWatch. A comparative case study on bioprospecting and biopiracy in Latin America later counted the maca dispute among the region's clearest examples of a foreign entity patenting knowledge that Andean communities had already possessed for centuries.

Peru's National Commission Against Biopiracy — coordinated by the intellectual-property regulator Indecopi — later determined that maca seed had left the country illegally around 2002 and 2003, smuggled out disguised as flour shipments routed through Bolivia and the port of Callao, according to reporting by Mongabay Latam. Iván Manrique, a researcher affiliated with Peru's International Potato Center, told the outlet that an amount of seed small enough to fit inside an empty pen — roughly four grams, or 8,000 seeds — was enough to plant half a hectare abroad.

## China's Boom, and the Price Crash That Followed

That smuggled seed found fertile ground in Yunnan province, where Chinese cultivation began around 2012 at a comparable altitude. By 2014, China was already cultivating roughly 12,000 hectares of maca to Peru's 5,000, according to the same Mongabay Latam investigation, with projections at the time putting Chinese acreage above 13,000 hectares within a few years. Analysts identified some 1,700 maca-related patent filings worldwide, roughly three-quarters of them Chinese.

The transition was not gradual. Between 2013 and 2015, Chinese buyers arrived in the Junín highlands offering $100 to $200 per kilogram in cash for raw maca — against a baseline of roughly $3 — with no receipts and no paper trail. Farmers who had grown other crops switched to maca, took on debt to expand plots, and watched informal economies of trucks and businesses spring up around the boom town of Huayre. It lasted about three years. By 2015, Chinese purchases from Peru had collapsed to essentially zero as domestic Chinese production displaced Peruvian maca in export markets across Europe and the United States, and prices in Junín fell back below pre-boom levels, leaving many producers in debt with unsold inventory. In June 2019, the FDA seized a shipment of a Chinese-branded "Maca Perú" product adulterated with sildenafil, the active compound in Viagra — a measure of how far the "Andean viagra" myth had traveled from both the plant's traditional use and its actual pharmacology.

Peru has tried to build institutional defenses since. In 2011, Indecopi granted maca from the Junín-Pasco region a formal denomination of origin — only the country's eighth — tying legal recognition of the name "maca" to a defined territory and to Andean cultivation practices. Indecopi has also issued free collective trademarks to smallholder associations in the region, giving small producers a shared brand to compete against undifferentiated imports.

## What the Science Actually Supports

None of this history answers the separate question of what maca actually does, and the two should not be conflated. Its active compounds — alkaloids and fatty-acid derivatives called macamides and macaenes, along with glucosinolates — have been studied for decades without full agreement on their mechanisms, according to the ethnopharmacological review cited above.

The most-studied claim, effects on male fertility, has produced mixed and modest results. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis published in *Frontiers in Pharmacology* found that, across five qualifying randomized controlled trials, maca showed suggestive but inconclusive effects on sperm concentration, with some trials showing improvement and others none, and no consistent effect on motility or morphology. A more recent evidence review found the plant appears reasonably safe at studied doses, but flagged that most fertility-specific trials remain small, short, and methodologically weak. Claims about memory, mood, stress resistance, or menopausal symptoms rest on a similarly thin evidence base, with promising animal data unmatched by large human trials. None of this shows that maca reliably treats infertility or sexual dysfunction — only that some biological plausibility exists alongside a marketing narrative that has consistently outrun the data.

## A Treaty Two Decades in the Making

The institutional response to cases like maca's culminated this year. Peru's Congress approved Legislative Resolution 32591 in May 2026, ratifying the Treaty on Intellectual Property, Genetic Resources and Associated Traditional Knowledge, adopted by WIPO member states in Geneva in May 2024. In July, at WIPO's General Assembly, Peru formally joined the treaty alongside Albania, Malawi, and Uganda — becoming the first country in Latin America and the Caribbean to do so. The treaty is the first binding international instrument requiring patent applicants to disclose the country of origin of genetic resources and any traditional knowledge used in an invention, a mechanism designed specifically to make future MacaPure-style patents harder to obtain without scrutiny.

Indecopi's biopiracy commission reported in May 2026 that it had prevented 180 biopiracy-related patent applications worldwide over the preceding two decades, with maca, sacha inchi, and sangre de grado identified as Peru's most frequently targeted biological resources, alongside cacao, camu camu, and several other native crops. The commission logged 67 new suspicious patent applications in 2025 alone — a declining but still active trend, according to Indecopi's own figures.

What the treaty and the denomination of origin cannot do is undo what already happened: the seed that left Junín two decades ago, the export markets Peru lost to Yunnan, or the years during which farmers in Huayre absorbed debt while a myth about their crop circulated the world in translation. The mechanisms now exist to make the next version of that story harder to repeat. Whether they arrive fast enough, and reach the smallholders furthest from Lima's institutions, is the part still being written.
