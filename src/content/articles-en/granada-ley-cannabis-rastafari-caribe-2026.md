While Mexico's Supreme Court spends its sessions parsing the paperwork for cannabis self-consumption permits, Brazil's health regulator negotiates the fine print of a five-year regulatory sandbox for patient associations, and Colombia's Congress fails, for the ninth time, to pass a recreational cannabis bill, a Caribbean country of roughly 120,000 people quietly did something the region's largest cannabis economies have not managed in years: it passed a cannabis law, on a bipartisan vote, in a single legislative season, without a court order forcing its hand.

Grenada's Drug Abuse (Prevention and Control) (Amendment) Act, 2026, tabled in Parliament on January 20 and assented to by Governor-General Dame Cecile La Grenade on February 20, decriminalizes small-scale personal cannabis possession and cultivation. It is not, its architects insist, a recreational-legalization law. But it does something few cannabis statutes in the hemisphere have bothered to do explicitly: it writes the cultivation and sacramental-use rights of the Rastafari faith directly into the text, alongside a formal promise — not yet delivered — of a regulated medicinal cannabis industry. For a magazine that tracks patient access across a region where courts, not legislatures, usually do the heavy lifting, Grenada's law is a useful, and partial, counter-example.

## What Phase One actually changes

The Act creates what officials are calling "Phase One" of a two-stage reform. Under it, adults 21 and older may possess up to 56 grams of cannabis flower or 15 grams of resin without facing criminal charges, and households may register with the Cannabis Legalization and Regulation Secretariat and the Ministry of Agriculture to cultivate up to four plants for private use, according to reporting by the Trinidad and Tobago Guardian and the International Cannabis Business Conference's trade newsletter. The law also creates an amnesty and expungement pathway for past convictions tied to those same small quantities, and it discontinues pending prosecutions that fall under the new thresholds.

Health Minister Phillip Telesford was explicit about the law's limits when the bill passed: "recreational use as per this Bill is not permitted," he told Parliament, according to the Guardian's account of the session — a reminder that decriminalizing possession is not the same as legalizing a commercial recreational market, a distinction that has often blurred in international coverage of the reform. Public consumption of cannabis remains an offense carrying a fixed EC$300 penalty.

The debate inside Parliament also had a generational hinge. Prime Minister Dickon Mitchell had initially pushed to set the legal age at 18, arguing "if you are an adult you are an adult, 18 is 18," but he ultimately accepted 21 after, in his words, deferring "graciously to the medical experts... who recognise that at 18 a brain is still developing." Attorney General Claudette Joseph framed the reform bluntly as overdue: "Grenada is one of the last jurisdictions to now move in the direction of the decriminalisation of cannabis," she said — a nod to the fact that Jamaica, Antigua and Barbuda, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, Barbados and Trinidad and Tobago had all decriminalized cannabis in some form years earlier.

## Writing faith into the statute book

The clause that sets Grenada's law apart from most of Latin America's cannabis statutes is its treatment of the Rastafari community. Cannabis arrived in Grenada with indentured Indian laborers in the 19th century, but it was Rastafarian growers who, by the late 20th century, had made ganja cultivation a marker of religious and cultural identity on the island — one that state authorities periodically suppressed, including a crackdown on unregistered growers in 1980, the year after Rastafarian cultivators had backed the island's revolutionary government.

The new law affirms the constitutional right of Rastafari adherents to use cannabis as a sacrament in registered places of worship and at approved "exempt events," and — unlike the household limit that applies to everyone else — it imposes no cultivation cap on registered Rastafari institutions. "We have not set any limits for the Rastafari with regard to their cultivation and their use," Nandy Noel, CEO of the Cannabis Legalization and Regulation Secretariat, said in comments reported around the bill's passage. "Again, our intent is not to control but safeguard the Rastafari community and ensure they can practice their sacrament securely and without hindrance."

That framing — cannabis policy as an instrument of religious-freedom protection, not just public-health or economic policy — is largely absent from the region's mainland debates, where Indigenous and Afro-descendant communities' traditional relationships to cannabis and other plants rarely receive equivalent, explicit statutory recognition. It is worth naming as a genuine, if narrow, human-rights advance, without romanticizing it: the law protects registered institutions and approved events, not open cultivation by any believer, and the enforcement details of that registration process have not yet been published.

## The unfinished promise: a medicinal framework still to come

For a publication concerned with patient access, the more consequential half of Grenada's reform has not happened yet. Officials described Phase One's decriminalization as a first step toward what they call Phase Two: a "comprehensive national cannabis policy framework and supporting legislation covering cultivation, processing, research, and medicinal use," expected within three to six months of the law's passage — a window that, measured from the February assent, runs out around August 2026. No licensing timeline for medicinal dispensaries, cultivation businesses, or a patient registry has been made public, and the current Act itself allows only narrowly "approved" religious, medicinal and scientific use pending that fuller framework.

Grenada's neighbors offer a cautionary note about how long "soon" can stretch. Trinidad and Tobago's Cannabis Control Act received presidential assent in 2022, after its House had unanimously passed the underlying bill establishing a Cannabis Licensing Authority for medical, therapeutic and scientific cultivation — yet, according to Trinidad and Tobago's own Parliament publication record and regional trade coverage, it remains formally unproclaimed, meaning the medical framework it created still has no legal effect four years later. It is the same pattern documented across much of Latin America, where patients wait not for a vote but for the regulation that is supposed to follow one: reporting this April by the Peruvian outlet Sativa Info found that, under laws dating to 2017 and 2021, roughly two of every three of Peru's more than 55,000 registered medicinal-cannabis patients still cannot obtain product through the formal system, according to that country's drug regulator, Digemid.

Grenada, in other words, has just done the easier half of cannabis reform — decriminalizing personal use and, notably, protecting a religious minority's relationship to the plant — faster and with less rancor than most of its larger neighbors managed. Whether it does the harder half, building an actual regulated pathway for patients who need cannabis as medicine rather than sacrament, will depend on whether its self-imposed six-month clock proves more binding than similar promises have elsewhere in the hemisphere.
