In a dry, rocky ravine in the Chicoana department of Salta province, Argentina, at roughly 1,850 meters above sea level, grows a flower that did not even have its own name until 2022. Botanists had long confused it with a related species until a taxonomic study split it off as new: *Hippeastrum escoipense*, a wild amaryllis known locally as amancay, recorded only in that ravine and a handful of nearby populations. Now the same plant has turned up somewhere unexpected: a computational study published in late June in the journal *Molecular Diversity*, examining whether one of its alkaloids could become an ally against Chagas disease, an illness that continues to kill quietly, by the thousands, across Latin America every year.

## A neglected disease in need of options

Chagas disease, caused by the parasite *Trypanosoma cruzi* and spread mainly by the triatomine bug (vinchuca), affects an estimated eight million people worldwide according to figures cited by Infobae, the vast majority in Latin America, and causes more than ten thousand deaths a year. In Argentina, official estimates and figures from organizations such as the Mundo Sano Foundation put the number of infected people between 1.5 and 1.6 million, with roughly seven million more living in at-risk areas. There is no vaccine. The only two drugs on the market, benznidazole and nifurtimox, are more than fifty years old, have limited efficacy in the chronic phase of the disease — the stage most patients are in by the time they're diagnosed — and cause side effects severe enough that many patients abandon treatment. "The medications currently administered on the global market are extremely toxic for patients, especially during the chronic stage of this disease," explained biochemist Gabriela Egly Feresin, a researcher with CONICET and the National University of San Juan who leads the research line, as quoted by Infobae and Canal 13 San Juan. That therapeutic gap is what drives the search for new molecules in under-explored sources, such as the native flora of Andean ravines.

## A species that exists in only one place

The taxonomic work documenting *Hippeastrum escoipense* as a cryptic species — a distinct species that had gone unnoticed because of its resemblance to another, in this case *H. argentinum* — was published in the journal *Darwiniana* in 2022. The paper, available through SciELO Argentina and Redalyc, describes a plant with erect leaves and a three-lobed stigma that flowers in mid-December and fruits in late January, native to steep grasslands and dry ravines of the Prepuna district. According to that same study, only small populations are known from the type locality, which led the authors to suggest, under IUCN criteria, a vulnerable conservation status. It's worth stating plainly: unlike other plants covered in this section, there is no documented tradition of Indigenous or campesino medicinal use tied to this particular species — it was a botanical unknown until just four years ago. What does exist is nearly fifteen years of scientific bioprospecting on the genus Hippeastrum and its alkaloids, which is what led researchers to this specific plant.

## From the test tube to the molecular simulation

The compound's story predates this year's study. In 2023, the same research group, together with colleagues from the University of Barcelona and the University of Costa Rica, published in the journal *Fitoterapia* that candimine — an alkaloid typical of the Amaryllidaceae family — killed different life-cycle stages of the *T. cruzi* parasite in laboratory assays, and that combining it with benznidazole produced a synergistic effect: the drug became more effective at lower doses, which, in theory, could translate into less toxicity for a future patient. That finding — in cell cultures, not whole organisms — was the starting point.

The study published on June 28 this year, also coordinated by Feresin with researchers from Costa Rica and Spain under the RENATEC network of the Ibero-American CYTED program, took a different step forward: it used computational molecular modeling to propose a mechanism of action. According to the published results, candimine binds stably to an enzyme called squalene synthase (TcSQS), essential for the parasite to build its cell membrane, and that binding held steady across 400-nanosecond molecular dynamics simulations. Computational toxicity analysis placed the compound in an intermediate risk category, with no red flags for liver, neurological, cardiac, cytotoxic, or mutagenic damage, and estimated good gastrointestinal absorption — a relevant detail for any eventual oral drug.

## What still isn't known

This is where the enthusiasm needs a check. What was published in June is, strictly speaking, a computer-generated prediction: a model suggesting how candimine might work, not a confirmation in a living organism. Feresin herself flagged this when asked by Infobae: computational simulations alone cannot establish a real mechanism of action, and experimental confirmation is needed before human trials can even be considered. The team now plans to move toward animal models, testing both the acute and chronic phases of infection — a step that, in neglected-disease drug research, often takes years and does not always succeed: most candidates that work in a test tube or a simulation never become treatments. After that, if results are encouraging, only then would clinical trials for efficacy, toxicity, and safety be warranted, a regulatory process that can stretch on for another decade.

There is also a tension rarely mentioned in news coverage of findings like this: the source of the molecule is a wild plant with small populations, categorized as vulnerable. Should candimine advance toward pharmaceutical development, the question of how the compound would be sourced at scale — laboratory synthesis, controlled cultivation, or wild harvesting — and who would benefit from any resulting patent is not a minor detail for a species that today survives in a handful of ravines in northwestern Argentina.

## Regional science, no shortcuts

Whatever the eventual outcome, this case illustrates something particular to Latin American pharmacological research into neglected diseases: it survives on regional cooperation networks — in this case, Argentina, Costa Rica, and Spain, linked through the CYTED program — precisely because these diseases, which mostly affect impoverished populations in the region, generate little commercial interest for the global pharmaceutical industry. Candimine does not cure anything yet. For now, it is a solid hypothesis, built on years of fieldwork, chemistry, and modeling, about a flower that science has only just begun to know.
