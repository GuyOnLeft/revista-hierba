---
title: "Mexico Allows Shared Cannabis Permits, But the Path Still Runs Through the Courts"
excerpt: "Mexico's Supreme Court ruled that one sanitary authorization can cover several people cultivating cannabis together. COFEPRIS complied, but the data show a system that still denies nearly everything administratively, forcing people into court."
tag: "Justice"
---

Nine strangers who sued the Mexican state for the right to grow cannabis together have just reshaped, in a small but concrete way, what it means to hold a legal cannabis permit in the country — at a moment when Mexico's Congress has spent eight years failing to write the law that would make courtroom-by-courtroom permits unnecessary in the first place. On June 25, 2026, Mexico's Supreme Court (SCJN) ruled that a single sanitary authorization can validly cover several people at once for recreational cannabis self-consumption, as long as each is explicitly named as a permit holder. Mexico's health regulator, COFEPRIS, announced on July 7 that it would comply. For a country whose cannabis policy has been built almost entirely through individual lawsuits rather than legislation, the ruling is a rare acknowledgment that the plant can be grown, and used, collectively — even if the rest of the system around it remains stubbornly individualized, discretionary, and, according to critics, quietly hostile to the people trying to use it.

## A Technicality With Outsized Consequences

The case traces back to a group of nine adults who, years ago, jointly petitioned COFEPRIS for authorization to plant, cultivate, harvest, prepare, possess, and transport cannabis for personal, non-commercial use. COFEPRIS initially rejected the request, arguing Mexico's General Health Law did not contemplate such permits. That denial became untenable after the Court's 2021 Declaratoria General de Inconstitucionalidad 1/2018, which struck down the statutory provisions banning recreational self-consumption outright and forced the agency to start issuing permits under judicial order. Acting on that mandate, COFEPRIS issued the nine applicants a single joint document in 2022, naming all of them as holders.

The group challenged that outcome, arguing that sharing one document diminished their individually won rights and that each of them was entitled to a separate authorization. In its ruling on the Recurso de Inconformidad, the Court's full bench disagreed. What matters, the justices held, is not the number of documents COFEPRIS issues but that every beneficiary is expressly and individually recognized within it, with a certified copy in hand. A joint authorization, the Court found, "did not limit or reduce" the rights already recognized to each person. COFEPRIS confirmed on July 7 that it would now process and recognize such joint authorizations going forward, covering the same range of activities — planting, cultivation, harvest, preparation, possession, and transport — for groups of adults applying together.

The underlying right traces back further still, to November 2015, when the Court's First Chamber granted the first amparo of this kind to four members of a collective called SMART (Sociedad Mexicana de Autoconsumo Responsable y Tolerante), recognizing that adult cannabis use falls under the constitutional "free development of personality." That ruling, repeated in four more individual cases over the following years, eventually triggered the mechanism under Mexican law that converts a consistent line of amparo victories into a binding general declaration — the 2021 Declaratoria General de Inconstitucionalidad 1/2018 — which is what forced COFEPRIS to start issuing permits at all, rather than treating each case as an isolated judicial favor. The nine-person case decided this June is, in that sense, a direct descendant of a legal architecture that was never designed by lawmakers, only assembled, case by case, by litigants and judges over more than a decade.

## The Permit System Mexico Actually Has

To understand why this technical point matters, it helps to look at how Mexico's personal-use authorization system has actually functioned since it opened in 2019. According to a peer-reviewed evaluation published in a Mexican academic journal, COFEPRIS received 19,230 applications between April 2019 and March 2024 and issued 7,019 authorizations — an overall acceptance rate of 36.5%, averaging about 117 permits a month. But that modest success rate obscures a sharper pattern: at the purely administrative stage, COFEPRIS has denied close to 100% of requests. Nearly every permit that has ever been granted followed a second, judicial phase, in which applicants filed an amparo or a complaint over non-compliance with the 2021 declaration and a federal judge ordered the agency to comply. Acceptance rates did trend upward over the five years studied — from 12.9% in the 2019–2020 cycle to 50% in 2023–2024 — but the underlying mechanism did not change: courts, not the regulator, are what actually deliver the permit. The same research found stark geographic disparities, with applicants in Querétaro filing in the thousands while states like Chiapas and Jalisco registered essentially none, a gap that tracks closely with where legal aid and cannabis-rights organizations are active.

## COFEPRIS Complies, With a Hard Limit Attached

In its July 7 statement, COFEPRIS framed its compliance narrowly. Joint authorizations, the agency said, "fully safeguard" the fundamental rights of each named beneficiary for personal cultivation and consumption. But it was equally explicit about the boundary: the ruling, COFEPRIS said, "does not open commercialization nor generally decriminalize cannabis" in Mexico. Sale, distribution, and supply remain criminal conduct. Use around minors, in unauthorized public spaces, or while driving or operating machinery remains prohibited. In other words, a group of adults can now, in principle, be recognized on paper as co-cultivators of a shared plot — a modest opening toward the kind of collective growing that cannabis clubs in other parts of Latin America already do formally — but nothing in the ruling creates a legal channel for anyone to sell what they grow, or for a would-be club to operate as an organization rather than a list of named individuals sharing one piece of paper.

## "Systematic and Unjustified" Denials, a Rights Group Says

The reaction from Mexico's cannabis-rights movement was less celebratory than wary. México Unido Contra la Delincuencia (MUCD), one of the organizations that has driven cannabis litigation in Mexico for over a decade, issued a statement the same day COFEPRIS confirmed compliance, arguing that the agency has for years "systematically and unjustifiably" denied both the initiation of the permit process and the permits themselves to large numbers of applicants — a pattern the acceptance-rate data appears to bear out. MUCD called on COFEPRIS and the federal government to issue clear, public guidelines that would make the permit process predictable rather than something that functionally requires a lawyer and a judge to complete, and urged that any future cannabis regulation be built on a public-health and rights framework — one that addresses use among minors through education and harm reduction rather than blanket prohibition, the group said, since prohibition itself has failed to reduce use while feeding illegal markets tied to violence in Mexican territory.

## Congress, Still Missing From Its Own Story

What the ruling does not do is resolve the deeper problem both the Court and civil-society groups keep naming: Mexico still has no general law regulating cannabis, recreational or otherwise. The Supreme Court first ordered Congress to legislate on the matter in 2021, when it issued the declaration that opened the permit system in the first place. Five years, several further rulings, and thousands of individually litigated permits later, lawmakers have not passed one. In that vacuum, the country's cannabis policy keeps being written — piecemeal, permit by permit, ruling by ruling — by the judiciary and by the individuals with the resources and persistence to sue for a right the Constitutional Court has already recognized. The joint-permit ruling makes that system marginally more workable for people organizing collectively. It does not make it a policy.
