For nearly five years, a multiple sclerosis patient in Panama City named Carlos Ossa did what thousands of patients across Latin America have had to do when the law promises medicine and the state fails to deliver it: he went looking for cannabis oil wherever he could find it, with no way to know what was actually in the bottle. Panama had legalized medical cannabis back in October 2021, becoming the first country in Central America to do so. But the law and the pharmacy shelf remained, for years, two entirely separate things. On July 9, 2026, that finally began to change — though not in the way patient advocates had spent half a decade fighting for.

Panama's Ministry of Health confirmed that Tilray Medical, through a joint venture with the Panamanian company Solana Life Group, had launched the first commercial medical cannabis product to reach the country's pharmacies under Law 242: an oral CBD solution distributed through Farmacias Arrocha, one of the largest pharmacy chains in the country. It is a milestone, and an uncomfortable one. The product was manufactured in Portugal, at a facility certified under European standards, and imported into a country where seven Panamanian companies had been waiting years for the same kind of license.

## Two Articles That Contradicted Each Other

To understand why it took nearly five years to get a single bottle onto a shelf, you have to look at a bureaucratic knot that Panama's own health officials openly admitted they had tied themselves.

Law 242 of 2021 authorized seven companies — among them Consorcio Panamericana de Cannabis, Consorcio Green Med, Farma Verde Corp., Canna Med Panamá, Consorcio Adeiwa Pharma and Consorcio Ecovida — to import, cultivate, produce and sell cannabis-derived medicine. Article 24 allowed those companies to temporarily import finished products during their first years, while they built up local production capacity. But Executive Decree 121, published in September 2022 to regulate the law, imposed a different condition in Article 109: companies had to comply with local manufacturing standards to get licensed at all. For a company that did not yet have a local factory — every one of them, by definition — that requirement was impossible to meet. Uriel Pérez, the ministry's national director of pharmacy and drugs, described the result bluntly to La Prensa in 2025: "It's a matter of contradictions in the requirements." No licenses were issued. Patients with epilepsy, cancer, multiple sclerosis, chronic pain and dozens of other conditions the law was written to cover had, on paper, a right to medicine. In practice, they had nothing.

It took an executive decree in April 2025 — Decree No. 6 — to start untangling the knot, by relaxing the local-manufacturing requirement for importers and giving physicians discretion to prescribe beyond a closed list of conditions. By November 2025, Pérez could report incremental progress: three licenses for handling controlled substances and five basic operating licenses, out of the seven companies originally authorized. A reform bill from Deputy Marcos Castillero of the Democratic Revolutionary Party, still pending before the National Assembly, seeks to formalize a five-year import window and add patient protections, including a corporate social-responsibility requirement obligating licensed companies to donate $50,000 to $100,000 a year, or provide free and reduced-cost medicine to low-income patients.

## When the Only Option Is the Black Market

The years of regulatory paralysis were not an abstract policy failure. They had a direct, physical cost for patients who could not simply wait for the government to sort out its own contradictions.

"We increasingly see more people and businesses taking advantage of patients' vulnerability and pain," Ossa told La Prensa in October 2025, describing an informal market of unregulated cannabis products sold to desperate patients with no way to verify content or dosage. Because nothing outside the narrow channel authorized by Law 242 is legal in Panama, any patient who bought oil from that informal market — often the only oil actually available — was, technically, committing an offense under the country's penal code. The law meant to protect patients from criminalization for using cannabis as medicine left, during its five-year implementation gap, exactly the opposite: chronically ill people forced to choose between going without treatment or breaking the law to get it, with no guarantee the product even worked.

This is the pattern that recurs across Latin America whenever a country passes a medical cannabis law without building the machinery to make it real — associations and patients in Brazil relied on individual habeas corpus petitions for a decade before regulators caught up; families elsewhere absorb similar gaps by paying black-market or import prices far beyond what a public health system could offer. Panama's version of that story is now, on paper, moving toward resolution — but the resolution that arrived first did not come from the patient movement that pushed for it.

## A Bottle From Portugal, Not a Panamanian Greenhouse

The Ministry of Health had spent 2026 building the administrative scaffolding for a functioning program. In May, a resolution approved the official patient registration form and medical certification format. In June, the ministry activated SIUCMAA, the Sistema para la Identificación de Usuarios de Cannabis Medicinal y Acompañantes Autorizados — the digital registry patients and their authorized caregivers must use to access treatment. Yelkis Gill, the ministry's director general of public health, told local media that "there are no limitations to access, except the medical indication," meaning a physician's judgment, not red tape, was now supposed to be the only gatekeeper. By June, roughly 400 patients had registered.

Then, in July, Tilray Medical's launch became the proof that the system could, in fact, put a product in a patient's hands — but it did so through a multinational's supply chain rather than through the domestic cannabis economy that Law 242 had originally been designed to build. "At Tilray Medical, we believe every patient deserves access to safe, consistent, pharmaceutical-grade medical cannabis products," said Rajnish Ohri, the company's president for international markets, in announcing the launch. The company's oral solution reached Panamanian pharmacy shelves faster than any of the Panamanian consortiums that had been waiting since 2021, because it came from an already-operating, EU-certified factory abroad rather than from a Panamanian cultivation and production facility still working to meet local licensing requirements.

## What Still Isn't Fixed

Panama's five-year gap between legalizing medical cannabis and delivering it is now, unevenly, closing. But the shape of that closing tells its own story about who a fragmented regulatory process ultimately serves. The seven Panamanian companies named in the original law are still, for the most part, not fully licensed. The legislative reform that would lock in clearer rules and require companies to subsidize treatment for low-income patients has not yet passed the National Assembly. Pricing for the first authorized product has not been disclosed. And for the patients who spent years buying unregulated oil because there was no legal alternative, the risk of exposure to unreliable products — and, technically, to the penal code — did not disappear the day a press release announced a launch.

What changed is that Panama now has, for the first time, at least one verifiable channel between a patient's prescription and a bottle on a pharmacy shelf. Whether that channel widens to include the local cooperatives, small producers and lower-cost options patients actually need — rather than remaining a route open mainly to a company with a factory in Portugal — will depend on decisions still pending in Panama's Ministry of Health and its National Assembly.
