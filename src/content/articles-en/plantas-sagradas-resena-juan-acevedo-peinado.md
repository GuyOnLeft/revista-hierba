Some books arrive at exactly the moment you need them. *Plantas sagradas: el linaje secreto del chamanismo sudamericano* (*Sacred Plants: The Secret Lineage of South American Shamanism*), by Juan Acevedo Peinado, was one of those for me. From the cover alone — that red evoking the blood of the earth, set against the white of an imagery that recalls the Aguada culture — the book already hints at the deep wisdom it holds in its pages. It is no accident that I feel it this way: I am writing this review very close to those lands, and I have taken university courses in American Archaeology.

Juan Acevedo Peinado was born in Argentina and trained as a psychologist at the University of Rosario. But what makes this work valuable is not so much his academic training as his lineage: the recognition of the ancestral knowledge that grandfathers and grandmothers pass on to those who dare to reconnect with it. From that place, Juan builds a narrative that weaves psychology, anthropology and American archaeology together with a deep personal experience of plants and their imagery.

In *Plantas sagradas* we find stories the author lived firsthand, told with the ease of someone who recognizes the magic in the moments and the beings around him. The medicine wheel, the sacred plants, the encounters in the salamancas, the witchcraft and the healers appear here not as distant folklore, but as experiences still alive today in communities across Mother Earth.

Juan recorded these experiences with methodical rigor, understanding that the here and now holds all the time travelled by those who came before us. They are experiences lived over the course of his life, in different places and alongside different Indigenous peoples, and they speak to a knowledge so carefully kept that it is rarely passed on, even among peers.

The book also takes us along the path to the otorongo's door, and introduces its guardians — the great otorongo, the jaguar that illustrates the cover. Moving through the four cardinal points of the medicine wheel, we travel with Juan through landscapes, characters and situations that are truly magical.

There is something I want to pause and think through with you, because it is the very heart of the book: the tension between keeping and sharing. Juan calls it "the secret lineage," and in doing so describes precisely the way this knowledge survived — in a low voice, by word of mouth, carried for centuries above all by women, grandmothers, mamanchis, who decided whom to pass it on to and when. That the author was given leave to tell it is no small detail. It is, in itself, part of this story: someone had to trust him for this book to exist, and that trust runs through every page with a care you can feel in the way it is told — never exhibitionist, always respectful of the mystery that is neither explained nor questioned.

I am also interested in the structure he chose. Organizing the book around the four cardinal points of the medicine wheel is not an aesthetic device; it is a way of thinking. Each direction brings its own color, its own animal, its own teaching, and Juan does not force his experiences into that scheme — he lets them breathe where they fit. That sets it apart from other books in the genre that list plants and rituals like a catalogue. Here the order is ceremonial, not expository.

I cannot leave out Juan's own lineage as a descendant of Mbyá Guaraní payés, because it explains why the book does not sound like an anthropologist observing from the outside, or a traveller back from the jungle with anecdotes. His training in medicine, psychology and anthropology — by way of Rosario and La Plata — gives him the tools to put into words what he lived, but what sustains the narrative is something else: his identity and ethnic belonging. You can feel the difference between someone who describes a ceremony and someone raised, even if only in part, within that worldview. That double position, inside and outside at the same time, is what makes these more than three hundred pages so readable without losing depth.

That is also why I understand the comparisons that have been drawn between this book and those of Carlos Castaneda: both recount a firsthand apprenticeship alongside teachers who hold ancestral knowledge. But while Castaneda writes from the gaze of someone arriving from outside into a foreign world, Juan writes from within a lineage of his own, and that difference makes this book uniquely valuable for anyone who wants to learn about the archaeological and ethnic cultures of our hemisphere.

Presenting this work is, for me, a source of pride: presenting a brother from Abya Yala sharing *The Secret Lineage of South American Shamanism*.

I share with him this unsettling but welcome need to deepen our relationship with plants and with ancestral knowledge.
