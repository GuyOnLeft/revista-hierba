---
title: "CBN and THCV Outperform CBD in Reducing Alcohol Consumption in New Rat Study"
excerpt: "A Vilnius University study in rats found that cannabinol and THCV curb alcohol drinking with fewer side effects than CBD — a finding relevant to Latin America, home to the region's heaviest per capita alcohol consumption."
tag: "Pharmacology"
---

Argentina drinks more alcohol per capita than any other country in Latin America — roughly 8 liters of pure alcohol a year, according to the World Health Organization's most recent figures — with Chile close behind. Across the region, alcohol use disorder (AUD) is one of the most common and least treated psychiatric conditions: pharmacological options are limited to a handful of drugs, most patients never receive them, and the one class of medication that specifically targeted the brain's cannabinoid receptors was pulled from the market worldwide after triggering severe psychiatric side effects. Into that gap comes a new animal study, published in April 2026 in the journal *Alcohol and Alcoholism*, that revisits the same biological target with a different set of molecules — and finds two of them working better, and more safely, than the cannabinoid most people already know.

## A cautionary history with the same target

The endocannabinoid system — the network of receptors, most notably CB1, that respond to compounds the body produces naturally as well as to plant-derived cannabinoids — has long been implicated in the reward circuitry that drives alcohol and drug consumption. In the mid-2000s, that link generated real pharmaceutical interest: rimonabant, a synthetic drug that blocked CB1 receptors, showed promise for weight loss and addiction control. It was approved in Europe in 2006 and then withdrawn worldwide by 2008, after clinical trials linked it to depression, anxiety, and in some cases suicidal ideation. The lesson researchers drew from that failure wasn't that the endocannabinoid system was the wrong target — it was that blunt, total blockade of CB1 receptors was too blunt an instrument.

That is the backdrop for the new study, led by researcher Ieva Poceviciute together with Martynas Arbaciauskas, Rokas Buisas, Osvaldas Ruksenas and Valentina Vengeliene at Vilnius University in Lithuania. Instead of testing another synthetic CB1 blocker, the team asked a narrower question: could naturally occurring, non-psychoactive phytocannabinoids — compounds that modulate the same receptor system without rimonabant's blunt mechanism — reduce alcohol consumption without the same psychiatric cost?

## Testing three cannabinoids in long-term drinking rats

The researchers worked with male Wistar rats given months of uninterrupted access to alcohol, building the kind of established, voluntary drinking pattern that better approximates chronic use than short-term exposure models. Once that pattern was set — roughly six months in — the animals received one of three phytocannabinoids for three consecutive days: cannabinol (CBN), tetrahydrocannabivarin (THCV), or cannabidiol (CBD), the best known of the three. CBN was dosed at 5 and 10 mg/kg, THCV at 5 and 20 mg/kg, and CBD at 20 and 40 mg/kg, all delivered by injection. In total the alcohol-drinking cohort involved 51 male rats; a separate group of 56 alcohol-naive rats (32 male, 24 female) was used to record ultrasonic vocalizations, a standard proxy for emotional distress in rodents, to check whether the compounds caused suffering rather than mere disinterest in drinking. The team also tracked water intake, body weight, and general movement, to distinguish a genuine reduction in alcohol-seeking from simple sedation.

## CBN and THCV outperform CBD

All three compounds reduced how much alcohol the rats drank, but not equally. CBN produced the clearest result: a dose-dependent drop in both alcohol intake and preference, accompanied by an increase in water consumption — a pattern the researchers read as evidence that the animals were specifically drinking less alcohol, not simply drinking or moving less overall. THCV, at its higher dose, produced a comparable effect on alcohol and water intake, though somewhat weaker than CBN's. CBD, by contrast, produced only a modest reduction in alcohol intake and did not change the animals' preference for alcohol over water — while also reducing their general locomotor activity more than the other two compounds, a sign of sedation rather than a targeted effect on drinking behavior. All three compounds caused transient sedation and small reductions in body weight at the higher doses, and the vocalization recordings in the alcohol-naive rats showed no signs of acute distress. "We conclude that CBN and THCV may have potential in treating AUD," the authors wrote.

That CBD came out as the weakest performer is itself a notable finding, given that CBD is by far the most commercially available and widely marketed of the three compounds — it is legal, sold over the counter in much of the world, and the subject of far more consumer-facing health claims. CBN and THCV remain comparatively obscure: CBN is best known, when known at all, for unproven claims about sleep, and THCV mostly for early-stage research into appetite suppression and glucose metabolism. Neither is currently formulated or marketed as an addiction treatment anywhere.

## What this study does not show

The limitations are substantial, and the authors were explicit about them. This is a rat study, not a human trial — the translation from rodent reward circuitry to human alcohol use disorder, a condition shaped as much by social context, psychiatric comorbidity, and access to care as by neurochemistry, is neither automatic nor guaranteed. The study used only male rats for the core alcohol-consumption experiments, so whether the same effects would hold in females is unknown; the authors flagged this explicitly as a gap requiring further study, given that sex differences are well documented in both alcohol use disorder and cannabinoid pharmacology. The comparison across compounds is also imperfect on its own terms: because of how the experiment was staged, the CBD-treated animals had roughly four months more alcohol exposure than the CBN-treated group before receiving their assigned compound, a difference that could have affected how each group responded independently of the drug itself. And the vocalization recordings used to assess emotional distress were carried out in alcohol-naive animals, not in the chronically drinking rats that actually received the compounds — a proxy, not a direct measurement, of how the treated animals felt.

Nor does the study establish a mechanism with precision. The researchers note that CBN behaves as a partial CB1 receptor agonist and THCV as a neutral CB1 antagonist — different pharmacological actions at the same receptor, both apparently sufficient to curb drinking without rimonabant's severe blockade-driven side effects — but the study was not designed to fully map why that distinction in receptor behavior produces safer outcomes. The authors themselves call for larger, longer studies and suggest that combination treatments, pairing compounds with different mechanisms, could be worth testing given the possibility of synergistic effects.

## Why it matters for a region that drinks heavily

None of this justifies buying CBN or THCV products to treat alcohol use disorder — the compounds tested here are not approved, regulated, or dosed for that purpose in any country, and the study was conducted in animals. What it does offer is a genuine research lead in a therapeutic area starved of them. The two approved medications most commonly recommended for moderate-to-severe AUD, naltrexone and acamprosate, remain underprescribed even where available, and access to any pharmacological treatment for alcohol use disorder is inconsistent across Latin America, where alcohol is woven into social life and treatment infrastructure is unevenly distributed. A safer class of drugs acting on the same receptor system that failed so visibly with rimonabant would be a meaningful addition to a very short list — if, and only if, these preclinical results hold up in the years of further research the authors say are still needed.
