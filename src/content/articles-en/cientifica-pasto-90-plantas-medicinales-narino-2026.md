In the highlands of Nariño, on Colombia's border between the Pacific and the Amazon, a garden tended by grandmothers has become the unlikely archive for a scientific thesis. Nohora Alejandra Quiguantar, a biologist from the Pasto indigenous people, spent her undergraduate years documenting 90 medicinal plant species used in the Mallamués reservation — not in a laboratory, but around a tulpa, the communal hearth where mothers, midwives, and elders gather to talk. The project, reported in June by Mongabay Latam, is a rare case of an academic thesis built entirely on community methodology, and it offers Latin America's plant-medicine debate something it often lacks: a firsthand account of what gets lost when ethnobotanical knowledge is filtered through institutions that were not built to hold it.

The story behind the research is inseparable from violence. On February 17, 2002, Quiguantar's grandmother, María Quiguantar — a traditional midwife, healer, and leader of a land-recovery movement against colonists and large landowners in Nariño — was tortured and murdered. The case remains unsolved, and the killing displaced Quiguantar's family. "I wanted to seek justice for what happened to my grandmother," she told Mongabay Latam. "To do that, I had to access information, and that information was in school." That search for justice, more than any early interest in botany, is what put her on a path toward biology at the Universidad del Quindío, where she became the first person in her maternal line to complete a university degree.

## A Thesis Built Around the Tulpa, Not the Lab Bench

Quiguantar's undergraduate project, titled on medicinal plants used in the Muellamués indigenous reservation, rejected the standard model of a lone researcher extracting data from informants. Instead, she organized mingas — collective work gatherings rooted in Andean indigenous tradition — and built her results through sustained conversation with mothers, women leaders, midwives, and taitas, or male elders. According to Mongabay Latam's account, the process surfaced 90 species that the community sorted into four working categories: plants used for spiritual protection, plants used to cure illness, plants used for natural dyes, and plants used in midwifery and childbirth. Among the specific species she recorded were guante blanco, used as a natural insecticide; jujún, applied for stomach ailments and what healers describe as "expelling cold" from the body; and culantrillo, a fern used to treat menstrual disorders. These are documented traditional uses and testimony from healers, not clinically verified treatments — a distinction Quiguantar's own account makes clear when she describes her role as recording knowledge, not certifying it.

That distinction did not spare her from friction with her professors. Quiguantar's early framing of plants as carriers of memory, protection, and spirituality was, by her account, dismissed by evaluators as incompatible with scientific method, and her results were initially invalidated for lacking rigor by conventional standards. She pushed back rather than abandon the community process, and eventually graduated — a resolution that reads less as a clean vindication of traditional knowledge over Western science than as a genuine, still-unresolved argument about what counts as valid method. Journals in the field, including the Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine, have spent the past decade building exactly this kind of participatory documentation into accepted practice, but the debate over whose evidentiary standards apply — and who gets to set them — remains active on the ground in reservations like Mallamués.

## What the Count of 90 Species Actually Measures

The headline number is not primarily a catalog of pharmacology; it is a measure of what is disappearing. Quiguantar's research found that grandmothers hold the majority of surviving medicinal plant knowledge in her community, with midwives occupying a particularly exposed position at the center of an eroding system. She attributes the threat to three converging pressures: climate change reducing the availability of certain plants, youth migration away from the reservation, and the simple mortality of the elders who carry the knowledge orally rather than in writing. That pattern is not unique to the Pasto people — Mongabay Latam has separately reported that the disappearance of indigenous languages across Latin America is closely tied to the loss of medicinal plant knowledge, since much of that knowledge exists only in language-specific terms with no direct translation. Quiguantar's insistence on documentation, however imperfect by strict academic standards, is a direct response to that clock.

She has also been explicit about a second extractive pattern she wanted her work to avoid: researchers who arrive at a reservation, gather knowledge, publish it elsewhere, and never return results to the community that produced them. "My research work wasn't for the university, but for my people," she said. She has since coordinated workshops reaching more than 200 young people across the Amazon basin on land tenure and forest stewardship, and contributed to a rapid biodiversity assessment of the Amazon published with the Universidad Javeriana, examining 19 case studies of women's roles in managing biodiversity and local bioeconomies.

## A Wider Fight Over Who Counts as a Scientist

Quiguantar's case has since become part of a broader Mongabay Latam feature on six indigenous women scientists across Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador, Mexico, and Peru, published alongside research suggesting that conventional scientific validation methods can erode the legitimacy of indigenous knowledge systems, and UNESCO data showing that only about a third of the world's scientists are women — a gap that widens further for indigenous women specifically. Quiguantar now sits on the Feminist Action Coalition for Climate Justice convened by UN Women and has coordinated workshops at COP30 in Belém, arguing that traditional knowledge needs a route into government decision-making, not just academic recognition. "What astonishes people is that I'm both scientist and indigenous," she has said, framing the real dispute not as whether indigenous peoples can practice science, but whether they are recognized as experts when they do.

Inside her own reservation, the fight is unfinished on other fronts too: leadership positions in Mallamués still require marital status, and no woman has yet led the reservation's governing authority — an inequality Quiguantar has raised publicly, at some cost to her standing within the community. Her account, in that sense, resists a tidy ending. It leaves the epistemological argument about method open, treats the murder of her grandmother as unresolved fact rather than backstory, and presents the 90 documented plants not as proof of efficacy but as a record — assembled under deadline pressure from language loss and displacement — of what a community still knows, and what it stands to lose next.
