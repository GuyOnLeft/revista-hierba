On July 15, Peru's electoral authorities handed the presidential sash credentials to Keiko Fujimori, confirming what a razor-thin runoff had already signaled a month earlier: on July 28, the Fuerza Popular leader will be sworn in as president after edging Roberto Sánchez of Juntos por el Perú by roughly 50.14% to 49.87% of valid votes, according to Peru's National Elections Jury (JNE) and the National Office of Electoral Processes (ONPE). Buried under the headlines about security policy and a "mano dura" agenda that dominated her campaign is a quieter, unresolved file that will land on her desk regardless: a medical cannabis system that, on paper, has existed for nearly a decade but that most of its own registered patients still cannot legally use.

According to the Peruvian outlet Sativa Info, which covers cannabis policy from a health and rights perspective, more than 55,000 patients are listed in the Health Ministry's National Registry of Cannabis Patients and Users (Renpuc) as of a Digemid database update dated March 18, 2026. Only 34% of them — around 18,837 people — have actually been able to buy a cannabis-derived product through a licensed pharmacy. The rest remain formally recognized as patients but functionally locked out of the system meant to serve them.

## Three laws, one bottleneck

Peru's medical cannabis framework was not built in a single stroke. Law 30681, passed in 2017, first recognized the medicinal and therapeutic use of cannabis and its derivatives. Law 31312, approved in 2021, went further, creating the Artisanal Production License with Associated Cultivation (LPA), a mechanism that lets associations formed exclusively by registered patients grow and process their own cannabis. A third law, 32195, passed in 2024, addresses industrial hemp with under 1% THC, though its implementing regulation remains held up by unresolved objections from the Ministry of Agrarian Development.

The supply side of the legal system is thin. Digemid's own figures, as reported by Sativa Info, count just two laboratories currently authorized to manufacture cannabis derivatives for medicinal use, alongside 45 licensed pharmacies (44 active) and 34 additional establishments authorized to import and sell derivative products. Most strikingly, of the associations that were supposed to be able to grow cannabis for their own members under the 2021 law, only one currently holds an active LPA license; a second institution that once had one is now inactive.

The chokepoint, patient advocates and legal analysts agree, is municipal. To obtain the security certificate that Peru's anti-narcotics police (Dirandro) requires before issuing an LPA license, an association first needs a municipal operating permit — and many municipalities, according to reporting by La Mula and legal commentary published on Sativa Info, either don't recognize cannabis cultivation as a compatible land use, don't understand the 2021 law, or simply decline to issue permits out of caution, misinformation or local politics. The Ámbar cannabis patients association has been flagging this specific obstacle since the regulation's public-comment period in 2021–2023. Dirandro loosened the requirement somewhat in January 2025, agreeing to accept a municipality's proof of a filed application rather than a final approval before associations could advance in the licensing process — a partial fix that has not resolved the underlying scarcity of licensed, associated cultivation nationwide.

Compounding the problem, Ámbar has publicly said the Health Ministry has refused to revise what the association calls a "questioned and conservative" regulation, leaving patients to either abandon treatment or turn to unregulated sellers offering products of unverified origin and potency — the opposite of the safety rationale the legal framework was meant to provide.

## What an incoming government has (and hasn't) said

Sativa Info's own review of the 2026 campaign, published by journalist Jorge Paucar Albino in April, found that candidates across the spectrum showed little familiarity with the cannabis laws already on the books and rarely addressed the access crisis directly; where drug policy came up at all, it leaned punitive rather than focused on the thousands of registered patients stuck outside the formal system.

Fuerza Popular's own history with medical cannabis is not a blank slate, but it is inconsistent. In October 2017, four Fuerza Popular congressmen — including Kenji Fujimori, the president-elect's brother — simultaneously introduced competing medicinal cannabis bills days before a floor vote on a measure that had already cleared committee, a move the outlet Wayka.pe characterized at the time as an attempt to seize control of the legislative narrative rather than a substantive policy shift; the same reporting noted at least one Fuerza Popular legislator abruptly reversing a previously hostile stance on patient advocacy groups without explanation. In later legislative sessions, other Fuerza Popular members of Congress's health committee skipped votes on medicinal cannabis measures altogether, according to Wayka.pe. Fuerza Popular's reinforced 2026 government plan, reviewed by this outlet, emphasizes integrating Peru's public and social-security health systems and creating an independent public health oversight body, but does not lay out a specific position on cannabis regulation or the associated-cultivation bottleneck.

That silence matters because the "mano dura" security framing that defined Fujimori's campaign has, in other Latin American contexts, translated into tighter drug enforcement that does not always distinguish between illicit trafficking and patients holding valid medical registrations — a tension Peru is not new to. On July 1, in an unrelated operation, Peruvian and Colombian armed forces seized more than nine tonnes of presumed cannabis sativa in a joint interdiction near the Amazonian border district of Ramón Castilla, Loreto region, according to Infobae — a reminder that enforcement capacity and political attention remain concentrated on trafficking, even as the formal patient-access system stays chronically under-resourced.

## A narrow opening for reform

There is at least one regulatory process still open that could shape access going forward. Peru's agricultural health agency, Senasa, together with the Ministry of Agrarian Development, opened an early public consultation on a dedicated Cannabis Seeds Regulation, with a first session held in mid-2025 and the issue listed among the agency's priority regulatory problems. Patient associations have used the process to push for rules that would let them legally source, test and register seed varieties suited to therapeutic use — a piece of the supply chain the current framework leaves largely unaddressed. Whether that consultation produces a final regulation under the incoming administration, and whether Fujimori's government revisits the municipal-licensing bottleneck or Ámbar's request to reform the underlying rules, will be an early test of how a "mano dura" security agenda coexists with a health system that already recognizes cannabis as medicine for tens of thousands of Peruvians — most of whom, for now, still cannot legally obtain it.
