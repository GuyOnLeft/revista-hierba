A bill introduced in Argentina's Chamber of Deputies at the end of May 2026 would go further than any cannabis policy the country has debated to date: full legalization of cultivation, sale and personal use, plus an amnesty that would erase criminal records for anyone previously prosecuted for possession, home growing or low-level distribution. The proposal, sponsored by national deputies Nicolás del Caño and Myriam Bregman of the Frente de Izquierda y de los Trabajadores–Unidad (FIT-U), does not amend Argentina's existing medical cannabis law, Ley 27.350. It would sit alongside it, opening a second, non-medical track that the current framework does not contemplate.

That distinction matters for how the bill should be read. Ley 27.350 and the REPROCANN registry it created were built to authorize therapeutic use — cultivation and possession for patients with a doctor's indication. Argentina's cannabis patient population has grown fast under that system, and so has its backlog: REPROCANN only began automating approvals for low-risk applications in February 2026, after what the Ministry of Health described as years of delay and "thousands" of accumulated case files. The FIT-U bill is not a fix for that backlog. It is a parallel proposal that would make cannabis legal for adults regardless of medical status, leaving the therapeutic registry to operate as it already does for patients who want the formal medical pathway.

## What the Bill Proposes

The text authorizes planting, cultivation, production, storage, possession and personal consumption of cannabis without criminal penalty, and it explicitly permits home cultivation — autocultivo — for individual or collective use, outside any state licensing requirement for personal quantities. On the commercial side, it would allow cannabis and its derivatives to be sold nationally under state oversight, with ANMAT, Argentina's medicines and food regulator, positioned to supervise commercial production and distribution rather than criminalize it.

A third piece of the bill creates a legal category for non-profit cannabis social clubs, limited to adult members, modeled loosely on the club systems used in Uruguay and parts of Spain to provide legal access outside both the illicit market and full commercial retail. And the bill's most immediately consequential provision, from the standpoint of people currently facing charges, is its amnesty clause: it would annul criminal convictions and dismiss pending prosecutions for cannabis consumption, possession or cultivation, retroactively clearing records built up under Argentina's existing narcotics law.

Del Caño and Bregman frame the proposal around a single argument: that decades of prohibition did not reduce cannabis use, but did hand a profitable, unregulated market to organized trafficking networks while filling courts and, in some cases, prisons with low-level users and small growers. State-regulated legal sale, in their framing, removes that revenue stream from traffickers, reduces the risk of adulterated product circulating in an unregulated market, and stops treating personal drug use as a criminal matter rather than a public-health one.

## An Uncertain Path

None of that argument guarantees the bill goes anywhere. The FIT-U holds a small minority of seats in the Chamber of Deputies, and Argentina's two previous attempts at comprehensive cannabis reform — including a broader decriminalization push floated in earlier legislative sessions — stalled in committee without reaching a floor vote. As of this writing, the bill has been formally submitted and is awaiting assignment to committee debate, with no timeline set for when, or whether, it will be taken up.

Opposition arguments reported alongside the bill's introduction center on two claims: that recreational legalization carries public-health risks the state is not equipped to manage, and that Argentina lacks the regulatory and enforcement infrastructure — dosage standards, retail licensing systems, tax collection mechanisms — that mature legal markets elsewhere have taken years to build. Neither claim is new to the cannabis debate; both have shaped the slower, medical-only path Argentina chose in 2017 when it passed Ley 27.350 instead of broader legalization.

## A Reform Debate With a Long History

Argentina's relationship with cannabis policy has moved in increments for nearly two decades, almost always pushed by courts or executive decree rather than a comprehensive vote in Congress. The Corte Suprema's 2009 ruling in the Arriola case established that criminalizing personal drug possession for private consumption was unconstitutional when it caused no harm to third parties — a precedent that decriminalized use in practice without ever legalizing supply, leaving patients and casual users in a legal gray zone that persists today. Ley 27.350, passed in 2017, addressed only the medical side of that gap, and even then partially: it took a 2020 regulatory decree to expand the law beyond a narrow list of conditions and authorize home cultivation for registered patients through REPROCANN.

The FIT-U bill is the first attempt in years to ask Congress to close the remaining gap by statute rather than leave it to courts and regulators. Uruguay, for comparison, took the legislative route directly in 2013, becoming the first country to fully legalize cannabis production, sale and consumption under state control — a model Del Caño and Bregman cite explicitly as evidence that a regulated market can function without the public-health collapse opponents warn of. Whether Argentina's Congress, more fragmented and more conservative on drug policy than Uruguay's was in 2013, will treat that precedent as persuasive is an open question the bill's committee assignment has not yet begun to answer.

## Why the Framing Matters

The bill's timing is not incidental. It arrives as Argentina's medical cannabis system remains strained — REPROCANN's backlog is only beginning to clear under the new automated approval process, and patients in several provinces continue to report friction between their medical authorization and everyday encounters with law enforcement, from roadside drug testing to prosecutions over cultivation quantities that exceed what a judge considers "personal." Advocates for the FIT-U bill argue that a purely medical framework will always leave gaps like these, because it depends on doctors, registries and case-by-case judicial interpretation to draw a line that, in their view, should not need to be drawn for personal use in the first place.

Critics of that position counter that conflating medical and recreational cannabis policy risks slowing the medical system's own credibility and complicating the political coalition that got Ley 27.350 passed in the first place — a coalition built substantially around patients and families, not recreational users. That tension between the two reform tracks, medical and recreational, is likely to shape how — or whether — the bill moves through committee in the months ahead.

For now, the proposal remains exactly that: a proposal. No committee has scheduled a hearing, and no government bloc with the votes to pass it has signaled support. But the bill puts a specific, detailed model of full legalization with retroactive amnesty formally on Argentina's legislative record for the first time in this Congress, giving the debate a concrete text to organize around rather than the more abstract calls for reform that have circulated in previous years.
