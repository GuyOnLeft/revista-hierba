While Brazil debates which patient associations can afford to cultivate under its new sandbox, and Mexico's Congress lets a promised cannabis law gather dust for another year, a smaller but instructive drama is unfolding two seas away, in the Caribbean nation that arguably has the deepest cultural claim to the plant of any country in the hemisphere. Jamaica is trying to bring its traditional ganja farmers — many of them Rastafari, many of them growers whose families cultivated in the hills long before "medical cannabis" was a licensing category — into a legal industry that, a decade after legalization, has mostly rewarded people with capital rather than people with knowledge. The reform effort now underway is a useful mirror for the debates playing out across Latin America: legalizing a plant is not the same as repairing the harm caused by criminalizing it.

Jamaica decriminalized possession of up to two ounces of cannabis and established a licensed medical, scientific, and religious cannabis framework under the Dangerous Drugs (Amendment) Act of 2015, ending, on paper, more than a century of prohibition. In practice, the formal industry that grew out of that law looked very different from the informal one it was meant to absorb. According to the Cannabis Licensing Authority (CLA), fewer than 200 licenses have been issued across cultivation, processing, retail, transport, and research — a small, capital-intensive sector layered on top of tens of thousands of small growers who kept cultivating, mostly unlicensed, because the compliance costs, banking barriers, and paperwork of the legal system were out of reach.

## A faith the law once hunted

To understand why this matters beyond agricultural policy, it helps to remember what prohibition actually did in Jamaica. Rastafari use cannabis, or ganja, as a sacrament — a practice inseparable from the movement's spiritual life since its emergence in the 1930s. For decades, that made Rastafari a target of state violence, not just drug enforcement. The starkest example is the Coral Gardens incident of April 1963, when police and military units swept through Rastafari communities in St. James parish following a land dispute that turned deadly, rounding up more than 150 people, forcibly cutting dreadlocks, and leaving several Rastafarians, civilians, and police officers dead. It took until 2017 for the Jamaican government to formally apologize for what it called a "repressive" episode and to establish a trust fund for survivors, funded at roughly 10 million Jamaican dollars.

That history is precisely why the current push to formalize small-scale ganja cultivation carries weight beyond economics. As anthropologist Felipe Neis Araujo, a criminology lecturer at the University of Manchester, argued in a piece for the harm-reduction outlet Filter, cannabis regulation in Jamaica has largely avoided any explicit reparative framework for the Rastafari and rural growers most harmed by prohibition — even as foreign capital moves comparatively easily into the licensed sector. Araujo described a Rastafari acquaintance, who served prison time for distributing ganja he regarded as a sacrament, asking a pointed question as legalization unfolded: who, exactly, would be getting the grow permits.

## New permits, old gap

The Jamaican government's answer, at least on paper, arrived this year. In March 2026, the CLA gazetted two new categories designed explicitly to bring small and traditional cultivators into the regulated system. The Cultivator's (Transitional) Special Permit lets experienced growers who lack formal financial or technical capacity operate legally for a non-renewable two-year window, with an option to extend, while they work toward full compliance. The Special Community Permit, which charges no fee, allows organized community groups to pool land and resources and cultivate collectively — up to ten acres under regulatory oversight.

State Minister Delano Seiveright, who oversees the portfolio at the Ministry of Industry, Investment and Commerce, has framed the permits as the foundation of "a more inclusive model that supports scale, structure, and shared opportunity at the community level." In July, his ministry launched a Cannabis Permit Road Tour, starting at Newell High School in St. Elizabeth parish, to explain the new categories directly to cultivation communities and help farmers apply. The government has paired that outreach with public warnings: on July 14, the CLA cautioned growers about scammers exploiting the moment of transition with fake promises, urging farmers to verify any offer directly with the authority before handing over money, land, or signatures.

## The bottleneck nobody has solved

Even sympathetic observers inside the reform say expanding who can legally grow ganja solves only part of the problem — and could make things worse if nothing else changes. Ras Iyah V, a longtime Rastafari cannabis advocate, chairman of the Westmoreland Hemp and Ganja Growers Association, and a CLA board member himself, welcomed the new permits publicly but warned, in comments to the Jamaica Observer in late July, that admitting more growers without expanding processing capacity would simply flood the market with raw flower nobody can buy. Existing licensed cultivators, he noted, already struggle to find buyers domestically and abroad; Jamaica's only GMP-certified processing facility reportedly shut down after its investor lost more than ten million dollars trying to keep it running. Without extraction infrastructure to turn flower into oils, distillates, and other exportable products, he argued, "this programme is not going to work."

Export markets remain the industry's other structural wall. The CLA has issued more than 200 authorizations to export cannabis and hemp products, but Jamaica's ability to actually ship medical cannabis into its largest potential market, Canada, has been blocked for years by Health Canada's refusal to fully implement the import provisions of its own Cannabis Act — a restriction Jamaica and Colombia have both formally protested. Meanwhile, according to Araujo's reporting, a Canadian company has been permitted to export cannabis into the Jamaican market, undercutting local producers on their home turf, an asymmetry that captures the imbalance critics say defines the current system.

## What legalization owes

None of this is unique to Jamaica. Latin American readers have seen versions of the same pattern: Brazil's new cultivation sandbox for patient associations requires upfront investment that can run past a million reais, pricing out exactly the low-income associations that built the movement through habeas corpus litigation. Colombia's licensed export sector has scaled quickly while small growers in regions with a long cannabis-growing tradition struggle to formalize. The common thread is a policy sequence that legalizes the plant before it repairs the relationship between the state and the people it once criminalized for growing it.

Jamaica's transitional and community permits are a genuine attempt to close that gap, and they emerged from real pressure by growers' associations and advocates like Ras Iyah V, who sit inside the regulatory process rather than only outside it. But permits without processing capacity, export access, or financing are, as critics note, an invitation without a way to attend. Whether Jamaica turns this reform into durable inclusion — or into another chapter where legalization mostly benefits whoever already had capital — will depend on decisions still ahead: how the export ban with Canada gets resolved, whether new processing facilities get built, and whether the government treats the Rastafari and rural growers who preserved ganja cultivation through a century of prohibition as partners owed something, not just applicants to be processed.
