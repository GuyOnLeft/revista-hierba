A clinical pharmacokinetic trial involving researchers from the FDA's Division of Applied Regulatory Science found that daily CBD (cannabidiol) use can raise blood levels of citalopram, one of the world's most widely prescribed antidepressants, to a clinically significant degree. The study, published in *Clinical Pharmacology & Therapeutics* under the title "Clinical Study to Evaluate Drug Interactions of Cannabidiol with Citalopram and Morphine in Healthy Adults," adds solid evidence to a warning that until now circulated more as pharmacological intuition than as measured human data.

The trial's design was straightforward: 40 healthy adults were split into two groups. One received a single 20 mg dose of citalopram before and after 12 days of twice-daily CBD, dosed at 2.5 mg per kilogram of body weight — comparable to many over-the-counter CBD products. The other group received a 15 mg dose of morphine, also before and after the same period of CBD exposure.

## What They Found

The results were asymmetric between the two drugs tested. For citalopram, CBD increased the drug's total systemic exposure (measured as area under the curve, or AUC) by 43% — with a 95% confidence interval of 1.34 to 1.55 in the geometric mean ratio — and its peak blood concentration (Cmax) by 12%. That's a pharmacologically meaningful interaction, likely explained by CBD's inhibition of certain cytochrome P450 enzymes, the family of liver enzymes responsible for metabolizing a huge share of commonly used medications, citalopram included.

For morphine, by contrast, CBD's effect was far more limited: the exposure ratio stayed between roughly 1.06 and 1.12, without clear clinical significance. In other words, CBD doesn't affect every medication equally — unsurprising, since it acts on specific metabolic pathways — and that's exactly why the citalopram finding can't automatically be generalized to every CBD-drug combination. What it does show, with controlled human data, is that at least one widely used combination carries a real, measurable risk.

## Why It Matters in Latin America

Citalopram and its active isomer, escitalopram, are among the most commonly prescribed antidepressants in the region, in both public health systems and private practice. At the same time, across much of Latin America CBD circulates in an ambiguous regulatory space: sold as oil, drops or capsules in pharmacies, health-food stores and online platforms, often without a prescription, without standardized dosing, and without the patient ever telling the doctor who prescribed their antidepressant.

That pattern isn't accidental. Revista Hierba has documented in other reporting how, in countries where access to regulated medical cannabis programs is slow, bureaucratic or simply nonexistent, patients turn to unregulated CBD products as a de facto substitute — often with no medical guidance at all. This study doesn't question CBD's potential therapeutic value, but it does challenge the widespread assumption that, because it's "natural" and sold over the counter, CBD is automatically harmless when combined with psychiatric medication.

## What It Means in Practice

A 43% increase in systemic exposure to an antidepressant isn't an abstract figure: it can translate into a higher risk of adverse effects tied to elevated citalopram levels, including cardiac rhythm disturbances (citalopram carries a known warning about QT-interval prolongation at high doses), excessive sedation, or serotonergic symptoms. For a patient already taking citalopram under medical supervision who decides to add CBD on their own — for sleep, anxiety or pain — that increase in exposure happens silently: there's no immediate symptom warning that blood levels of the antidepressant have climbed 43%.

The researchers themselves, affiliated with the FDA's Division of Applied Regulatory Science, frame the finding within a broader effort to generate rigorous pharmacokinetic data on interactions between cannabinoids and commonly used medications — an evidence gap that became more urgent as CBD use spread far faster than the clinical research meant to keep pace with it.

## A Warning, Not a Verdict

It's important to read this study with the same caution with which it was designed: it doesn't conclude that CBD is inherently dangerous, or that it should be avoided. It concludes, with concrete data, that combining it with certain medications — in this case, citalopram — requires medical supervision and dose adjustment, exactly as happens with many other drug combinations that interact through the cytochrome P450 system. The problem isn't CBD; it's CBD use the treating physician doesn't know about.

For health systems across the region, the finding also makes a case for better integrating conversations about cannabinoids — medicinal or over-the-counter — into routine medical visits, rather than treating them as a separate topic patients manage on their own. Until that happens, the risk documented in this study will remain, for many patients, an invisible one.
