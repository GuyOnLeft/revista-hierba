On July 18, 2026, Paraguay's Armed Forces and National Police intercepted 299 bricks of marijuana — 6,877 kilograms in total — hidden inside the Yvytyrokái Nature Reserve near the Itaipú reservoir, in the Alto Paraná department. Naval prefect Captain Milciades Coronel confirmed that those responsible for the shipment fled before authorities arrived. Two days earlier, Paraguay's National Anti-Drug Secretariat (SENAD) had dismantled three processing camps in Cerro Corá and destroyed more than 21 tonnes of marijuana and seven hectares of crops, in a strike investigators estimated cost trafficking organizations roughly $3 million.

Those are just two operations in a single week, but together they capture a contradiction that has shaped Paraguayan cannabis policy for years: even as the country functions as South America's largest illegal marijuana producer — supplying, by industry estimates, as much as 80% of the marijuana consumed in Brazil — the law that in 2017 was supposed to open a legal, regulated path to medical cannabis remains, nearly a decade later, largely unenforced.

## A Corridor Into Brazil

Geography explains much of the story. Alto Paraná, the department where the July seizure took place, borders the Brazilian state of Paraná across the Itaipú reservoir, one of the world's largest hydroelectric dams and also one of the region's most porous border crossings. The illegal trade in Paraguayan marijuana into Brazil moves an estimated $700 million a year, according to press estimates based on seizure data, and much of that distribution chain inside Brazil is controlled by the Primeiro Comando da Capital (PCC), the country's largest organized crime group.

Production is concentrated in rural areas of eastern Paraguay, where for decades marijuana cultivation became an economic alternative for small farming families displaced by the expansion of genetically modified soy and land consolidation. This is not a boutique or artisanal crop: these are extensive plantations, often protected by armed groups, supplying a Brazilian demand that shows no sign of shrinking. The violence tied to those trafficking routes has pushed some Paraguayan border departments to homicide rates comparable to Honduras, one of the most violent countries in the hemisphere.

## The Law That Never Took Off

In contrast to that multimillion-dollar illegal economy, medical cannabis in Paraguay remains largely stuck on paper. Law 6007, passed in 2017, authorized the production, importation and medical or scientific use of cannabis and its derivatives, and was celebrated at the time as a regional pioneer. Nearly a decade later, the law's real-world implementation — licensing domestic producers, getting products onto pharmacy shelves, covering patients — moves at a far slower pace than the illegal trade surrounding it.

The result is a paradox Revista Hierba has already documented in other countries across the region: patients who need cannabis oil or flower for conditions like refractory epilepsy, chronic pain or chemotherapy side effects have, in practice, no legal, safe and accessible channel to obtain it, even as security forces devote significant resources to pursuing an illegal production chain that isn't oriented toward the medical market at all, but toward cross-border trafficking.

## Two Different Stories, One Plant

It's important not to conflate the two stories, even though they involve the same plant. The marijuana seized at Yvytyrokái and Cerro Corá was not headed for patients — it was feeding an illegal recreational export market, with all the structural violence that business carries. Paraguayan patients' fight for legal access to medical cannabis is a different problem, with different causes — lack of operational regulation, no licensed domestic pharmaceutical industry, health-bureaucracy bottlenecks — and it will not resolve itself simply because trafficking is fought harder, nor will trafficking disappear because the medical law gets streamlined.

But both stories do share a political lesson that regional activists have been making for years: total prohibition, on its own, has not contained marijuana production or trade in Paraguay. Military operations destroy shipments and camps, but they don't alter the economic structure pushing farming families toward this crop, nor the price gap that makes illegal export to Brazil so profitable. Meanwhile, the regulated path — which in theory could offer producers a legal economic alternative and patients safe access — is still waiting for the political will and administrative resources to actually function.

## What's Next

Neither the Paraguayan government nor SENAD has announced a change of approach following the July operations: the strategy remains military and police interdiction in cultivation zones and along border crossings into Brazil. On the medical side, patient organizations in Paraguay continue to call for faster regulation of Law 6007, with no public reform timeline announced so far. For the region's broader debate over cannabis regulation — one that in countries like Uruguay and Colombia is moving toward increasingly wide legal markets — Paraguay remains the clearest example of what happens when prohibition and regulation coexist without either one fully taking hold.
