In June, Manaus hosted Brazil's first-ever National Congress on Microbial Diversity, an unprecedented gathering of more than 600 researchers and nearly 800 scientific works organized around the challenges of climate change. Among the posters filling the exhibition hall was one that quietly linked two worlds Western science usually keeps apart: the sap of a rainforest tree that Amazonian Indigenous peoples have used for generations to close wounds, and the global crisis of antibiotic-resistant bacteria. A team from the Federal University of Roraima (UFRR) presented laboratory results on the antimicrobial activity of copaíba oleoresin against a multidrug-resistant strain of *Staphylococcus pseudintermedius*, a pathogen that now leads hard-to-treat skin and ear infections in dogs and is increasingly detected in the people who live alongside them.

## A resin healers already trusted

Copaíba is not a laboratory discovery. It is one of the most thoroughly documented plant substances in Amazonian folk pharmacy: harvested without felling the tree, through taps bored into the trunks of species in the genus *Copaifera* that can yield up to 40 liters of resin a year, its medicinal use in northern and northeastern Brazil traces back, according to folk-medicine records, to the sixteenth century. An ethnobotanical study published in the journal *Gaia Scientia* in 2019 by researchers from the Federal University of Amazonas and INPA documented, among the Makuxi community of Darora—within the São Marcos Indigenous Land, in the Roraima savanna—21 distinct uses of copaíba (*Copaifera pubiflora*) reported by 60 interviewees. Forty-seven percent of those citations were medicinal, mostly topical: wound healing, treatment of skin infections, and pain relief. The genus *Copaifera* spans much of the Amazon basin—Brazil, Peru, Bolivia, Colombia, Venezuela, the Guianas—so this knowledge belongs to no single people; it circulates, with local variations, among dozens of Indigenous and riverine communities.

## What was presented in Manaus

The UFRR study, listed as poster number 263 and presented on June 23 under the "Antimicrobials" category, was titled "Evaluation of the in vitro antimicrobial activity of copaíba oleoresin against multidrug-resistant *Staphylococcus pseudintermedius*." Authored by Millena dos Santos Cruz and a team of seven other researchers, the study exposed bacterial cultures to the resin under controlled laboratory conditions. As of this writing, the congress's public program did not include the exact inhibition figures or minimum effective concentration—data typically held back for later publication in a peer-reviewed journal—so the finding is best treated for what it is: an active line of research, not a confirmed cure. What matters, regardless, is the question it posed: whether a traditional compound can slow a bacterium that no longer responds to several commonly used antibiotics.

That question carries real weight. *Staphylococcus pseudintermedius* lives naturally on the skin and mucous membranes of dogs, but its methicillin-resistant variants have become the leading multidrug-resistant bacterial cause of canine skin infections in veterinary clinics worldwide, and documented cases of transmission between dogs and immunocompromised people make it a genuinely One Health problem—human, animal, and environmental at once.

## The evidence that gives it grounding

The hypothesis the Roraima team explored did not come out of nowhere. In 2013, researchers at the Federal University of Santa Maria, in Rio Grande do Sul, published a study in the journal *Pesquisa Veterinária Brasileira* that exposed *Copaifera reticulata* oleoresin to coagulase-positive *Staphylococcus* strains isolated from canine otitis cases, many of them also multidrug-resistant. The results showed a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC90) of 0.164 mg/mL and a minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC90) of 1.3 mg/mL: the resin was bacteriostatic and, at higher concentrations, bactericidal, even against resistant strains. Gas chromatography identified beta-caryophyllene, beta-bisabolene, and (E)-alpha-bergamotene as the dominant compounds—all sesquiterpenes with anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial activity already described in independent research. That precedent—more than a decade old, but peer-reviewed and backed by hard numbers—is what turns the Manaus poster into the logical continuation of a legitimate scientific question, rather than an isolated anecdote.

## Why it matters beyond one poster

The World Health Organization estimates that antimicrobial resistance is directly linked to more than a million deaths worldwide each year, and warns that the spread of multidrug-resistant bacteria—so-called superbugs—threatens to make infections considered minor today untreatable. In that context, the Amazon occupies a paradoxical place: a study from the University of São Paulo, reported by Agência FAPESP, found that deforestation of the rainforest itself promotes the proliferation of soil bacteria carrying resistance genes, by disrupting the microbial balance that native vegetation sustains. In other words, the forest that could yield new molecules to fight antimicrobial resistance is also the one disappearing fastest. It is estimated that only 10 to 15 percent of plant species with known antimicrobial activity have been thoroughly studied, leaving Amazonian bioprospecting—done rigorously, and with explicit recognition of the Indigenous authorship behind the knowledge that originates it—an enormous and still largely unexplored field.

None of this makes copaíba a cure-all. The road from an in vitro assay to a safe clinical treatment is long: it requires isolating active compounds, testing toxicity, standardizing doses, and eventually clearing clinical trials. But the underlying gesture—bringing into the laboratory, with method and peer-reviewed publication, a remedy that communities like the Makuxi of Darora documented and sustained over generations—is exactly the kind of dialogue serious ethnobotany should pursue: neither romanticizing ancestral knowledge as untouchable, nor dismissing it for lacking a lab coat, but holding it to the same rigor as any other scientific hypothesis.
