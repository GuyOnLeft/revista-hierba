In Mocoa, the small Amazonian-Andean capital of Colombia's Putumayo department, a room stacked with pressed leaves and dried seeds is doing something most public institutions in the region have never managed: cataloguing, one voucher sheet at a time, which plants eleven indigenous peoples still turn to for fever, grief, difficult births and infection, and pairing each one with the Latin name that Colombian courts, mining companies and environmental agencies are obligated to recognize.

The Herbario Etnobotánico del Piedemonte Andino-Amazónico "Jajen Saima'a," housed at the Institución Universitaria del Putumayo in Mocoa, holds more than 22,000 samples representing roughly 1,500 plant species, according to reporting by Mongabay Latam. It was formally created in 2022, with backing from the regional environmental authority Corpoamazonía and Conservación Internacional Colombia, but its real infrastructure is older and more human: it runs on the participation of monitors from the Kamëntšá, Inga, Siona, Cofán, Coreguaje, Murui, Kichwa, Nasa, Pasto, Embera and Awá peoples, alongside campesino and Afro-descendant communities, who contribute specimens, names and stories that Latin taxonomy alone cannot hold.

This is not a museum exercise. Putumayo is one of Colombia's most biodiverse and most contested departments, home to an estimated 5,826 plant species, 316 of them found nowhere else, and also to active mining titles, coca-driven land pressure and decades of armed conflict that scattered the very communities whose plant knowledge the herbarium is trying to preserve. The mining company Libero Cobre holds four titles covering roughly 7,800 hectares in the region, with an estimated two million tons of copper beneath ground that includes part of the Yunguillo indigenous reserve. Communities have begun citing the herbarium's documentation, species names, distribution data, recorded uses, as evidence when they contest those projects, turning the same technical language extractive companies rely on back against them.

## A catalogue against disappearance

The herbarium's method is less a scientific expedition than a slow, negotiated collaboration. Sixteen community monitors from the Yunguillo Inga reserve alone have taken part in plant surveys tied to the project. A parallel initiative, Árbol Semilleros, has identified 71 tree species and documented propagation methods for each, an attempt to make sure that if a tree disappears from the forest, its seed and its cultivation knowledge do not disappear with it. A 2023 program called Nacederos produced educational booklets identifying the plants communities consider guardians of water sources, tying ethnobotany explicitly to hydrology. The herbarium has also published a bilingual reference describing 25 culturally significant plants, an attempt to make the record usable by the communities that generated it, not only by outside researchers.

None of this is abstract conservation. Jorge Contreras, the herbarium's coordinating biologist, and researchers such as Nicolás Castaño, from the Amazonian research institute SINCHI, work alongside people like Ángela Jhoana Jacanamejoy, a Kamëntšá biologist and artist, and Luis Felipe Mora, a Cofán environmental engineer, translating field observation into something a herbarium sheet, and eventually a legal filing, can carry.

## Three plants, three warnings

Three species documented by the project illustrate what is at stake, and why a single institution cannot substitute for a living forest.

Granadillo, a hardwood tree used both in traditional construction and, in some communities, medicinally, is now scarce enough that Inga monitor Juliana Torres Jiménez told Mongabay Latam she doubts it will still exist locally within a decade: in ten years, she said, there will barely be granadillo trees left.

Caimo de monte, or cascabel, carries a different kind of scarcity. Its seed holds ceremonial and artisanal significance for several Putumayo peoples, but researchers monitoring the Yunguillo reserve's 26,480 hectares have found only eight surviving specimens in the entire territory, a population small enough that its local disappearance is a realistic possibility within a generation, not a distant hypothesis.

Native cacao is the clearest case of a plant treated as medicine rather than symbol. Community members describe it as good for the cardiovascular system, for stress, as food and as highly nutritious, and grandparents recall preparing it for women during and after pregnancy. Some of that testimony has partial backing in the wider scientific literature: cacao's flavanol content has been studied for cardiovascular effects in clinical research outside Colombia, and European food-safety regulators have recognized a cardiovascular claim tied specifically to cocoa flavanols. The claim about pregnancy is harder to verify and, if anything, cuts the other way: most current clinical guidance urges moderating caffeine and theobromine intake during pregnancy rather than increasing it. The herbarium's role is not to adjudicate which pieces of testimony hold up under a clinical trial; it is to make sure the testimony itself, contradictions included, does not vanish before anyone has the chance to study it.

## Science as ally, not substitute

It would be easy to tell this as a redemption story: ancestral wisdom finally validated by modern botany. That framing flatters everyone involved but misses what the herbarium's own struggles reveal. A separate ethnobotanical survey conducted in Colón, a Putumayo municipality, interviewed 100 residents in 2022 and documented 38 medicinal plant species across 18 families, but the most-cited species were chamomile, lemon verbena, oregano, peppermint and lemongrass, all introduced garden plants rather than native Amazonian species. Traditional medicine in Putumayo today is not a pristine, undisturbed inheritance; it is a hybrid, adaptive practice built from whatever has been available across generations of displacement, migration and trade. That mix is worth documenting precisely because it is real, not despite it.

That risk is not hypothetical in the region. Latin America has repeatedly watched documented traditional knowledge migrate into outside patents and pharmaceutical products with little or no benefit returning to the communities that generated it. The herbarium's coordinators describe their documentation as primarily defensive, a way for communities to hold the evidence themselves rather than waiting for an outside institution to hold it for them. Whether that intention survives contact with commercial interest, once cacao's flavanols or some as-yet-unstudied Putumayo species attract outside attention, is a question the project has not yet had to answer.

The precarity is also real. As of the most recent reporting, the herbarium still has no permanent building of its own, its collection is split between the university and Corpoamazonía's facilities, there is no dedicated curator position, and its coordinator works without a stable employment contract, four years after the project's formal founding. The university's vice-rector, Miguel Ángel Canchala, has said a permanent site should be ready within two to three months, but similar promises have a way of stretching in institutions without secure funding.

That fragility is the honest ending to this story. A herbarium that can name the eight remaining specimens of a ceremonially vital tree, and that indigenous communities are already using to contest mining titles in court, is treating plant knowledge as evidence rather than folklore, which is exactly what a magazine built around patients' and communities' rights should want to see. But evidence stored in a room without walls of its own is still vulnerable to the same forces, mining pressure, funding cycles, institutional neglect, that are thinning the forests it is trying to describe.
