Before the cannabis oil, Zaia was seizing every 20 to 40 minutes. She was four years old, diagnosed with severe refractory epilepsy — the kind that does not respond to conventional anticonvulsants. With the homemade oil her parents prepared at their house in Itapiranga, in the far west of Santa Catarina state, the seizures nearly disappeared. Her physician documented the improvement and confirmed that the artisanal oil worked better for Zaia than the industrialized alternatives available to the family.

On June 25, 2026, a court in Itapiranga convicted Zaia's parents, Henrique Carvalho and Rosimara Renz, of drug trafficking and criminal association. He received a sentence of 12 years, 8 months, and 13 days in closed-regime prison, plus a fine of roughly R$96,000. She was sentenced to 10 years, 10 months, and 20 days, with a fine of approximately R$82,000. Henrique remains in custody. Rosimara is appealing while free, wearing an electronic ankle monitor.

The cultivation that sustained their daughter's treatment had begun as something entirely different: a judicial authorization for Henrique to treat his own medical condition with cannabis. What happened between that authorization and a decade-long prison sentence has become one of the most cited cases among those warning about the fragility of home-grow medical cannabis models across Latin America.

## An Authorization With an Expiration Date

In the absence of a stable regulatory framework for medicinal cultivation, thousands of Brazilian families turned — almost as their only option — to the *salvo-conduto*: an individual judicial authorization, obtained through habeas corpus, that allows a person to grow cannabis to treat a specific medical condition without exposure to criminal prosecution. Henrique had obtained his for his own treatment. According to prosecutors, that document covered only his case — not Zaia's, not his mother-in-law's, not the family's pets — and had lapsed in January 2025, nearly a year before the raid.

For the family and their defense, that lapse was more administrative than substantive: renewing a salvo-conduto carries costs many families cannot sustain, and Zaia's treatment continued because stopping it meant, in practice, sending her back to seizures every twenty minutes. Professor Paulo César Pontes Fraga, a drug policy specialist at the Federal University of Juiz de Fora, described the salvo-conduto system as "precarious for guaranteeing a right to health," and asked publicly: "Who benefits from imprisoning a father?"

## A Raid With a Weapon Pointed at a 12-Year-Old

In December 2025, Santa Catarina police carried out Operação Menor Protetor ("Operation Child Protector") at the family's home. More than ten armed officers took part. According to the family's account, one officer pointed his weapon at Rosimara's 12-year-old daughter during the operation; both girls reportedly developed symptoms of post-traumatic stress afterward. Zaia's grandmother was also detained and held for 24 hours.

The raid seized 125 cannabis plants and roughly 25 kilograms of processed material, packaged into about 120 portions stored in refrigerators and freezers. Officers also confiscated precision scales, paperwork that prosecutors described as price lists, records of Pix digital payments, and documentation of shipments sent by mail to other Brazilian states.

The defense, led by attorneys Matteus Jacarandá and Jamil Issy Neto, has challenged both the procedure and the technical evidence. They argue that of the 62 bottles seized, only six were tested, and that three of those contained no prohibited substance at all — only chamomile and rosemary. They also note that the tests performed were qualitative, confirming the presence of THC, but not quantitative — meaning they did not precisely measure its concentration — even though the sentence relied on figures of 15% to 26% THC in the plants. According to local press reports, the substitute judge who issued the conviction, Victor Mattos, reportedly based part of his analysis of cannabis potency on a quick internet search, a detail the defense cites as evidence of flaws in the trial's evidentiary standard.

## Trafficking, or a Cost-Sharing Network Among Patients?

The core of the dispute — the point that separates a trafficking narrative from a healthcare-access narrative — lies in how the financial evidence is read. Santa Catarina prosecutors maintain that the family's production was not limited to personal use: that price lists, Pix payments, and out-of-state mail shipments existed, and that together these describe a commercial operation. The public prosecutor's office was explicit about its position: moral debates over drug legislation belong in Congress, not in the courts, which must apply the law as written.

The family and their defense offer a different reading. Rosimara, a veterinarian by profession, had been building an association called "Flor de Zaia" ("Zaia's Flower"), intended to supply medicinal cannabis to students at a local institution supporting people with disabilities, under a cost-sharing arrangement among patient families — the same associative model that, elsewhere in Brazil and in other countries in the region, operates with formal legal recognition. Under that reading, the Pix payments and shipments do not prove a for-profit sale but rather the logistics of an access network for patients who could not afford imported cannabis. "This is a public health issue, not party politics. We're a family struggling here, with no way to change this reality, no voice," Rosimara said after the conviction.

This article cannot resolve that dispute: both accounts — an organized commercial trafficking operation, or an informal cost-sharing network among patients — were presented to the court, and only one prevailed at first instance. What is established is that the judicial authorization on record covered only Henrique, that it had expired, and that the conviction for trafficking and criminal association now stands for him and remains under appeal for Rosimara.

## A Case With Regional Reach

The Itapiranga case matters beyond Brazil because it exposes a structural vulnerability shared by several medical cannabis access models across the region. In Argentina, the Reprocann registry authorizes home cultivation and solidarity cultivation under medical prescription; Colombia, Peru, and Uruguay have similar mechanisms of individual or associative recognition. In every case, that recognition depends on an administrative or judicial process that can expire, be delayed, or be left to the discretion of whoever reviews it.

The Brazilian case shows what happens when that process fails: a family that documented their treatment before a judge, that had a physician's backing, and that dramatically reduced the suffering of a four-year-old girl, ended up labeled a drug-trafficking organization. The distance between "authorized patient" and "convicted trafficker," in this case, turned out to be the expiration date on a document.

For the movement of families who depend on medicinal cannabis in Brazil and across the region, Itapiranga stands as a warning: legal recognition on paper does not automatically protect patients if regulatory frameworks lack sustainable renewal mechanisms, if the interpretation of financial evidence is left entirely to each court's discretion, or if police and judicial discretion can, in practice, reverse a recognized right. Meanwhile, Henrique remains behind bars, Rosimara is fighting her appeal with a monitor on her ankle, and Zaia still needs the treatment that sent her parents to prison.
