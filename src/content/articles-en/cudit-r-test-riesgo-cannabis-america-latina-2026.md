As Colombia, Uruguay, Argentina, Mexico and Brazil expand — each at its own pace and under different regulatory frameworks — their medical cannabis programs, a problem less visible than legislation is starting to surface: what tools are clinicians and public-health systems actually using to identify who is developing risky use patterns? Until now, the answer has almost always been the same: instruments designed, calibrated and validated in the United States, Canada, Australia or Europe, imported into Latin American settings with little or no adjustment.

A new study led from Bogotá calls that assumption into question. Published on February 24, 2026 in the Revista de la Sociedad Española del Dolor (RESED), the research put the CUDIT-R — the Cannabis Use Disorder Identification Test-Revised, the most widely used screening questionnaire in the world for flagging risky cannabis use — to the test in a sample of nearly 500 people from the region. The result: the instrument showed a sensitivity of only 60.77%, which in plain terms means it fails to detect nearly four out of every ten people who actually have a risky use pattern.

## What the CUDIT-R Is, and Why It's So Widely Used

The CUDIT-R is a brief self-report questionnaire — eight items — originally designed as a screening tool, not a diagnostic one, for clinical and epidemiological use. It does not determine whether someone has cannabis use disorder in the formal clinical sense; its function is more modest, and for that reason far more widely deployed: to flag, quickly and cheaply, who has an elevated probability of being in a risk zone and should receive a fuller evaluation or a brief intervention.

That efficiency is exactly what explains its success. The CUDIT-R is used in primary-care clinics, university epidemiological studies, occupational health programs, and medical cannabis research across dozens of countries. But like any psychometric instrument, it was built and calibrated on specific populations — predominantly North American, Australian and European — whose cultural patterns of use, perceived risk thresholds, and even the way questions get interpreted, do not necessarily match those of other regions. That a tool performs well where it was created is no guarantee it performs equally well once transplanted elsewhere without modification.

## The Study: What the Researchers Did and Found

The research was led by Ana Ruth Valencia Cárdenas and Nubia Fernanda Sánchez Bello, affiliated with Hospital Militar Central and the Sociedad Colombiana de Anestesiología y Reanimación (SCARE), in Bogotá. It is an observational, analytical psychometric validation study — it does not measure whether cannabis itself is more or less risky, but whether the instrument used to measure that risk actually measures it well.

To do so, the researchers worked with a subsample of 465 people over the age of 18 who had used cannabis in the previous six months, drawn from a broader pool of 4,712 undergraduate students across seven countries in the region. On those 465 cases, they applied a standard psychometric protocol: they tested the questionnaire's factor structure using Bartlett's test of sphericity and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of sampling adequacy, ran an exploratory factor analysis with Promax rotation, measured internal consistency with Cronbach's alpha, and assessed criterion validity through Spearman correlation against an external reference standard.

The central finding was twofold. First, the CUDIT-R's sensitivity in this Latin American sample was 60.77%, with a specificity of 85.06%. Second, when the researchers removed items 1 and 8 from the questionnaire, both criterion validity and internal consistency improved. In other words: two of the instrument's eight original questions appear to be adding more noise than signal in this population, and a trimmed or reworded version of the test could outperform the standard version still being applied unchanged today.

## Sensitivity and Specificity: What Those Numbers Mean in Practice

These two terms — sensitivity and specificity — are the backbone of any screening test, and it's worth pausing on them, because that's where the study's most quotable figure comes from.

Sensitivity measures a test's ability to correctly identify people who actually have the condition being screened for. A sensitivity of 60.77% means that, out of every 100 people who genuinely have a risky use pattern, the CUDIT-R correctly identifies only about 61 of them; the remaining 39 are classified as low-risk when they are not. That's what statisticians call a false negative, and in public-health terms it translates into something very concrete: people who would need an intervention, follow-up, or at least a clinical conversation, slipping through a screening system that fails to flag them.

Specificity, by contrast, measures a test's ability to correctly identify people who do not have the condition. At 85.06%, the CUDIT-R is reasonably good at avoiding false alarms: most people without risky use are correctly classified as such. That contrast — high specificity, lower sensitivity — is clinically significant: the instrument tends to err more by omission (letting real risk cases pass unnoticed) than by over-flagging (labeling low-risk people as risky).

In a context of expanding medical cannabis access, with more people encountering cannabis products for the first time and health systems still building out support protocols, a detection rate of only about six in ten real cases represents early-intervention opportunities that simply are not happening — not because the underlying problem doesn't exist, but because the tool used to find it isn't calibrated for this population.

## What This Study Does Not Show

It's worth being explicit about the limits of this research, precisely because it is easy to misread. This is not a study about whether cannabis is more or less dangerous in Latin America than elsewhere in the world. It does not measure the prevalence of problematic use, does not compare countries against each other, and does not establish that the region has higher or lower rates of cannabis use disorder. It is, strictly, a study about the accuracy of a measurement instrument — whether the ruler being used to measure risk is properly calibrated for what it's being asked to measure in this particular context.

That distinction is not a minor caveat. Conflating a measurement-tool problem with a substance problem would lead to mistaken conclusions — and potentially to mistaken policy. What the study demonstrates is that a test built elsewhere, on other reference populations, does not automatically translate — without loss — into a different context. This is a methodological finding, not an epidemiological one.

## Homegrown Science in a Field That Usually Imports Its Tools

What makes this work distinctive, beyond the specific number it produced, is that it is methodological research produced and led within the region, examining a tool the region uses every day without ever having tested it locally. In a field — cannabis research — where much of the evidence base, clinical protocols, and assessment instruments arrive ready-made from North America or Europe, a psychometric validation study conducted in Bogotá, using original data from seven Latin American countries, is unusual — and valuable precisely because of that.

The practical conclusion is not to discard the CUDIT-R, but to stop treating it as a universal, neutral instrument. The authors themselves note that the tool retains real utility, but that its performance improves once adjusted — by removing items that underperform in this context — and that further validation across diverse populations within the region is still needed. As more Latin American countries build regulated systems for medical cannabis access, the question of whether the screening instruments their health systems rely on actually work for their populations stops being a technical footnote and becomes a central piece of responsible public-health policy.
