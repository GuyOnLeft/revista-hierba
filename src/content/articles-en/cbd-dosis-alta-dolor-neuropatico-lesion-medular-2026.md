For millions of people across Latin America living with spinal cord injuries — from traffic collisions on Andean highways to gunshot wounds in Brazilian cities to falls on construction sites in Mexico — chronic nerve pain is often the cruelest part of recovery. It resists opioids, resists gabapentin, and resists the pregabalin that public health systems from Bogotá to Buenos Aires ration carefully because of cost and side effects. So when a rigorously designed clinical trial reports that cannabidiol (CBD), taken at high doses, produces a measurable reduction in that pain, it is worth paying close attention — and worth being precise about what "measurable" actually means.

That trial, led by researchers at the Brain and Mind Centre at the University of Sydney and Neuroscience Research Australia, was published in May in eClinicalMedicine, a Lancet Discovery Science journal. It is described by its authors as the first randomized, placebo-controlled study to examine high-dose CBD specifically for chronic neuropathic pain caused by spinal cord injury — a population that has been largely absent from cannabinoid research even as it carries some of the heaviest chronic pain burdens in medicine.

## A Rare Rigorous Trial for a Stubborn Kind of Pain

Lead author Dr. Rebecca Robertson and co-investigator Professor Iain McGregor designed a double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial: forty adults with long-standing neuropathic pain following spinal cord injury were randomized to receive either oral CBD or a matched placebo, then switched groups after a washout period, so each participant served as their own control. The CBD dose was escalated gradually from 200 mg to 800 mg a day over six weeks — far above the doses found in most over-the-counter oils sold in pharmacies and dispensaries across the region. Participants rated their pain on a standard 0-10 visual analog scale three times a day.

That dosing detail matters enormously for how this finding should be read in Latin America, where CBD products marketed for pain are frequently sold at 20-50 mg per serving, doses this trial did not test and cannot vouch for.

## What the Numbers Actually Show

Thirty-eight participants completed the primary analysis. On average, pain scores fell by about 14 percent during the CBD phase compared with 6.5 percent during placebo — a modest but statistically significant difference (p<0.001 for the mean difference in pain intensity). More strikingly, when researchers looked at who achieved a clinically meaningful improvement — a reduction of 30 percent or more, the threshold generally considered relevant to a patient's daily life — 37.8 percent of participants hit that mark on CBD, compared with 11.1 percent on placebo.

That gap is the real headline: roughly one in three patients had a response worth noticing, versus roughly one in nine on placebo. It is not a cure, and the authors are explicit about that. "Chronic neuropathic pain is notoriously hard to treat, with traditional therapies ineffective in about 50 percent of cases," Robertson noted, framing CBD as a modest addition to a very difficult clinical picture rather than a breakthrough replacement for existing care.

Safety findings were reassuring but not spotless. High-dose CBD was generally well tolerated, with side effects — mostly tiredness, nausea and diarrhea — reported by 68.4 percent of participants during the CBD phase versus 52.6 percent during placebo, a difference researchers consider consistent with known, manageable CBD effects rather than a red flag. The study also flagged two honest limitations worth repeating rather than glossing over: 78 percent of participants who received placebo correctly guessed their assignment, which can inflate the apparent effect of the active drug through expectation, and most participants were taking other medications concurrently, so drug interactions were not fully controlled for. McGregor said the team's next priority is understanding "why CBD worked better for some people than others" — the kind of individual variability that shows up constantly in cannabinoid research and rarely gets resolved by a single trial.

## Why This Is Not the Same as What Your Local Dispensary Sells

Revista Hierba's editorial commitment is to separate scientific evidence from marketing claims, and this study is a useful test case. The CBD used in the Sydney trial was a pharmaceutical-grade, standardized formulation dosed under medical supervision and escalated far beyond typical consumer products. Extrapolating this result to any bottle of CBD oil bought informally, or even to a legally purchased low-dose product, is not supported by the data. The trial also enrolled a specific population — adults with spinal cord injury and long-standing neuropathic pain — and its findings should not be generalized to other forms of chronic pain, including the low back pain or fibromyalgia populations examined in separate, distinct trials.

## What It Means for the Region

Latin America carries a disproportionate share of the world's spinal cord injuries relative to its population, driven by high rates of road trauma and interpersonal violence in several countries, and post-injury pain management remains inconsistent across public health systems. Regional reviews of neuropathic pain in spinal cord injury populations have found that a striking majority of patients — some studies put the figure between 64 and 82 percent — live with chronic pain long after the initial trauma, often cycling through opioids, anticonvulsants and physical therapies with incomplete relief. Rehabilitation specialists in the region have flagged medical cannabis as an area needing better local evidence for years; this trial, while conducted in Australia, is the kind of controlled data that has been largely missing from that conversation.

Where medical cannabis programs exist — in Colombia, Uruguay, Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, Peru and Chile, each with its own regulatory framework — CBD is often the most accessible cannabinoid because it is non-intoxicating and, in several countries, easier to prescribe or import than THC-containing formulations. This trial offers something that has been scarce in the region's medical cannabis conversation: a controlled dose, a controlled comparison, and a controlled measure of what actually happened to real patients' pain scores, rather than testimonials or industry-funded observational surveys.

It also offers a caution. If public formularies or private clinics in the region begin recommending CBD for spinal cord injury pain on the strength of this study, the responsible version of that recommendation involves pharmaceutical-grade CBD, physician oversight, and doses far higher than most currently regulated consumer products provide — not a repackaging of existing low-dose oils as validated medicine. The authors themselves call for larger, longer trials before CBD moves from "promising modest option" to standard of care. Until then, this study is best read as real, careful evidence of a real, if partial, effect — exactly the kind of finding that deserves neither dismissal nor overselling.
