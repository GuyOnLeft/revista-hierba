Guilherme Viel spent the end of 2025 in a jail cell in Lins, in the interior of São Paulo state, charged in connection with drug trafficking, while regulators in Brasília were, almost simultaneously, drafting the rule meant to give his association a legal path to exist. Viel is the founder and president of Associação Santa Gaia, which by its own count serves roughly 9,000 medical cannabis patients across Brazil, 170 families of them free of charge. Civil police raided its headquarters on October 16, 2025, seized hundreds of plants, and arrested him on the spot. A federal court granted the association a form of judicial safe-conduct days later, but state courts denied at least four subsequent habeas corpus petitions, and Viel remained in custody. His is the most visible case in a pattern that has repeated across at least three Brazilian states in under a year: patients and associations operating in the legal gray zone of a law the courts themselves ordered the state to finish writing.

## Raids in three states while Brasília writes the rules

Santa Gaia is not an isolated case. In January 2026, Rio de Janeiro's military police raided the rural headquarters of Associação Mama Flor Ação Terapêutica, which serves about 300 patients with epilepsy, autism, and chronic pain. According to reporting by the investigative outlet Agência Pública, police seized roughly 120 plants, destroyed part of the crop, and took association leaders to a police station while opening a trafficking investigation. The association had a federal lawsuit pending at the time, seeking authorization to cultivate; days after the raid, a federal judge denied its request for an injunction, ruling that it had to wait for the new regulation to take effect.

A year earlier, in March 2025, federal police carried out Operação Desvio Verde in Santa Maria and Caçapava do Sul, in Rio Grande do Sul state, incinerating 422 plants and 480 seedlings linked to ASCAMED, a local medical cannabis association. That case has a wrinkle: it began with an individual patient's court authorization to grow at his own residence, and investigators allege the cultivation found exceeded that authorization in both volume and location, with signs of distribution lacking Anvisa's sign-off. The association said it held a favorable opinion from the Federal Public Prosecutor's Office and would pursue the matter in court. The case underscores something all three associations share: even patients who obtain some form of judicial permission operate within margins that police and prosecutors interpret narrowly — margins a single inspection can cross.

Caroline Penques, Santa Gaia's director, described the Lins raid as disproportionate and poorly coordinated across levels of government. "The association was never a secret in this city. Everyone knew what we did," she told the outlet Cannalize, adding that the timing felt particularly contradictory because "the Union itself is asking for more time for a fairer regulation, one that includes the associations."

## What Anvisa approved, and why it arrives too late for some

That request for more time has a case file attached to it. Since 2024, Brazil's Superior Court of Justice (STJ) — the country's highest court for non-constitutional matters — has been ordering the federal government to regulate medicinal cannabis cultivation. In November 2025, the STJ itself extended the deadline to March 31, 2026, also requiring Anvisa and the federal government to report compliance with each intermediate stage of the timeline within five days of completing it.

Anvisa made the deadline. On January 28, 2026, its board unanimously approved three resolutions: one governs industrial pharmaceutical cultivation limited to CBD or THC at or below 0.3%; a second creates a five-year regulatory "sandbox" — RDC 1.014/2026 — designed specifically for nonprofit patient associations at least two years old, which can apply through a public call to operate under health-authority supervision and generate data Anvisa will use for a future, permanent regulatory decision; a third sets research criteria allowing higher THC concentrations. Much of Brazil's legal press has described it as the first time the health regulator has formally recognized associations as a stakeholder to be integrated rather than an obstacle to be eliminated.

It is worth being precise about what is established fact here and what is advocacy framing. That the STJ ordered cultivation regulated under threat of a court-set deadline, that Anvisa approved the three resolutions on January 28, 2026, that police raided Santa Gaia, Mama Flor, and the ASCAMED-linked properties on the dates described, and that Viel remained in custody after at least four denied habeas corpus petitions as of November 2025 — these are matters of judicial and police record, documented by Anvisa, the STJ, and local reporting. That these raids amount to excessive criminalization of the right to health — the argument made by legal scholars writing in Conjur, Brazil's leading legal-affairs publication, and by drug-policy reform networks such as the Plataforma Brasileira de Política de Drogas, a coalition of more than 50 health and human-rights organizations — is a serious interpretation grounded in the constitutional right to health, but it remains an argument, not a verdict. None of the three associations had been convicted of anything as of this writing; all face open investigations or proceedings.

## What changes, and what doesn't, for patients waiting now

Anvisa's sandbox is a genuine novelty, but its fine print limits its immediate reach. Only nonprofit associations at least two years old, willing to submit to monitoring plans and input traceability, can apply — and only a capped number of projects will be selected per call. By the regulation's own design, that administrative process can take months to open and up to five years to produce a definitive outcome. In the meantime, none of the three raided associations automatically regained normal operations: Mama Flor is still litigating for the authorization a judge denied it just after the raid, and Santa Gaia depends on state courts reversing its president's detention despite the federal safe-conduct that, in theory, already protects the entity.

For the patients who relied on these three networks — people with treatment-resistant epilepsy, autism, chronic pain, and other conditions without a registered pharmaceutical alternative in Brazil — the distance between the regulation being approved in Brasília and the raid that cuts off supply on a farm outside Lins or in rural Rio de Janeiro is not abstract. It is the difference between having oil next week or not. The question associations, lawyers, and patients' families are raising is not only when Anvisa will finish regulating, but what happens to everyone who falls outside that narrow window while the clock keeps running.
