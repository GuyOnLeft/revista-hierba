In Colombia's Vaupés department, a two-hour walk from the town of Mitú, the Indigenous community of Ceima Cachivera has spent the past four years running a medicinal plant garden born out of a very specific emergency: during the COVID-19 lockdown, reaching a clinic or finding medicine became nearly impossible in one of the most isolated corners of the Amazon. That isolation pushed local traditional authorities to lean back on what they already had. The result is called O'KO PAK'O — "mother of medicine" in the local language — and it has become one of the best-documented examples of how an Amazonian community combines botanical memory, self-organization and, more recently, institutional scientific support.

The garden brings together the Cubeo, Desano, Piratapuyo, Tukano and Yuruti peoples under the Association of Traditional Indigenous Authorities Neighboring Mitú (AATIAM), which groups the Ceima Cachivera, Mituseño Urania, San Roque de Tucunaré and Macaquiño reserves. Eleven growers — eight women and three men — currently cultivate 42 plant species for therapeutic use. Among them is one the community's knowledge-keepers describe as both the most demanding and the most valued: Bûûrö, known in Spanish as "planta puño," or fist plant, for the shape of its bulb.

## A plant science has not finished naming

So far, Bûûrö has been documented only as *Gloxinia sp.* — identified to the genus level, with no published species-level taxonomic determination yet on record. That detail says a great deal about the actual state of scientific knowledge of Amazonian flora: while Ceima Cachivera's oral knowledge has, for generations, precisely described the plant's bulb, its blue-green leaves and its white-and-purple flowers, formal botany is only beginning to give it a complete name.

María Lourdes Uribe Salgado, a traditional knowledge-keeper and president of AATIAM, summarized its use this way: "That Bûûrö cleans out every illness inside the belly," she told Mongabay Latam. The plant is traditionally used against ulcers, stomach pain, food poisoning and diarrhea, following a regimen of daily doses that healers themselves compare, in its discipline, to a Western pharmaceutical prescription. It is precisely that point — the rigor of the protocol, not the promise of a magic cure — that the community's knowledge-keepers emphasize when explaining why Bûûrö is never handed out without guidance. "If you don't take care of it, all the plants die," Uribe Salgado warned, adding that the garden "gladly provides the plants" to anyone who needs the treatment, but always under the supervision of someone who understands how to manage it.

It is worth being explicit here about what exists and what does not: as far as could be confirmed, there are no published phytochemical studies or clinical trials validating the purgative or anti-ulcer properties attributed to Bûûrö. What supports its use is the evidence of generations of traditional clinical practice, transmitted and cross-checked within the community itself — not conventional biomedical evidence. The two forms of knowledge are not interchangeable, and treating them as if they were — claiming that tradition alone "proves" a pharmacological effect — is exactly the romanticism trap this kind of reporting needs to avoid.

## From family garden to bioeconomy infrastructure

What has changed in recent years is the level of technical support. Instituto SINCHI, Colombia's scientific research institute for the Amazon, based in Mitú, backed the formalization of the botanical garden and helped install a small processing facility for non-timber forest products, which is already generating initial income for the community. Raúl Antonio Jaramillo, AATIAM's secretary, described the shift: "With the training we've had, we're learning to innovate; this plant works like a small laboratory where we process and dehydrate aromatic plants to sell as powder."

Products already coming out of that small facility include crackers made from dehydrated cassava starch, tapioca flours and packaged powders of aromatic plants sold commercially. With Bûûrö specifically, the team is still exploring new ways to transform it — syrups, for instance — that could extend the fist plant's use beyond fresh, traditional preparation, though that development remains at an experimental stage rather than stable production.

In parallel, with support from Colombia's Ministry of Science, Ceima Cachivera's knowledge-keepers are preparing a compendium documenting all 42 species in the garden, listing traditional names, scientific names (where they exist) and Spanish common names, along with cultivation and use instructions. The material is intended for schools in the region, as a way of ensuring the knowledge does not depend solely on oral transmission between knowledge-keepers and apprentices, but gets recorded in a format that can also speak to formal education.

## What this garden actually proves, and what it does not

O'KO PAK'O is not, strictly speaking, a case of scientific validation of Amazonian traditional medicine. It is, rather, a case of well-documented community infrastructure, with visible gender leadership — the garden's day-to-day work rests mostly with women — and an institutional bridge, through SINCHI and the Ministry of Science, that is beginning to build the record needed for someone, eventually, to study Bûûrö with the rigor its community use deserves. That record — names, doses, protocols, associated species — is exactly the input currently missing from the scientific literature on this plant.

For Vaupés, the garden already serves a concrete function independent of any future clinical study: it reduces dependence on a health system that, during the 2020 lockdown, proved it could not arrive in time. And for botanical science, it leaves a clear task unfinished: fully identifying a species an Indigenous community has already known, cultivated and dosed with precision for generations.
