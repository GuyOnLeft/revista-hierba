Long before sunrise, in Kichwa communities scattered across Ecuador's Alto Napo province, families gather around a fire to drink a bitter, dark infusion brewed from the leaves of a tree most of the world has never heard of. It is not coffee. It is guayusa (*Ilex guayusa*), and the ceremony built around it — known as Wayusa Upina, or in Kichwa simply "la guayusada" — has structured daily life in the Ecuadorian Amazon for generations, long before anyone in a laboratory in Quito or Boston thought to ask what was actually in the cup.

That question has now been answered with unusual rigor. In July 2025, a team of Ecuadorian researchers led by Paco Noriega at the Universidad Politécnica Salesiana published a comprehensive review in the peer-reviewed journal *Molecules*, cataloguing decades of chemical and pharmacological findings on guayusa. The timing matters. Ecuador supplies an estimated 98% of the world's guayusa, a market now worth millions of dollars and growing — and the same country that holds a near-monopoly on the plant has spent over a decade fighting, mostly unsuccessfully, to stop foreign patents from claiming its properties without the consent or benefit of the Kichwa communities who have used it for centuries. Guayusa sits at the exact intersection this magazine exists to cover: a medicinal plant, a traditional practice, and the unfinished business of who gets to profit from both.

## A Ceremony Before Sunrise

Wayusa Upina is not simply a caffeine habit. According to a description published by Ecuador's Association of Municipalities (AME), in communities like Killuyacu, near Misahuallí, families gather starting around 2 or 3 a.m. — hours before dawn — to drink an infusion prepared by the women of the household from freshly picked guayusa leaves. The early hour is deliberate: it is set aside for "contar y descifrar los sueños," counting and interpreting the night's dreams, which many Amazonian cultures treat as guidance for the day ahead. Elders lead purification rites before the group disperses to farm, fish, or hunt. "This drink was traditional among our ancestors, and it was important because it gave energy," Kichwa community member Manuel Shiguango told AME, describing a practice younger generations increasingly consume without knowing its deeper history.

Among the neighboring Achuar people, the tradition takes a more intense form: a ceremony called "Wayus," in which large volumes of the infusion are drunk deliberately to induce vomiting as a means of bodily purification, according to the *Molecules* review. Read outside its context, that detail risks sounding exotic or extreme. Read inside it, it is closer to what plenty of cultures do with fasting, cold exposure, or sweat lodges: a disciplined, socially sanctioned reset, not a stunt.

## What the Molecules Confirm — and What They Don't

The *Molecules* review is notable less for discovering something new than for assembling, for the first time in one place, what decades of scattered studies actually show. Guayusa leaves contain a substantial dose of caffeine — the review cites 75.7 milligrams per gram of dry matter — along with theobromine, trace theophylline, and a set of triterpenes, chief among them ursolic acid, plus antioxidant compounds including chlorogenic acid, quercetin, and kaempferol.

Laboratory and animal studies cited in the review associate these compounds with several biological effects: antioxidant activity, anti-inflammatory effects tied to nitric oxide suppression, antimicrobial action against bacteria including *Staphylococcus aureus*, and blood-sugar-related effects through activation of the TGR5 receptor by ursolic acid. Toxicity testing found a favorable safety margin in rats, and mutagenicity assays on aqueous extracts came back negative.

What the review does not find is just as important. There are, as of its publication, no clinical trials of guayusa in humans — no studies in pregnant women or children, limited pharmacokinetic data on how the body actually processes its compounds, and inconsistent results across studies depending on extraction method. In other words: the chemistry backing up centuries of Kichwa and Achuar use is real and increasingly well documented, but the leap from "bioactive compound in a petri dish" to "proven medicinal effect in a person" has not been made. That gap is exactly the kind of evidence/opinion/testimony distinction worth naming plainly rather than blurring into marketing copy, as guayusa increasingly appears in the same wellness aisles as matcha and turmeric.

## Ecuador's Green Gold, and Its Old Wound

The commercial story around guayusa has genuine successes to point to. Since the U.S.-based company Runa began working with Kichwa growers in Napo province in the late 2000s, guayusa has gone from a plant almost unknown outside the Amazon to an export reaching more than two dozen countries, grown under Fair Trade certification by an estimated 2,000-plus Kichwa producer families. Crucially, guayusa is typically grown in *chakras* — small, mixed agroforestry plots that mimic the structure of standing forest, interplanting guayusa with cacao, timber species, and fruit trees rather than clearing land for monoculture. In 2022, Ecuador's Ministry of Production helped organize a formal Guayusa Export Promotion Consortium — bringing together six exporting companies with backing from Japan's international cooperation agency — explicitly aimed at winning "Novel Food" recognition in the European Union and a protected Denomination of Origin for *Ilex guayusa*, the kind of status that already benefits products like tequila or Champagne. Annual guayusa exports now exceed 3 million dollars, and the *Molecules* review puts the global market at roughly 9.6 million dollars in 2024, projected to reach 12 million by 2029.

But that growth sits on top of an unresolved wound. In its first formal state report on biopiracy, Ecuador's intellectual property authority — then known as IEPI, now Senadi, working with the national science secretariat Senescyt — documented 128 patents or patent applications derived from 16 endemic Ecuadorian species filed without state authorization, according to reporting compiled by the independent outlet Servindi. Guayusa was named among the affected species. The communities whose knowledge pointed researchers toward the plant's properties in the first place saw none of the resulting commercial or intellectual property benefit. Ecuador's constitution explicitly bans the appropriation of collective and ancestral knowledge, but enforcement against patents filed abroad has proven difficult, and as of the country's National Biodiversity Strategy for 2025–2030, the state is still building out the legal architecture meant to close that gap — this time framing indigenous knowledge rights as a matter of strategic biodiversity policy rather than an afterthought.

## Two Truths, Held Together

None of this makes guayusa's expanding export economy a bad-faith story. Fair Trade certification, agroforestry plots, and a state-backed push for Denomination of Origin status represent a real, if partial, attempt to route benefit back toward the growers rather than only toward importers thousands of kilometers away. But the biopiracy record is a reminder that "the science finally caught up with the Amazon" is a satisfying headline that can obscure a harder question: caught up for whose benefit?

The Kichwa families who still rise at three in the morning to share dreams over a bitter cup of guayusa were never waiting for a peer-reviewed journal to tell them the plant worked. What they are still waiting for, in many cases, is a legal and economic system that treats their knowledge as the origin of the value guayusa now generates — not simply as a colorful footnote to it.
