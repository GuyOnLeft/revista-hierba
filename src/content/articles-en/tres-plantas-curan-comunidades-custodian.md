On March 3, 2026, *Mongabay Latam* published a piece by Astrid Arellano that deserves more attention than it received. Framed around World Wildlife Day, the article profiles three Latin American medicinal plants that are not exotic, not new, not laboratory discoveries: they are plants that diverse communities have been using for centuries, and that survive — or do not — depending on the will of those same communities to keep tending them.

The question the piece raises, without phrasing it this way, is the underlying question of all contemporary Latin American ethnobotany: what does it take for traditional knowledge not to end up converted into a pharmaceutical-industry input, stripped from those who built it?

## Planta puño, in the Vaupés

In the Vaupés Department of the Colombian Amazon, the Cubeo, Desano, Piratapuyo, Tukano, and Yurutí peoples maintain the use of planta puño — a *Gloxinia*, also called *bûûrö* in local language. Its leaves are used for internal cleansings: ulcers, stomach pain, food poisoning, dietary diarrhea. The tradition is not academic. But academic recognition arrived.

The sabedora María Lourdes Uribe Salgado, president of AATIAM (Asociación de Autoridades Tradicionales Indígenas), describes the turn frankly: "We were defenseless, but we realized we possess medicine from our ancestors." That recognition led to the founding, around 2022, of the O'KO PAK'O botanical garden in the Ceima Cachivera community. It is not an exhibition garden. It is an instrument of transmission: the sabedoras are preparing, with support from Colombia's Ministry of Sciences, a compendium of 42 plants with their traditional names, their scientific nomenclature, their Spanish common names, and their uses. That compendium will be distributed in schools. Cultural identity is not preserved by storing it; it is preserved by passing it on.

## Oregano queretano, between carvacrol and NOM-059

In central Mexico — Querétaro, Hidalgo, Coahuila, San Luis Potosí — grows *Lippia origanoides*, oregano queretano. Its small leaves are an adaptation to the semi-desert: they reduce solar exposure and water loss. Its essential oils contain carvacrol, thymol, and p-cymene — compounds with antibacterial, antifungal, and anti-inflammatory properties documented in the scientific literature.

That chemical profile explains the traditional use — respiratory, digestive, dermatological — and at the same time fuels the problem. The plant is not listed in NOM-059-SEMARNAT-2010, the Mexican norm cataloging species at risk. That means its extraction is not formally regulated. It also means illegal harvest does not encounter a clear regulatory framework: in December 2025, Profepa seized more than 500 kilograms of oregano queretano collected outside any sustainable scheme.

The Regional Botanical Garden of Cadereyta de Montes, in Querétaro, works with the plant not as an exhibition object but as an object of applied research. The biologist Beatriz Maruri Aguilar, of the garden, frames it this way: "The botanical garden's thesis is to highlight the bond we have with nature." That bond, in this case, measures between 600 and 2,000 plants per hectare, with biomass of 300 to 1,500 kilos per hectare — figures that matter because they are the baseline against which one can measure whether a harvest is sustainable or whether it is hollowing out the ecosystem.

## Uña de gato, where the bark decides whether the plant lives

*Uncaria tomentosa* — cat's claw — grows in the Peruvian Amazon: Loreto, Ucayali, Madre de Dios, San Martín, Huánuco, Junín. The Asháninka, Shipibo-Conibo, and Yánesha peoples have used it for generations for cicatrization, joint pain, arthritis, rheumatism, gastrointestinal inflammation, and postpartum recovery. Contemporary research has documented, with evidence ranging from low to moderate certainty, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and antioxidant properties.

Pressure on the plant came through the commercial route. Since the 1990s, international demand turned uña de gato into an intensive-extraction product. The historical harvest technique involved "girdling" the bark — an operation that, performed badly, kills the plant. Deforestation, agricultural expansion, cattle ranching, and road infrastructure add additional pressure on wild populations.

The institutional response has come along two paths. The Peruvian Amazon Research Institute (IIAP) studies the ecology, propagation, and regeneration of the species in primary and secondary forests. The Universidad Nacional de San Martín developed in-vitro multiplication techniques that reduce pressure on wild populations. The biologist Isabel Villalba, of Sernanp (the National Service of Protected Natural Areas), summarizes: "Ancestral knowledge and medicinal plants are something truly valuable that we must safeguard." In areas such as Ampiyacu-Apayacu, agroforestry models prioritize harvesting leaves over bark — an apparently minor technical change that, at scale, changes the viability of the plant.

## What three plants share

Three countries, three ecosystems, three peoples. But the structure of the problem is the same in each case, and it is worth naming.

First: the knowledge about these plants was not built by Western science. It was built over centuries by indigenous and rural communities who maintained intergenerational transmission against every force that tried to interrupt it — colonization, religious persecution, academic devaluation, rural migration, the market. When a laboratory "discovers" today the anti-inflammatory properties of uña de gato, it is documenting what Shipibo-Conibo midwives were teaching their apprentices four centuries ago.

Second: legal recognition of traditional knowledge remains, in 2026, a pending matter. Intellectual-property frameworks are designed for individual inventions with date and author. Ethnobotanical knowledge is collective, anonymous, distributed across time and territory. That asymmetry is the terrain on which biopiracy becomes possible: a pharmaceutical company can patent an isolated active principle, while the community that for centuries identified the plant receives nothing.

Third: the institutional experiences that are working — O'KO PAK'O, the Cadereyta Botanical Garden, IIAP's agroforestry models — share a feature. They are not extractive operations disguised as conservation. They are alliances in which the community retains authority over the use and transmission of knowledge, and where science contributes tools — documentation, in-vitro propagation, population management — at the service of that authority, not above it.

## The pharmacy that is still there

It is estimated that between 50,000 and 70,000 plant species worldwide are harvested for medicinal or aromatic purposes, and that more than 20 percent of those used therapeutically are threatened. The number is global and depressing. But the *Mongabay* piece serves as a reminder of something more concrete: in the Peruvian Amazon, in the Querétaro semi-desert, in the Colombian Vaupés, communities are sustaining — with scarce resources, without marketing, without much noise — the pharmacy on which they, and ultimately all of us, depend.

The question is not how to "rescue" that pharmacy. The plants were not lost. The question is whether institutions — states, universities, international legal frameworks — are willing to respect the authority of the communities that have been tending it for centuries.
