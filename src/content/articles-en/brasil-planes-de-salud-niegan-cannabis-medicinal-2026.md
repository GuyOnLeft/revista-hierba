Brazil now has more registered medicinal cannabis patients than any other country in Latin America — more than 873,000 people, according to the 2025 Medicinal Cannabis Yearbook, moving roughly R$970.9 million (about US$175 million) a year through a market that reaches 85% of the country's municipalities. By the numbers, it looks like the region's biggest success story in patient access. But for a growing share of those patients, having a prescription and a legal supply chain is not the same as being able to pay for the medicine. Brazil's Superior Court of Justice (STJ) has ruled that private health insurers are not obligated to cover cannabidiol-based treatment for home use, and the country's Supreme Court (STF) is now weighing a binding, nationwide answer to the question that ruling left open: when, if ever, can a patient force the health system — public or private — to pay.

For a region where medicinal cannabis programs are usually judged on whether they exist on paper, Brazil's experience is a reminder that legalization and access are not the same fight. The law can recognize a treatment while the health system quietly declines to fund it, leaving patients to discover the gap only after a diagnosis, a prescription, and a denial letter.

## A Market That Outgrew Its Safety Net

Brazil's regulatory shift has been real. In January 2026, the health regulator Anvisa approved a new framework authorizing the full domestic production chain for medicinal cannabis, from cultivation to pharmaceutical manufacturing, a step the STJ itself had ordered in a 2024 ruling. Patients now reach cannabis-derived medicine through four channels: direct personal importation (40% of the market), pharmacies (33%), patient associations (25%), and the courts. That last category is the one growing fastest. Health-law specialist Anna Júlia Goulart, quoted in Brazilian outlet Revista Apólice, describes judicial recourse as functioning as an "access guarantee" precisely because the administrative and insurance routes are not designed to get patients treatment on their own. Between August 2024 and July 2025, courts granted 69.5% of injunctions filed over health-insurance denials — a striking rate that reflects less a generous judiciary than an insurance system built to say no by default.

## What the Courts Have Actually Decided

The core of the problem is a 2025 ruling by the STJ's Third Panel, consolidating three appeals (REsp 2.181.464/RJ, REsp 2.182.344/RJ and REsp 2.200.785/SP) under reporting justice Nancy Andrighi. The court drew a sharp line between supervised "home care" — medically administered treatment at home, which insurers must fund — and ordinary self-administered home use of cannabidiol, which they do not have to cover at all. Andrighi's reasoning was procedural as much as medical: Brazil's health-plan law cannot simultaneously exclude an obligation from insurers in one article and then impose it through another. Products without a full Anvisa sanitary registration, or absent from the National Supplementary Health Agency's (ANS) coverage list, fall outside the mandatory minimum — full stop, according to the court, regardless of whether a physician says the patient needs it. Exceptions survive only for oral cancer drugs, supervised home-care regimens, and items the ANS has explicitly listed. For everyone else — the epilepsy patient using CBD oil at home, the person managing chronic pain without hospital supervision — the ruling effectively tells insurers they are free to refuse.

## The Supreme Court's Pending Verdict

That is not the end of the story, because Brazil's constitutional court has not yet spoken. In June 2026 the STF formally recognized "general repercussion" in Tema 1466, a case that asks the Court to set binding, nationwide rules for when patients can obtain cannabis-derived products through the courts — what documentation and clinical evidence should be required, whether federal or state courts and budgets bear responsibility, and how the public system (SUS) fits alongside private insurance. More than 5,000 individual technical opinions on cannabis-derived products are already sitting in Brazil's judicial databases, a volume that itself signals how much of the country's cannabis-patient population is being sorted case by case rather than by a coherent policy. Until the STF rules on the merits — no date has been set — "nothing changes," as one Brazilian legal outlet put it: patients keep relying on the same four unequal channels, with litigation as the backstop for those who can afford a lawyer and the patience for a lawsuit.

## A Two-Tiered System, and Who Pays the Price

The gap between regulatory promise and financial access falls hardest on patients without money or legal know-how. Groups like Apepi (Associação de Apoio à Pesquisa e Pacientes de Cannabis Medicinal), founded and still led by Margarete Brito, have spent more than a decade pushing Anvisa and the courts to widen access — work that, as Brito has said, fills a gap the state itself leaves open. Associations now account for a quarter of how patients actually obtain their medicine, essentially functioning as a parallel, patient-run health infrastructure because neither SUS nor private insurance reliably does the job. That arrangement works only for patients organized enough, or geographically placed well enough, to reach an association — leaving out rural and lower-income patients across a country where regional inequality already shapes who gets timely medical care. The pattern echoes older fights over HIV medication and cancer drugs in Brazil's courts, where judicialização da saúde became the last-resort mechanism for enforcing a constitutional right to health that the delivery system did not honor on its own. Cannabis is simply the newest treatment to run into that same structural fault line, at a moment when the patient population is large enough, and vocal enough, to make the gap impossible to ignore.

The stakes reach beyond Brazil's borders. Argentina's REPROCANN registry, Colombia's new pharmacy-sale rules, and Chile's self-cultivation allowances all wrestle with a version of the same question — whether formal legality actually converts into affordable, dependable access, or whether it just shifts the burden of proof onto patients themselves. Brazil's judicialização da saúde shows what happens when a country legalizes the product but never resolves who pays for it: patients with epilepsy, chronic pain, cancer, and other conditions become plaintiffs by necessity, and access to a legally recognized medicine turns into a function of who can litigate. Whatever the STF eventually rules in Tema 1466 will shape not just Brazilian jurisprudence, but the template other Latin American courts look to as their own medicinal cannabis markets grow faster than their health systems can absorb.
