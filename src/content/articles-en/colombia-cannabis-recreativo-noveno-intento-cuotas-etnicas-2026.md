In Colombia, a constitutional reform bill to regulate adult-use cannabis has just cleared its first hurdle in Congress. Again. And again, no one can say with confidence it will get further than its eight predecessors. On May 12, 2026, the First Commission of the Chamber of Representatives unanimously approved, in first debate, Constitutional Reform Bill 023 of 2025, which would amend Article 49 of the Constitution to permit the purchase, sale, distribution and consumption of cannabis among adults, according to reporting by Infobae, El Planteo and the Cartagena daily El Universal. It is the ninth legislative attempt since 2020. The previous eight were all archived.

The bill's author, Representative Alejandro Ocampo of the Historic Pact and chair of the Chamber's drug policy commission, sums it up with a line he has used in earlier versions of the project: "the only way to steer children away from this issue and fight illegal economies is to move toward regulated adult use," he said, according to the Chamber of Representatives' own legislative news portal. It is a public-policy argument, not a wellness pitch: neither Ocampo nor the bill's text claims cannabis is harmless — the argument is that total prohibition has failed as a strategy to reduce consumption or the power of illegal economies.

## A Reform That Already Came Close

Colombia has spent more than a decade on failed attempts. The closest call was in 2023: that bill cleared seven consecutive debates — a historic first on its own — before sinking in the Senate plenary on June 20 of that year. According to El Tiempo's reconstruction of the vote record, the initiative secured only 47 votes in favor, with 43 against and 16 abstentions, falling seven votes short of the threshold required for a constitutional amendment. Opposition was led by the Democratic Center bloc, while Radical Change reversed earlier support and voted against as a bloc, arguing that certain amendments hadn't been properly considered. Weeks earlier, Senator Karina Espinosa Oliver had led public health-cost objections to the bill — an argument the 2026 version must contend with again, without, so far, specific cost evidence having entered the public debate record.

That history explains why the unanimous approval in May 2026 — with support Congress itself described as cross-partisan, spanning Liberal, Green Alliance and even some Democratic Center lawmakers alongside the Historic Pact — is being greeted cautiously. A constitutional reform in Colombia requires eight debates split across two legislative "rounds": the first four (committee and plenary, in both the Chamber and the Senate) must be completed within a single ordinary legislative session, or the bill is automatically archived when that session ends — and any subsequent attempt has to start over from scratch.

## Ethnic and Peasant Quotas, and the Coca-Substitution Argument

What sets the 2026 version apart from its predecessors is the weight it gives to communities that historically grew cannabis illegally or were caught inside the armed conflict. According to the text reviewed by Infobae and El Planteo, at least 50% of legally distributed cannabis flower would have to come from Indigenous, Afro-Colombian and peasant growers' associations with a prior tradition of cultivation, and 70% of cultivation licenses would be reserved for those communities. The bill also sets a 20% tax on the gross sale price of adult-use cannabis and its derivatives, with part of that revenue earmarked for social programs in rural, conflict-affected territories.

The framing connects to an argument President Gustavo Petro's government has repeated for years: that regulating cannabis could offer a legal economic alternative to communities that currently depend on illicit crops, including coca. "Cannabis can be a product that helps substitute coca crops, which carry more complex effects," then-Interior Minister Luis Fernando Velasco argued during the 2023 debate, in remarks the Interior Ministry still keeps published as part of its case for the policy. Petro himself has publicly asked Congress to "legalize marijuana and take this crop out of the violence," Semana reported. It is a reasonable policy hypothesis — Colombia is one of the continent's largest cannabis producers, and a regulated crop could genuinely open a legal market to communities that lack one today — but it remains exactly that: a policy bet, not a demonstrated outcome. Colombia's coca crop substitution efforts have in practice been slow and produced uneven results, by the government's own accounting, and there is no evidence yet that regulated cannabis will replicate at scale what currently happens in the illegal market.

The bill also includes more conventional safeguards: a total exclusion of minors from production, distribution and consumption; daily possession limits; consumption restrictions near schools and recreational spaces; home cultivation of up to twenty plants for non-commercial use; and cannabis clubs with caps on plant counts and membership. Colombia has permitted personal possession of up to 20 grams and cultivation of up to 20 plants for personal use since 1994; what the reform would change is the possibility of a regulated retail market.

## The Legislative Clock, Again

The ordinary session in which the first debate was approved closed on June 20. By then, the bill needed to clear all four debates of its first legislative round — including the Chamber plenary and two stages in the Senate — to avoid being archived when the session lapsed. Several outlets, including Infobae and El Planteo, had already warned in May that a packed parliamentary calendar might push the remaining debate into the new legislative session that opens on July 20, 2026 — just three days after this piece was published. If that happened — and as of this writing there is no public record that the bill completed its second debate in the Chamber plenary before the recess — any renewed attempt would have to start again from the first debate, formally making it the tenth legislative attempt since 2020.

For Revista Hierba, what matters is not only whether Colombia legalizes recreational cannabis, but what kind of regulated market it builds if it does. The quotas for Indigenous, Afro-Colombian and peasant growers — if they survive intact through a legislative process where such provisions are often the first concessions traded away — would mark a real departure from legal markets elsewhere, where the industry has tended to end up dominated by players with prior capital while problematic use remains largely criminalized among the poorest. That the bill keeps returning to Congress with this framing intact is a sign that Colombia's debate, unlike others in the region, is not only about whether to legalize, but about who gets to benefit.
