From Bogotá to Buenos Aires, the last decade of Latin American cannabis policy has been a story of laws that, however imperfect, eventually turned into pharmacies, prescriptions, and registered patients. Just off the coast of Venezuela, in the twin-island republic of Trinidad and Tobago, the story stalled at the halfway point. Parliament passed a Cannabis Control Act in 2022 to create a licensing authority, regulate medical cannabis, and give doctors a legal way to prescribe it. Four years later, according to the bill's own status page on the Trinidad and Tobago Parliament website, the law has received presidential assent and is still, simply, "awaiting proclamation." It has never been switched on. For a patient with cancer, epilepsy, or chronic pain, that legal technicality is the difference between a pharmacy shelf and nothing at all.

## A law that exists on paper only

Trinidad and Tobago decriminalized simple cannabis possession in December 2019, when then-president Paula-Mae Weekes proclaimed an amendment to the Dangerous Drugs Act, according to background compiled by the Trinidad and Tobago legal information site nathuLAW. Possession of under 30 grams carries no criminal penalty, and households may grow up to four plants without breaking the law. But the same amendment set steep fines for anything beyond that threshold — up to $50,000 for 30 to 60 grams, up to $75,000 above that — and preserved much harsher penalties, including up to five years in prison, for smoking in public or possessing cannabis near a school or while driving, per the same tracker. Decriminalization, in other words, made personal use less risky. It did not make cannabis medicine.

That was supposed to be the job of the Cannabis Control Act, passed by the Senate in May 2022 to establish the Trinidad and Tobago Cannabis Licensing Authority and set out, for the first time, a legal pathway for doctors to prescribe cannabis and for patients to obtain it from a regulated source. Instead, the Act sits in the same limbo it has occupied since assent: on the books, but not the law of the land, because no proclamation has brought it into force. There is no licensing authority operating today, no registered dispensary, and no mechanism for a physician to write a legal cannabis prescription — a gap confirmed by reporting on the country's medical cannabis rollout that describes "no clear legal or regulatory framework to guide the production, prescription, or distribution of cannabis-derived medication," even as personal possession remains formally decriminalized.

## The patients waiting on a proclamation

The clearest public voice on what that gap costs has come from inside the country's cancer-care establishment. Dr. Marisa Nimrod, Chair and CEO of the Caribbean Association for Oncology and Hematology, told the Trinidad Express that the slow rollout of medical cannabis regulation is "holding back potential health gains" for patients who could benefit immediately from plant-based treatment — not only in oncology, but for epilepsy, arthritis, and anxiety. Her argument is not abstract: at least half of Trinidad and Tobago's population lives with some form of non-communicable disease, whether cardiovascular illness, cancer, or chronic lung disease, she said, describing a patient population large enough that a stalled regulation has population-level consequences, not just individual ones.

In practice, a patient in Trinidad who wants to use cannabis medicinally today has three imperfect options, none of them what the 2022 Act promised. They can rely on the decriminalized personal-possession allowance, buying from the same unregulated market that supplies recreational users — cannabis of unknown potency, unknown contaminants, and no dosing guidance, purchased with no legal protection once quantities creep past the decriminalized threshold. They can try to import a cannabis-derived medicine or hemp-derived CBD product from abroad, a route that customs authorities treat inconsistently: hemp-derived CBD under a 0.3 percent THC threshold is generally importable with documentation, but anything drawn from the cannabis plant itself, or any product making a medical claim, can be held or refused entry by the Customs and Excise Division. Or they can simply go without — a real outcome for a population where, Nimrod noted, half already carries a chronic disease that cannabis-based treatment might ease.

## A government that promised farms before it promised patients access

Trinidad and Tobago got a new government in 2025, and cannabis reform was part of its platform — but not, primarily, framed around patients. Prime Minister Kamla Persad-Bissessar campaigned on promoting cannabis cultivation for medicinal and export purposes, promising to enact the long-stalled Cannabis Control Authority Bill and to build a commercial licensing framework with training and incentive programs for small farmers, according to reporting by the Trinidad and Tobago Newsday on her April 2025 remarks. It was an economic pitch: agro-processing, cooperatives, export revenue. It said comparatively little about what a patient with epilepsy or cancer pain would actually be able to walk into a pharmacy and buy, or when.

That framing has continued since she took office. In July 2025, Persad-Bissessar's government moved to raise the legal age for marijuana use from 18 to 25, alongside similar increases for gambling and alcohol, citing "the negative impact of early marijuana use on adolescent brain development," according to coverage of the announcement. It is a defensible public-health position on its own terms, and one grounded in real developmental research — but it is also a policy about restricting access, announced with more urgency and specificity than anything so far offered to the patients Dr. Nimrod represents. A government can simultaneously tighten rules for teenagers and loosen them for the terminally ill; Trinidad and Tobago's, so far, has only done the first.

Meanwhile, the enforcement machinery that predates any of this reform talk keeps running at full strength. In May 2026, Customs and Excise officers at Piarco International Airport seized cannabis valued at nearly six million Trinidad and Tobago dollars from a passenger's baggage, according to a media release from the Ministry of Finance — a reminder that interdiction capacity, unlike medical regulation, has never needed a proclamation to function.

## What the neighbors already built

The contrast sharpens when set against Trinidad and Tobago's own Caribbean neighbors. Jamaica's Cannabis Licensing Authority, created in 2015, had issued more than 90 licenses and over 100 conditional approvals by 2025, according to the Jamaica Information Service, supplying a network of licensed "herb houses" where registered patients can legally buy cannabis with a prescription. Barbados moved more recently but just as concretely: its Medicinal Cannabis Industry Act, passed in 2019, produced Island Therapeutics, the island's first licensed dispensary, which opened in Worthing, Christ Church in June 2025, offering locally grown cannabis dispensed by a pharmacist against a doctor's prescription, according to local reporting on the launch.

Both are smaller economies than Trinidad and Tobago, and neither moved instantly — Barbados took roughly six years from statute to storefront. But both eventually closed the gap between a law's text and a patient's access to medicine. Trinidad and Tobago passed its own enabling legislation in the same general era as Barbados and has, so far, produced no equivalent: no authority, no dispensary, no prescription pathway, four years after assent. Until a proclamation actually brings the Cannabis Control Act into force — something no government has scheduled — patients in Trinidad and Tobago remain exactly where decriminalization left them in 2019: legally permitted to possess cannabis in small amounts, but with no legal way to obtain it as medicine, and no one certain when that will change.
