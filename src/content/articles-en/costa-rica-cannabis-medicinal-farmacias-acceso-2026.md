Costa Rica shipped its first legal export of medical cannabis to Europe in March 2026. A European buyer had already committed to purchasing the entire annual harvest, and the plants grown at Hybrida Farms, a facility in Cartago, had cleared Good Agricultural and Collection Practices certification and full European Union regulatory compliance — crop yields, according to the company, came in 15 to 20 percent above European benchmarks. It was framed, accurately, as a milestone: a small Central American country positioning itself as a medicinal cannabis hub for the region.

What the export story did not mention is that four years after Costa Rica's medical cannabis law took effect, a Costa Rican patient walking into a pharmacy in San José still has a decent chance of being told the product simply is not on the shelf. The law exists. The certifications exist. The buyers in Europe exist. What is still missing, in large part, is a functioning path from that law to the person who needs the medicine.

## A four-year-old law, an empty shelf

Costa Rica's Ley 10113 — the Law on Cannabis for Medicinal and Therapeutic Use and Hemp for Food and Industrial Use — was approved by the Legislative Assembly on March 1, 2022, and signed by then-president Carlos Alvarado. It recognized cannabis as a legitimate treatment for a defined list of conditions: pain and spasticity linked to multiple sclerosis or spinal cord injury; nausea from chemotherapy, radiotherapy or HIV and hepatitis C treatment; appetite loss in palliative cancer and AIDS care; chronic pain tied to cancer or nervous-system disease; severe childhood seizure disorders and epilepsy; and glaucoma resistant to standard therapy.

Passing a law, however, was only the first step. It took until late 2023 for the Ministry of Health to issue the first commercial cultivation licenses. It took until January 2025 for the ministry to formally announce the technical regulation — RTCR 515:2024 — that would actually let a cannabis-based product be registered as a medicine. And it took until June 22, 2025, for that regulation to enter into force, opening a registration window, through the government's Regístrelo platform, for dried flower and pharmaceutical formulations such as tablets, capsules, creams and oral liquids. Only after a product clears that registration can it legally reach a patient — and only through a pharmacy, with a digital medical prescription.

In practice, that meant that even after the regulatory switch was flipped in mid-2025, Costa Rica's largest pharmacy networks were in no hurry to stock anything. Grupo Dökka — which operates the Fischel and La Bomba pharmacy chains, among the most recognized in the country — told the outlet La República that it had made no changes to its commercial offering and was still evaluating the regulation "from a healthcare, logistical and commercial perspective," in the words of its CEO, Eduardo Jiliberto. For a patient with treatment-resistant epilepsy or cancer-related pain, a law that took three years to become a regulation, and a regulation that pharmacies are in no rush to act on, is not meaningfully different from no law at all.

## What patients did before the law existed

The years of delay are not an abstraction; Costa Rican patients spent them building the workarounds that the law was supposed to replace. Years before Ley 10113 passed, a Costa Rican outlet interviewed patients who had been medicating with cannabis for years with no legal supply chain to rely on. Karla Artavia, diagnosed with multiple sclerosis at 28, said the plant helped more with the fever, fatigue and tingling caused by her interferon treatment than the medications she was prescribed for it — but she learned to use it through trial and error, sourcing it from the illicit market, home cultivation and informal suppliers, since no doctor could legally guide her. Mauricio Liscano, living with a degenerative brain-stem disease that has left him in a wheelchair since 2012, later founded the Fundación Costarricense pro Cannabis Medicinal (FUCOCAME) to build a support network for other patients navigating that same legal void. One mother interviewed for the same story described giving her daughter CBD to reduce epileptic seizures while living with the fear that Costa Rica's child-welfare agency, the PANI, could open a case against her if it found out.

That fear — of a father or mother helping a sick child and risking legal trouble for it — is precisely the kind of criminalization Ley 10113 was written to end. The law itself was a genuine advance. The gap between the law and a stocked pharmacy shelf is where the advance has stalled.

## The pharmacy "oligopoly" fight

The most concrete criticism of the current system has come, perhaps unsurprisingly, from the cannabis industry itself — but the substance of the complaint bears directly on patients. The regulation restricts all sales of medicinal cannabis products to licensed pharmacies; no dispensaries, no specialized retail, no alternative channel exists. Roy Thompson, CEO of the hemp and cannabis company MasVerde, has been the most vocal critic, telling the Tico Times that "the current design does not promote the industry. As the law stands, an oligopoly is granted to pharmacies." Thompson and a group of lawyers have said they are preparing a constitutional challenge — an amparo before Costa Rica's Sala Constitucional — arguing that Ley 10113 itself never established pharmacy exclusivity, and that the restriction was added later, at the regulatory level, without legislative backing.

Not everyone in the health sector agrees the model is the problem. Andrés Guzmán, representing the Colegio de Farmacéuticos, defended the pharmacy-only channel, calling the regulation "an opportunity to offer complementary therapies to patients who require them, as long as the established criteria are respected" — arguing that pharmacist oversight is precisely what keeps a psychoactive medicine safe. It is a legitimate public-health argument. But it does not resolve the more basic problem that the pharmacies holding that exclusive authority have, so far, largely declined to use it.

## Built for export, not yet built for patients

In February 2026, Costa Rica's Ministry of Health, Ministry of Agriculture and Livestock, and the Instituto Costarricense sobre Drogas launched a new tool: a fully digital, centralized platform through the Ventanilla Única de Inversión (VUI) to process hemp and cannabis licensing — cultivation, industrialization, import, export and sale — in one place instead of forcing applicants through multiple in-person procedures. Hemp authorizations are free and valid for six years; cannabis licenses run on the same six-year cycle, with a differentiated track meant for small producers. It is, by design, a tool for businesses and growers, not for patients trying to fill a prescription.

That same month, Hybrida Farms' cannabis crop was already headed to Europe, and by March the first shipment had left the country. Costa Rica now has functioning export infrastructure, GACP-certified cultivation, and a streamlined digital licensing system for producers — built, in under a year, once the state and the industry had the incentive to build it. What Costa Rica does not yet have, three and a half years after Ley 10113 was signed, is a reliable way for a patient with intractable epilepsy or a mother once again weighing legal risk to walk into a pharmacy and actually find the medicine on the shelf.

Thompson's amparo, if it moves forward, could force the question of who is allowed to sell medical cannabis in Costa Rica back into the courts. Whether that ends the pharmacy bottleneck or simply relocates it, the country's patients — the people the law was written for — are still, for now, the last link in the chain to benefit from it.
