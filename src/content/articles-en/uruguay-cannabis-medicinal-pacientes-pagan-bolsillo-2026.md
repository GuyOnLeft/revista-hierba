---
title: "Uruguay Exports Medical Cannabis Across Europe — Its Own Patients Pay Out of Pocket"
excerpt: "Uruguay, the world's cannabis legalization pioneer, exported 45 tonnes of cannabis products in 2025 to Switzerland, Portugal and Brazil. At home, patients still have no public coverage because medical cannabis has yet to make it onto the national drug formulary."
tag: "Access"
---

Uruguay was the first country in the world to legalize and regulate cannabis from seed to sale, back in 2013. Thirteen years later, the country's cannabis sector is booming as an export business: in 2025 it shipped 45 tonnes of cannabis products abroad — nearly triple the 2024 figure — worth roughly $2.6 million, with seeds, low-THC flower, oils and isolates landing in Switzerland, the Czech Republic, Portugal, Germany and, increasingly, Brazil. One Uruguayan producer alone reportedly signed a three-year, $130 million supply contract to serve the Brazilian medical market.

None of that reaches most Uruguayan patients. Cannabis-based medicines are still not part of the Formulario Terapéutico de Medicamentos (FTM), the list of drugs covered by the country's National Integrated Health System (SNIS). That means a Uruguayan with chronic pain, multiple sclerosis or refractory epilepsy who wants a ministry-approved cannabis product has to buy it out of pocket, at prices that can run 5,000 to 9,000 pesos a month — while the raw material grown a few hours away is being packed into containers for Geneva and São Paulo.

The gap has become the defining irony of Latin America's oldest legal cannabis market: a regulatory pioneer whose own health system has not caught up with the industry it created.

## Three requests, no formulary

The mechanism that is supposed to fix this already exists. Uruguay's 2019 medical cannabis law (Ley 19.847) created a Comité Técnico Asesor en Cannabis Medicinal y Terapéutico — a technical advisory committee housed at the Ministry of Public Health (MSP) — whose job includes evaluating whether cannabis-based treatments should be added to the FTM. According to reporting picked up by regional outlets this year, the committee has now formally requested inclusion three times. The first two requests were turned down.

MSP officials have not denied that a fix is coming, only that it isn't finished. The head of the ministry's Medicinal Cannabis Program has described a five-year strategic plan whose central goal is folding cannabidiol into the SNIS formulary, but has been explicit that the proposal still needs to clear "a technical and regulatory evaluation" before anything changes for patients. There is no public timeline for when that evaluation will conclude.

In the meantime, coverage is a patchwork. A handful of public institutions — including Pereira Rossell, the country's main pediatric hospital, and the Banco de Previsión Social (BPS) — provide cannabis treatment free to some patients. Two private insurers, Casmu and Asociación Española, run specialized cannabis clinics, but their members still pay part of the cost themselves. Everyone else pays full retail: reporting on the domestic market has put prices for ministry-approved products at roughly 2,000 to 3,000 pesos for a 10 ml bottle and up to 10,000 pesos for 30 ml — a level that pushes many patients toward unregulated products sold without quality control or MSP approval, precisely the outcome a legal, regulated market was supposed to prevent.

Officially, MSP authorization for cannabis-based treatment is currently limited to refractory epilepsy in patients under 18. In practice, physicians say patients are already using it — usually self-funded, sometimes with unapproved products — for Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's-related symptoms and chronic pain, conditions the formal system does not yet recognize as qualifying uses.

## The doctors don't know it either

Cost is only half the barrier. The other is that most of Uruguay's physicians have never been trained to prescribe cannabis at all.

Of the country's roughly 7,300 doctors, only a few dozen have formal training in cannabinoid-based therapy, concentrated mostly around SUEN, the Sociedad Uruguaya de Endocannabinología, founded in 2017 by graduates of Latin America's first medical course on cannabis medicine. Julia Galzerano, an internist who set up Uruguay's first hospital-based cannabis clinic at Casmu and has trained hundreds of doctors since 2013, put it bluntly in comments picked up by regional cannabis press this year: physicians, she said, "have the same biases as the general public" — trained to see cannabis as a drug of abuse rather than a therapeutic tool, even as their patients arrive already taking multiple medications without adequate relief.

The MSP and the Sindicato Médico del Uruguay have tried to close that gap with a free, self-paced course on medicinal cannabis for physicians and dentists, now in its second edition as of July 2026. Demand outstripped supply on the first run: roughly 110 applicants for 50 available spots. Noelia Speranza, a pharmacology specialist who teaches the course, has acknowledged the obvious follow-on problem — most doctors "still aren't using medicinal cannabis extensively," she said, despite products already sitting on pharmacy shelves, because they don't know the pharmacological profiles or what conditions the evidence actually supports.

## What the evidence says

That evidence is not nothing. A 2023 study published in the Revista Médica del Uruguay, cited in recent coverage of the access debate, found that cannabinoid-based treatment for chronic non-cancer pain reduced opioid use among patients by 15.7%, with 8.3% of patients stopping opioids altogether, and cut use of NSAIDs — the anti-inflammatory painkillers linked to long-term kidney and cardiovascular harm — by 23.2%. The strongest clinical evidence globally, and the conditions Uruguayan specialists cite most often, cover refractory epilepsy, chemotherapy-induced nausea, multiple sclerosis-related spasticity, neuropathic pain and chronic pain syndromes such as fibromyalgia and migraine.

None of that evidence base is in dispute inside Uruguay's health institutions. What is missing is the administrative step of writing it into the formulary that determines what the public system actually pays for.

## A pioneer without romance

It would be easy to tell this story as a straightforward success — Uruguay legalized cannabis in 2013, and now it exports tens of millions of dollars in product to European pharmacies and Brazilian clinics. That version is true, but incomplete, and Revista Hierba's own editorial line warns against exactly this kind of romanticism: celebrating a legal framework's international reach while ignoring who it fails to serve at home.

The Uruguayan case is instructive for the rest of Latin America precisely because it shows that legalization and access are not the same policy problem. A country can build a fully licensed, internationally competitive cannabis export industry — with growers, processors and quality control systems robust enough to satisfy Swiss and German buyers — and still leave its own patients negotiating between a public system that hasn't updated its drug list, private insurance that only partially covers treatment, and a black market of unregulated oils that exists because the legal one is out of reach.

The Comité Técnico Asesor's third request to add cannabis medicines to the national formulary is, as of this writing, still pending before the Ministry of Public Health. Whether it succeeds will determine whether Uruguay's much-cited pioneer status extends to the patients who actually need the medicine — or remains, for them, mostly a story other countries tell about Uruguay.
