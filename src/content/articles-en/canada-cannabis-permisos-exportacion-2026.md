---

On July 23, 2026, Health Canada announced a set of administrative changes to cannabis import and export rules. None of them amend a law: they are adjustments to guidance documents and forms, framed as part of the "Red Tape Reduction" initiative the Canadian government launched in 2025. None of this made headlines outside the cannabis trade press. But for a handful of Colombian companies — and, to a lesser extent, Uruguayan and Peruvian ones — that bet a good part of their business model on selling medicinal cannabis into markets like Canada's, every adjustment to import paperwork carries a weight it does not carry for the rest of the industry.

The question worth asking is not whether the change is positive — in a narrow sense, it is — but how much it can actually move the needle for a Latin American export industry carrying problems far deeper than the length of a permit.

## What exactly changes in Canada

The adjustments announced by Health Canada, as reported by The Marijuana Herald and StratCann, include four concrete measures:

- **Import permits double their validity**, from a maximum of six months to twelve months.
- **The validity of Canadian export permits now aligns with that of the import permit** issued by the receiving country's authority, instead of following a separate, potentially unsynchronized calendar.
- **The requirement to list ports of entry and exit is eliminated** from cannabis and industrial hemp import and export permits.
- **Documentation on odour-filtration systems is simplified** for applicants seeking indoor production licenses: it is no longer necessary to demonstrate, in detail, at the time of initial application, the adequacy of those systems. The filtration systems themselves remain mandatory; what changes is the documentary burden that precedes approval.

Health Canada framed these changes as an "early phase" of a broader review of its regulatory guidance, with further adjustments expected as additional forms and processes are examined. It is worth noting that this reform arrives alongside budget and staffing cuts at the agency tied to the end of specific Cannabis Act funding, as reported by StratCann — a detail suggesting the logic behind the changes is as much about internal efficiency as about trade facilitation.

Of the four measures, two are the ones that directly matter to a foreign exporter: the extended validity of import permits and the synchronization of export permits with the receiving country's import permits. The simplified odour documentation, by contrast, mainly benefits domestic Canadian license holders — not sellers shipping in from abroad.

## The export bet Colombia built on markets like Canada

To understand why these details matter, it helps to look at the industry receiving them. Colombia regulated cannabis cultivation for medicinal and scientific purposes starting in 2016, with the expectation — repeated for years by the government and by consultancies like Fedesarrollo — that it would become one of the world's major suppliers of low-cost cannabis to developed markets that could not grow it competitively outdoors. Canada, with its regulatory framework matured since 2018 legalization, was one of the most coveted destinations of that strategy.

The symbolic milestone came when Clever Leaves became the first Colombian company authorized to export cannabis to Canada, with support from the investment firm Northern Swan Holdings in navigating the import authorization process on the Canadian side. Andrés Fajardo, then the company's president, described the achievement as a sign that Colombia could position itself as "a leader in a highly specialized industry, typically dominated by developed nations." Clever Leaves today cultivates in Pesca, Boyacá — with plans to expand to 15 hectares — and processes at a pharmaceutical plant in Tocancipá, Cundinamarca. The government of Gustavo Petro has since expanded the list of countries authorized to receive Colombian plant material and seeds to more than twelve, including Canada, Argentina, Spain, the United States, the United Kingdom, Peru, Uruguay, Switzerland, Thailand, and, since August 2024, North Macedonia.

But the gap between the promise and the actual number is enormous. Fedesarrollo had projected that Colombian cannabis exports would reach around $800 million by 2024. The actual figure for 2023 was $10.8 million; in the first half of 2024, it barely topped $3 million. Nor is that total evenly distributed: Clever Leaves accounted for 63.1% of 2023 exports, while the leading destinations by volume were Brazil (32%), Australia (25%), and Germany (14%). Canada, despite its symbolic relevance as the first developed market to open its doors to Colombia, does not rank among the top three destinations carrying the bulk of the trade.

The gap between licenses granted and actual exports is even more revealing. Colombia has issued more than 6,300 cannabis-related licenses — spanning seeds, cultivation, and derivative manufacturing — but of roughly 3,000 licensees registered with the ICA (the agricultural regulator), only 18 managed to export commercially: less than 1%. The pace of new licensing, moreover, has collapsed: from a peak of 928 licenses granted in 2020 to just 57 in 2023 and 16 through July 2024.

Behind that decline is an additional reason rarely mentioned alongside the export figures: Colombia's domestic market never took off. Fewer than 1,000 patients formally access medicinal cannabis treatment in Colombia, compared with hundreds of thousands in more mature medical markets. Although cannabis was added to the Basic Health Plan in 2022, few insurance providers (EPS) cover it in practice, and an estimated half of medicinal cannabis purchases in the country still occur through the informal market. Sandra Carrillo, president of the Colombian Medical Cannabis Association, described the sector as being "in intensive care"; Julián Wilches of Clever Leaves put it more bluntly: "the industry is in decline." It is precisely that combination — an atrophied local market alongside installed production capacity — that pushed Colombian companies to depend almost entirely on exports to survive.

## What the reform fixes, and what it leaves untouched

Against that backdrop, extending Canada's import permit from six to twelve months carries a concrete, verifiable benefit: it halves the number of renewal filings required for each authorized shipment, which matters particularly for a trade pattern like Colombia's — sporadic shipments handled by very few companies, rather than continuous, high-volume flows.

Synchronizing Canadian export permits with the receiving country's import permits addresses a specific, real bureaucratic problem: until now, it was possible for an export permit to expire at a different moment than the corresponding import permit issued by, say, Colombia's INVIMA or another authority — forcing simultaneous renewals across two separate administrations to avoid losing the shipping window. Removing that mismatch reduces the risk of a shipment getting stranded over a simple calendar misalignment.

There, however, the directly verifiable effects on a Latin American exporter's economics end. None of the four changes lowers the cost of obtaining Good Manufacturing Practice certification, offsets price competition from Canadian producers already supplying much of their own domestic market, or resolves the quality and traceability requirements that explain why only 18 of 3,000 Colombian licensees ever managed to export. Canada is reducing administrative friction; it is not generating new demand, nor correcting the underlying reason Colombia's export promise fell so far short of projection.

## What comes next

Health Canada has already signaled that these changes are an "early phase" and that it will review further guidance and forms in the coming months. For Uruguay and Peru — both included among the countries authorized to receive Colombian plant material, and both with their own still-nascent medicinal export frameworks — future Canadian adjustments will matter in the same limited way: as administrative relief, not as a growth engine.

The real bottleneck of Latin America's export-oriented cannabis industry is not in Ottawa. It sits in domestic markets that never fully materialized, in certification costs that push out smaller producers, and in a global price competition that no adjustment to an import form can alter.
