On October 8, 2025, the island village of Caye Caulker became the only place in Belize where anyone has ever cast a ballot on cannabis. Voters were asked whether the government should build a "responsible, regulated, and taxable" cannabis industry on their six-square-kilometer strip of sand. They said no — decisively, with 678 votes against and 176 in favor, a 79-to-21 margin on a turnout of nearly 59 percent that beat the island's participation in Belize's own general election eight months earlier.

Nine months later, that result is still the closest thing Belize has to a public verdict on cannabis reform. The national bill that would have legalized a regulated adult-use market has sat unsigned since 2022. No medical cannabis program has ever existed in the country. And in the entire arc of this story — a stalled 2022 law, a shelved national referendum, a first-ever local vote in 2025 — patients have not been part of the conversation even once.

## A reform built without patients in it

Belize decriminalized possession of up to 10 grams of cannabis on private property in 2017, alongside removing industrial hemp from its controlled-substances list. Everything past that line remains a crime: cultivation, sale, public consumption, and importation of any quantity, which is treated as trafficking regardless of amount. For patients, that framework has never included an exception. There is no prescription pathway, no registry, no licensed dispensary, and no legal defense available to someone using cannabis for epilepsy, chemotherapy-related nausea, chronic pain, or HIV-related wasting — conditions Belizean health commentary has repeatedly named as sources of unmet demand in a country with no way to meet it lawfully.

The 2022 legislation that came closest to changing that — the Cannabis and Industrial Hemp Control and Licensing Bill — cleared both houses of the National Assembly with a design that, according to legal reviews of its text, including one circulated by the Belize Bar Association, never created a distinct license category for medical or therapeutic cannabis at all. It was built to authorize a recreational retail market, cultivation and processing licenses, and an industrial hemp sector, overseen by a new Cannabis Control Commission. Patients were simply outside its scope, the same way they have been outside every version of this debate since.

## How a law without patients still failed

Even a bill that never mentioned medical use ran into a wall. As soon as it passed, an evangelical coalition — including the National Evangelical Association of Belize, the Belize Association of Evangelical Churches, and the Belize Council of Churches — mobilized congregations to petition for a national referendum under Belize's Referendum Act, arguing voters should be able to override Parliament. They gathered enough signatures to force the question. Tourism and banking interests, wary of reputational and financial risk, added their weight to the opposition.

By July 2022, the government shelved its own referendum, citing an estimated cost of roughly BZ$5 million, and the bill was left "parked" — neither repealed nor brought into force. It remains that way today. The only concession that followed came in 2024, when Belize passed the Criminal Records (Rehabilitation of Offenders) Act, allowing automatic expungement for a first-time simple-possession conviction where the fine did not exceed $1,000. Cultivation and distribution convictions were excluded. It was relief for people already punished under the old rules, not a new door for anyone seeking cannabis as medicine.

Kareem Musa, the minister of home affairs who also oversees the Ministry of New Growth Industries and has long pushed for legalization on economic grounds, said in January 2025 that he hoped the question could reach the ballot during that year's general election. It did not. Belize held its general election in March 2025 with no national cannabis referendum attached, and the ruling People's United Party government has not brought the 2022 bill back to the floor since.

## The only ballot Belize has held

Into that vacuum stepped Ilya Rosado, a village councillor in Caye Caulker, who launched a petition in May 2025 that gathered 365 verified signatures — more than the 25 percent of registered electors required under the Referendum Act to trigger a local vote. His campaign, Legalize It Caye Caulker, argued a regulated cannabis industry could fund drainage, healthcare, and schools on an island whose infrastructure lags its tourism boom, while curbing the smuggling networks that Musa has linked to gang violence spilling over from Mexico.

The cabinet split publicly over whether the vote should even happen. Musa backed it. Tourism minister Anthony Mahler opposed holding a referendum with national implications in a single village. Area representative Andre Perez called it "a form of a test" of national sentiment without committing to a side. Prime Minister John Briceño noted that the Referendum Act itself does not clearly define whether a single village can vote on a matter of national scope — a legal ambiguity the government has still not resolved.

The opposition that mobilized against the measure, a campaign called Hope Not Dope led by church senator Pastor Louis Wade Jr. and several island pastors, argued almost entirely in moral and values-based terms rather than economic or health ones. "This is a moral issue, not a money issue," Wade said. After the island's decisive win for the "no" side — certified by Chief Elections Officer Josephine Tamai — Wade called it a moral victory for the community, while "no" campaign spokesperson Celina Fuentes described the result more simply: "This is the island itself speaking. It is a resounding no." Hope Not Dope has since called for a national referendum, effectively proposing to extend Caye Caulker's rejection to the entire country. In neither campaign's public messaging — for or against — did a patient's need for cannabis as medicine surface as an argument.

## What the rest of the region already has

That absence is what sets Belize apart from its neighbors. Costa Rica's Law 10113 has governed prescribed, pharmacy-dispensed medical cannabis since 2022, with implementing regulations approved last year. Barbados' Medicinal Cannabis Industry Act, in force since 2019, produced the country's first licensed dispensary in June 2025. The Bahamas' 2024 Cannabis Act created parallel medical and Rastafari sacramental licenses, with dispensaries expected by 2027. Jamaica, which has run a medical cannabis licensing system for years, moved in April 2026 to fold traditional ganja growers into that system through new special permits. Each of those frameworks is imperfect and unevenly enforced. None of them is the void that exists in Belize.

For a Belizean living with epilepsy or cancer-related pain, none of that regional company changes the reckoning at home: no prescription is valid, no dispensary is licensed, no registry exists to count how many people are even in that situation. The country has now held exactly one referendum on cannabis, and it was about tourism revenue, drainage projects, and moral conviction. The next one, if it ever comes, has yet to be about them.
