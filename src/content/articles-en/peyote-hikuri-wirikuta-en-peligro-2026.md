In the high desert of San Luis Potosí, in a stretch of scrubland the Wixárika people call Wirikuta, a small gray-green cactus is now legally recognized as being on the edge of disappearing. In April 2025, Mexico's environmental ministry, SEMARNAT, updated NOM-059 — the country's official standard for classifying species at risk — to move peyote (*Lophophora williamsii*) from "subject to special protection" into "in danger of extinction." For a plant that has anchored Wixárika, or Huichol, cosmology, pilgrimage, and medicine for centuries, and that has more recently become a magnet for foreign visitors chasing a mescaline experience, the new legal status lands less as a surprise than as an overdue confirmation of something communities in the region have said for years: the desert is running out of peyote.

How peyote got this designation, and what is happening on the land where it grows, says as much about Mexico's environmental bureaucracy as it does about the pressures of a booming, largely unregulated psychedelic-tourism economy converging on a landscape that, until recently, was protected mainly by its own remoteness.

## The math behind "endangered"

The reclassification did not come from a government initiative. It began in 2021, when a collective called Hablemos de Hikuri, working with the Faculty of Engineering at the Universidad Autónoma de San Luis Potosí (UASLP), submitted a technical risk assessment to SEMARNAT. The team — environmental engineering student Juan Carlos Covarrubias Bautista, UASLP researcher José Luis Flores Flores, and agroecologist Pedro Nájera Quezada — applied Mexico's official extinction-risk evaluation method, known as the MER, the same scoring system used across NOM-059. Peyote scored 2.09, just above the 2.0 threshold that triggers the "in danger of extinction" category, a result driven largely by fragmented, shrinking habitat and wild populations that can no longer regenerate as fast as they are being removed.

Four years after the proposal was filed, SEMARNAT adopted it. Under the new classification, unauthorized extraction is now punishable under Article 420 of the Federal Penal Code — one to nine years in prison, plus fines of 300 to 3,000 units of measurement and update (UMA) — with an explicit carve-out: Wixárika communities may continue collecting peyote for ceremonial use without penalty.

## Spiritual tourism and the poaching problem

That carve-out points to the actual crisis on the ground. According to reporting from regional outlets covering Real de Catorce, the town that serves as gateway to Wirikuta, illegal extraction persists largely because outsiders harvesting peyote falsely claim indigenous ceremonial status to sidestep the ban. The municipal president, Javier Sandoval Torres, told local press that enforcement had cut the practice by only around 15 percent; officers have detained people carrying as many as 20 harvested plants in a single day.

Layered onto that is what several Mexican outlets describe as "turismo espiritual" — informal guides, some with no connection to Wixárika communities, charging foreign visitors as much as 5,000 pesos (roughly 250 dollars) for guided peyote excursions into the desert. Reporting by openDemocracy and other outlets describes both outsiders and, in some cases, Wixárika individuals digging up entire plants, roots included, to sell locally or export illegally to Europe. Because peyote grows extremely slowly — a specimen can take a decade or more to reach a harvestable size — this kind of extraction empties out precisely the older, larger plants that produce the most viable seed, degrading the wild population's genetic health for generations to come.

Community leaders have pressed regional authorities for resources to patrol the area. In April 2025, Wixárika representative Florencio López Carrillo asked San Luis Potosí's state congress for 2 to 3 million pesos to fund vigilance, noting that the patrol vehicles previously used to monitor the desert had broken down years earlier and were never replaced.

## A World Heritage site, still fighting mining

Poaching is not the only pressure converging on Wirikuta. In July 2025, at UNESCO's 47th World Heritage Committee session in Paris, the "Wixárika Route to the Sacred Sites of Wirikuta" — a roughly 500-kilometer pilgrimage corridor spanning Jalisco, Nayarit, Durango, Zacatecas, and San Luis Potosí, with more than twenty sacred sites — was inscribed on the World Heritage List. Mexican officials noted it was the first time a living indigenous tradition in Latin America had received the designation. For the Wixárika, though, the listing functioned less as an ending than as leverage: the Regional Wixárika Council immediately called for the cancellation of all mining concessions inside Wirikuta and for the area's protected status to be elevated from state to federal, declaring, in a phrase that circulated widely in Mexican press, that "Wirikuta no se vende, se ama y se defiende" — Wirikuta is not for sale; it is loved and defended.

That fight reached a partial resolution in March 2026, when a federal district court in San Luis Potosí ruled on an amparo lawsuit the Wixárika had filed back in 2011, canceling 43 mining concessions inside the state's protected natural area and recognizing the community's right to prior consultation over any future permits. But the ruling left 20 concessions held by the Canadian company First Majestic Silver Corp active in a buffer zone just outside the park's formal boundary, and, according to the outlet Fábrica de Periodismo, the court declined to weigh environmental evidence presented at trial about aquifer depletion in the region. The Wixárika Regional Council filed an appeal within weeks, vowing to pursue the case "through all necessary legal instances." Meanwhile, expanding poultry, hog, and vegetable operations across hundreds of hectares in the surrounding Altiplano-Wirikuta region are adding new pressure on the same limited aquifers the desert ecosystem depends on — a threat with none of mining's headline visibility, but comparable stakes for a landscape already stressed by drought and extraction.

## What the science actually says

It would be easy — and, by this magazine's own editorial standards, a mistake — to fold this story into an uncomplicated narrative of ancient plant medicine finally getting its scientific due. That is not quite where the research stands. Mescaline, the alkaloid responsible for peyote's psychoactive effects, is a serotonin 5-HT2A/2C receptor agonist that also acts on dopamine and adrenaline receptors, and a wave of new clinical trials — mostly outside Mexico, concentrated in the United States and Europe — has begun testing it for depression, PTSD, anxiety, and substance-use disorders. Early self-reported and small-cohort results point in a promising direction on several of those measures. But a recent systematic review of the ethnopharmacological and clinical literature on mescaline concluded that the evidence base remains too thin for clinical decision-making, even as its physical side-effect profile — elevated blood pressure, headache, nausea — is already well documented.

That scientific caution sits alongside a very different, and equally serious, kind of knowledge: the Wixárika cosmological framework in which peyote is not primarily a compound to be metabolized but hikuri, encountered through ritual pilgrimage, fasting, and communal preparation that can take months to complete. Writing for Chacruna Latinoamérica, anthropologist Johannes Neurath has described Wixárika practice as transforming pilgrims into hikuritamete — "peyote people" — through a process that has little to do with a pharmacological dose-response curve and everything to do with a relationship sustained across generations. Treating that framework as interchangeable with, or reducible to, biomedical research on mescaline flattens something that took centuries to build. It is exactly that flattening, several of the sources cited here suggest, that helps fuel demand for peyote excursions marketed to tourists as a shortcut to insight.

None of Wirikuta's threats — poaching, unregulated tourism, mining, agribusiness — is new by itself. What has changed is that Mexico's own scientific bureaucracy has now put a number on how close the wild population has come to disappearing, and an international body has put a name on how much cultural weight rides on keeping it there. Whether that translates into enforcement with teeth, adequately funded desert patrols, and mining concessions that actually get canceled — rather than merely relocated to a buffer zone a few kilometers outside a park boundary — is the question the Wixárika Regional Council is now litigating, concession by concession, court date by court date.
