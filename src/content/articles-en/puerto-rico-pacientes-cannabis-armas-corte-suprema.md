excerpt: "As the US Supreme Court ruled the government cannot automatically disarm cannabis users without proving dangerousness, Puerto Rico's Senate weighs a bill that would do exactly that to over 100,000 registered patients."

In San Juan, more than 100,000 people carry a government-issued card that identifies them as medical cannabis patients. For years, many of them have quietly avoided applying for a firearms license, or hidden their treatment from the Puerto Rico Police, because local law treats a medical cannabis card the same way it treats a felony record. On June 18, 2026, the United States Supreme Court ruled unanimously that this logic is unconstitutional at the federal level. Nineteen days later, Puerto Rico's own Senate was still sitting on a bill that would enshrine that exact logic into local law — while the island's police force was, according to a firearms-rights organization, refusing to return weapons already confiscated under it.

The contradiction is not abstract. It is playing out in real time, in a US territory with one of the largest medical cannabis patient registries in the Caribbean and Latin American context, and it captures something essential about how criminalization survives even after the law that justified it collapses.

## A landmark ruling out of Washington

The Supreme Court case, *United States v. Hemani*, began far from Puerto Rico. Ali Danial Hemani was the subject of an FBI search of his family's home in Denton County, Texas, where agents found a Glock 9mm pistol along with marijuana and a small amount of cocaine. Hemani cooperated with investigators, surrendered the firearm, and admitted he used marijuana roughly every other day. More than six months later, federal prosecutors charged him under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(3) — the same Gun Control Act provision that led to Hunter Biden's 2024 conviction — which bars firearm possession by anyone who is "an unlawful user of or addicted to any controlled substance."

On June 18, 2026, the Court sided with Hemani, 9-0. Writing for the majority, Justice Neil Gorsuch rejected the government's core argument, stating that prosecutors were effectively asking the Court "to conclude that anyone who regularly uses marijuana is categorically violent and dangerous" — a claim the Court found unsupported. The 19-page opinion held that the federal government cannot disarm someone based solely on their status as a marijuana user; it must show an individualized risk of dangerous behavior. NPR, SCOTUSblog, CNN, and the ACLU all covered the ruling as a narrow but consequential limit on the federal government's power to strip Second Amendment rights from drug users absent proof of actual danger. Legal analysts at State Court Report and the Reason Foundation noted the ruling's limits: it does not touch bans on felons, addicts, or people intoxicated at the time of possession, and it leaves Congress room to legislate around specific, evidence-based risk categories. What it forecloses is the categorical, status-based presumption that cannabis use alone equals dangerousness.

## Puerto Rico's own bill moves the opposite way

While that precedent was being written into federal law, Puerto Rico's House of Representatives had already approved, on March 19, 2026, a very different approach. House Bill 651, sponsored by Representative Pedro "Pellé" Santiago Guzmán, passed by a 36-12 vote without public hearings, according to reporting by the outlet Revista Crónicas. The bill amends Article 2.02 of the island's 2020 Firearms Law to add medical cannabis license status as an automatic disqualifier for a gun permit. It would also require applicants to submit blood, urine, or hair drug tests within 15 days of applying, impose a 12-month waiting period after any positive result, and grant the Police Department access to the Health Department's medical cannabis registry to cross-check applicants — without, the bill's text specifies, revealing their underlying medical diagnoses.

The measure is now before the Senate, where it has sat since the House vote. José Aleczer Rivera, executive director of G9 Alliance — an organization representing operators in Puerto Rico's medical cannabis industry — has been the most vocal opponent, calling the bill's premise a "dangerous generalized presumption" that treats patients as "addicts or incapable persons, without individual evaluation or scientific basis," according to Metro Puerto Rico and Revista Crónicas. Rivera has pointed out that Puerto Rico's own Health Department, in its evaluation of the bill, acknowledged that medical cannabis patient status "does not imply mental incapacity nor substance abuse" — undercutting the legislation's own stated rationale. He has also argued the bill forces patients to choose "between medical treatment or livelihood," particularly for people in professions that require a firearms license, and has called the measure "a significant setback in public policy" at a moment when other jurisdictions are moving toward evidence-based approaches.

## Hundreds of confiscated weapons, no returns

The contradiction sharpened further after the Hemani ruling, when attention shifted from the pending bill to what Puerto Rico's police were already doing under the old framework. Ariel Torres Meléndez, president of CODEPOLA — the Corporación para la Defensa del Poseedor de Licencia de Armas de Puerto Rico, an organization representing licensed gun owners — publicly asked, according to NotiCel, what the Police Department planned to do about the "hundreds" of firearms licenses it had revoked and weapons it had confiscated from licensed citizens whose only infraction was legal medical cannabis use.

On July 7, 2026, Torres escalated that request directly to the Police, asking the agency to produce a brief accounting of affected patients and begin returning confiscated licenses and firearms in light of Hemani. According to NotiCel's reporting, the Police Department responded that it needed an extended administrative review period — reportedly framed in terms of years — before it could act. Torres characterized that position as "erroneous and unjust," arguing that while some regulatory adjustment is legitimate, it does not require the extensive delays the agency was describing. He also raised a separate concern: the methods Puerto Rico police use to cross-reference medical cannabis license holders against firearms applicants may expose protected health information in ways that resemble a HIPAA violation, since gun licensing personnel would be accessing a health registry never designed for law-enforcement screening.

## Two systems, one population caught between them

What makes the Puerto Rico case notable — and why it belongs in a conversation about patient rights rather than gun politics alone — is the structure of the conflict. This is not a debate between a state seeking public safety and an industry seeking deregulation. It is a case where a federal court has just held that the government must individualize its assessment of risk before it can suspend a fundamental right based on someone's status as a patient, and a local legislature is simultaneously trying to pass a law built on the opposite premise: that being a medical cannabis patient is, by itself, sufficient grounds for suspicion.

Puerto Rico's medical cannabis program has already been shrinking on its own terms. According to a Health Department-commissioned market study cited by local outlets, registered patients fell from a 2022 peak of roughly 123,000 to just over 108,000 by December 2025, even as remaining patients increased their consumption by 53% between 2018 and 2025 as prices dropped 34%. Advocates worry that a law criminalizing patients' access to firearms — a right explicitly protected for the broader population — would push more people to conceal their medical status, avoid registering, or abandon legal treatment altogether rather than accept a state-imposed trade-off between their health and their constitutional rights.

The Senate has not scheduled a floor vote on House Bill 651 as of this writing. Advocates on both sides — G9 Alliance representing patients and the cannabis industry, and CODEPOLA representing gun owners — have separately urged senators to weigh the bill against the Hemani precedent before advancing it further. Whether that precedent from Washington reaches San Juan in time, and whether the Police Department complies with it retroactively for the citizens already affected, remains, as of this month, an open question — and a live test of whether a favorable ruling in a courtroom in Washington actually changes anything for a patient standing in a police station in San Juan.
