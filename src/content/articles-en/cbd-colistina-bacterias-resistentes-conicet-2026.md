Two in five deaths from infections in Latin America are now linked to antimicrobial resistance, according to figures cited by regional health authorities — a burden that falls hardest on public hospital intensive care units, where pathogens like *Klebsiella pneumoniae* and *Acinetobacter baumannii* have learned to shrug off nearly everything in the antibiotic arsenal. In that context, a laboratory finding from Argentina is drawing attention not because it offers a cure, but because it offers a mechanism: a non-psychoactive cannabis compound that, combined with one of medicine's last-resort antibiotics, restores its ability to kill bacteria that had stopped responding to it.

The study, led by researcher Paulo Maffía at the Universidad Nacional de Hurlingham (UNAHUR) and Argentina's National Scientific and Technical Research Council (CONICET), was published in the peer-reviewed journal *Pharmaceutics* in its January 2026 issue. It is an entirely laboratory-based, in vitro investigation — no animals, no patients — and its authors are explicit that any clinical application remains years and several research phases away. But the molecular detail it provides is unusually precise, and it lands squarely inside one of the most urgent problems in global infectious disease: the collapse of options against multidrug-resistant gram-negative bacteria.

## A Molecule That Fails Alone

Cannabidiol, or CBD, has a well-documented antibacterial track record against gram-positive bacteria such as *Staphylococcus aureus* — a body of research that includes a widely cited 2021 study in *Communications Biology* showing it could kill MRSA in lab conditions. But gram-negative bacteria are a different problem. Their outer membrane, an extra lipid layer that gram-positive bacteria lack, blocks most antibiotics — CBD included. On its own, the Argentine team confirmed, CBD shows no meaningful antimicrobial activity against gram-negative species. That is consistent with everything published on the subject before this study.

What the researchers — Merlina Corleto, Matías Garavaglia, Melina Martínez, Melanie Weschenfeller, Santiago Urrea Montes, Martín Arán, Leonardo Pellizza, Diego Faccone and Maffía, working across UNAHUR, CONICET, the Fundación Instituto Leloir and the ANLIS "Dr. Carlos G. Malbrán" national infectious disease institute — set out to test was narrower and more specific: whether CBD could enhance colistin, a decades-old antibiotic reserved for infections that no longer respond to anything else, against colistin-resistant strains.

## What the Lab Actually Found

The team worked with eight clinical bacterial isolates collected from Argentine hospitals — strains of *Escherichia coli*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *Salmonella typhimurium* and *Acinetobacter baumannii* — all confirmed resistant to colistin through two distinct mechanisms: the plasmid-borne *mcr-1* gene, which can spread between bacteria, and chromosomal mutations. Using standard synergy-testing methods (checkerboard assays and time-kill curves), they found that adding CBD, at concentrations as low as 0.5 to 2 micrograms per milliliter, alongside colistin produced a bactericidal effect neither compound achieved alone — cutting bacterial counts by at least 99% within two hours, regardless of which resistance mechanism was at play. The combination also disrupted biofilms, the sticky bacterial communities that make hospital-acquired infections especially hard to clear.

The more striking part of the paper is mechanistic. Using scanning electron microscopy, the team observed that colistin alone caused only mild surface changes to the bacterial membrane, while the CBD-colistin combination caused visible, irreversible structural damage. Then, using nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy at the Instituto Leloir, they showed that CBD and colistin form a direct 1:1 molecular complex — the two molecules physically bind to each other, with a measured dissociation constant of 36 micromolar. When the researchers washed away one compound before adding the other, the synergy disappeared entirely. Both molecules have to be present at the same time, interacting directly, for the effect to occur — evidence against a simple two-step mechanism and in favor of a cooperative, combined attack on the bacterial membrane.

The effect also proved specific: when the team tested CBD alongside two unrelated antibiotics — meropenem and gentamicin, which attack bacteria through entirely different mechanisms — no synergy appeared. Whatever CBD is doing, it appears to be tied specifically to colistin's chemistry, not a generic boost to any drug in a cell culture dish.

## What This Is Not

It would be easy to read headlines about "cannabis defeating superbugs" and walk away with the wrong impression, and the researchers themselves have been careful to avoid that framing. This is preclinical, in vitro science: bacteria in a dish, not infections in a body. There is no evidence yet — because it has not been tested — that this combination works in an animal model, let alone in a hospitalized patient with a bloodstream infection.

The cytotoxicity data underline why caution matters. Tested against human lung cells, CBD became toxic at concentrations of 16 micrograms per milliliter — well above the 0.5-to-2 microgram range where synergy with colistin was observed, but tested in only a single cell line, and far short of the safety profiling any drug candidate would need before human trials. Colistin itself is notorious for kidney and nerve toxicity at the doses currently required to treat resistant infections; the appeal of this finding, if it eventually holds up in living organisms, is the possibility of achieving the same bacterial kill with less colistin — and therefore less harm to the patient. That remains a hypothesis, not a demonstrated clinical benefit.

The authors are also candid about what they don't yet know: they have not fully mapped which molecular regions of CBD and colistin make contact, and their model of "cooperative membrane insertion" is a working hypothesis built on indirect evidence, not a definitively solved structure. Preclinical animal studies, and eventually controlled human trials, would be required before this translates into anything a physician could prescribe — and the authors' own next-step language is measured, describing the hope for "a new therapeutic option" in the future rather than announcing one now.

## Why It Matters for a Region Under Pressure

None of that diminishes why this finding is worth watching from Latin America. The region's public hospitals are on the front line of a resistance crisis that international health bodies have flagged as one of the most severe in the world: pre-pandemic data attributed roughly 141,000 deaths across the Americas directly to antimicrobial resistance, with over half a million more deaths where resistance was a contributing factor. Studies from Brazilian intensive care units have documented carbapenem resistance rates above 90% among *Acinetobacter baumannii* isolates, with fatality rates near 80% in the most severe cases. Colistin is often what's left when everything else has failed — which is exactly why a molecule that could extend its usefulness, even modestly, matters enough to track closely.

It also matters as a story about where this kind of research gets done. The work was carried out at a public Argentine university and funded through CONICET, the country's main public science agency — a reminder that rigorous, internationally published cannabinoid pharmacology is not the exclusive domain of well-funded labs in wealthier countries. The next steps, as the authors themselves note, are preclinical validation and, eventually, clinical trials. Whether this molecular handshake between an old antibiotic and a cannabis compound ever reaches a hospital ward is a question only years of further research can answer.
