More than 2.3 million people live with HIV in Latin America and the Caribbean, according to the Pan American Health Organization, and even when antiretroviral therapy keeps the virus undetectable, a large share of those patients still contend with a quieter, harder-to-treat problem: HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder, or HAND, a spectrum of memory, attention and processing-speed deficits that clinical reviews estimate affects roughly a third of people living with HIV even on effective treatment. It is in this context — where cannabis is already informally used by many patients across Argentina, Brazil, Colombia and Mexico to manage HIV-related nausea, pain, sleep loss and anxiety — that a new laboratory study out of Michigan State University matters. It does not prove cannabis treats HAND. But it is the most precise mechanistic account yet of a biological pathway by which THC and CBD might blunt the brain inflammation thought to drive it.

## What the study actually did

The research, led by toxicologist Norbert Kaminski's team and published in the Journal of Neuroimmune Pharmacology on July 4, 2026, is an in vitro study — cells in laboratory dishes, not patients in a clinical trial. Researchers isolated CD16+ monocytes, an inflammatory subtype of immune cell known to cross the blood-brain barrier, from both HIV-positive and HIV-negative donors, and co-cultured them with astrocytes, the brain's support cells that help regulate neuroinflammation. To simulate the kind of chronic immune activation seen in HIV infection, they stimulated the cells through toll-like receptors 7 and 8, then measured how the monocytes made astrocytes behave, with and without exposure to THC or CBD.

The result, in the authors' own words, was that "THC and CBD impair CD16+ monocyte IL-1β–mediated astrocyte production of IL-6, IL-8, and MCP-1" — three inflammatory signaling molecules central to the neuroinflammatory cascade researchers associate with HAND. Both cannabinoids dialed down that signaling. That alone would be a modest, fairly expected finding in cannabinoid pharmacology, where anti-inflammatory effects are well documented across many cell types.

## Where THC did something CBD did not

The more distinctive result concerned cell movement, not just chemical signaling. Monocytes from HIV-positive donors migrated more aggressively than those from HIV-negative donors — consistent with the idea that these cells are primed to invade the central nervous system and drive neuroinflammation in people living with HIV. THC suppressed that excess migration; CBD, notably, did not. The authors traced THC's effect to changes in actin polymerization — the cytoskeletal machinery cells use to crawl — and to altered expression of integrin receptors, the molecular "grips" monocytes use to adhere to blood vessel walls before crossing into brain tissue. That is a specific, testable mechanism, not a general anti-inflammatory gesture, and it is the kind of granular pharmacological detail that separates a rigorous mechanistic paper from broader "cannabis reduces inflammation" claims that circulate without much specificity.

The Michigan State group is not starting from zero. In 2020, the same lab published a human observational study in the Journal of Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndromes showing that HIV-positive cannabis users had lower levels of circulating activated monocytes and of IP-10, a protein linked to HAND progression, compared with HIV-positive non-users. That earlier work, drawn from blood samples of people, not a randomized trial, could only show association, not cause. The new cell-culture study is an attempt to explain, at the molecular level, why that association might exist — which is a different and narrower kind of evidence, but a necessary complement to it.

## Why the distinction matters for patients in the region

This is where precision about study design becomes a patient-safety issue rather than a pedantic one. Nothing in the new paper demonstrates that smoking, vaping or ingesting cannabis improves memory, attention or daily function in people living with HIV. It demonstrates that two cannabinoids, applied directly to isolated human immune and brain-support cells in a dish, altered inflammatory signaling and, in THC's case, cell migration, under stimulation designed to mimic — but not replicate — what happens inside a living brain. No blood-brain barrier, no drug metabolism, no interaction with antiretrovirals, no years of chronic exposure were part of this experiment. The authors themselves are explicit that the findings "warrant further research" rather than clinical application.

That caution matters acutely in Latin America, where medicinal cannabis access is legally patchier and more improvised than the headlines suggest. Argentina's Law 27,350 created a national patient registry but has left many people relying on self-cultivation or unregulated oil preparations of uncertain cannabinoid content. Brazil's health regulator, Anvisa, has authorized more than 16,000 individual imports of CBD-based products for patients with chronic conditions, a path that is legal but slow and expensive. Colombia has become a licensed exporter of medicinal cannabis to markets including Brazil, Peru and Mexico, even as domestic patient access lags behind export infrastructure. People living with HIV navigating these systems — often older patients managing HAND symptoms alongside years of antiretroviral therapy — deserve accurate information about what a study like this does and does not show, especially because THC and CBD are metabolized through liver pathways (notably CYP3A4 and CYP2C9) that overlap with several antiretrovirals, a drug-interaction question this study did not address and that any patient considering cannabis alongside HIV treatment should raise with a physician.

## A precise clue, not a treatment

What the Michigan State paper offers the field is specificity: a named cell type (CD16+ monocytes), a named brain cell partner (astrocytes), named inflammatory mediators (IL-6, IL-8, MCP-1) and a distinct, THC-specific mechanism (actin polymerization and integrin expression governing chemotaxis) that CBD does not replicate. That is scientifically valuable groundwork for the next stage of research — animal models, and eventually controlled human trials measuring cognitive outcomes in people living with HIV who use cannabinoids under medical supervision. Until those trials exist, the honest reading for patients and clinicians in Latin America is that this is a promising biological lead, not evidence to change how HAND is treated today. Regional cannabis research networks — including CONICET's medicinal-cannabis consortium in Argentina and university programs in Brazil and Chile — are increasingly positioned to test exactly this kind of mechanistic lead locally, in cohorts of Latin American patients living with HIV, rather than waiting for findings generated abroad to be extrapolated wholesale.
