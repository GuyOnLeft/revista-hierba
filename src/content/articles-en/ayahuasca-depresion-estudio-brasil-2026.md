title: "Brazilian Study of 280 Patients Tracks Sustained Depression Relief After Ayahuasca Rituals"
excerpt: "A 280-patient observational study in São Paulo recorded a 43% drop in depression scores after ayahuasca ceremonies. Lacking a control group, the strongest evidence remains a London trial using synthesized DMT."
tag: "Mental Health"
---

For six months, researchers in northwestern São Paulo state followed 280 adults through a ceremony most of Latin America still associates with tourism circuits or religious sects: a bitter, ochre-colored brew shared in candlelit rooms under the guidance of trained facilitators. What they recorded was a steep, sustained drop in depression scores — the kind of result that, if it held up under stricter scrutiny, would matter well beyond Brazil's borders. It probably will not survive that scrutiny unchanged, and the researchers themselves say as much.

The study, published in Frontiers in Psychiatry on January 29, 2026, was carried out by Gláucio Silva Camargos, Geraldo Magela de Faria Júnior, Marcelo Lourenço and Gerardo Maria de Araújo Filho, working through the Faculty of Medicine of São José do Rio Preto (FAMERP) alongside the UNI Shamanic Institute of São José do Rio Preto (IXUNI), a legally operating ayahuasca ceremonial institute. The trial unfolded in private clinics across Regional Health Division XV, a catchment area of more than two million people in São Paulo state, and was picked up in Brazil by the outlet Tribuna do Sertão in late February.

## A Six-Month Signal, Not a Verdict

The 280 participants — 53% women, average age 42.6 — arrived with a mix of diagnoses: 40.4% (144 people) with major depression, 25.2% (71) with generalized anxiety disorder, and 22% (62) with treatment-resistant depression, the hardest category to move with any intervention. Each received a ceremonial dose of a traditional decoction of Banisteriopsis caapi and Psychotria viridis, mixed in the classic 5:1 ratio, administered in a single 50-milliliter serving with an optional 25-milliliter top-up after two hours if the facilitators judged it necessary. The protocol also wrapped three psychodynamic "integration" sessions around the ceremony, before and after.

Researchers tracked depression severity with the Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) at six points across 180 days. The mean score fell from 43.04 at baseline — a severe range — to 24.56 by day 180, a reduction of roughly 43%. The steepest drops came between days 7 and 14, with effect sizes the authors describe as large (Cohen's d between 1.58 and 2.07). The overall statistical model was strong by conventional thresholds: F(5,140) = 89.25, p &lt; 0.0001, with the effect explaining a large share of the variance (partial η² = 0.761).

Numbers like that invite headlines. They should also invite caution, and the authors do not dodge it.

## What the Numbers Can't Prove

The FAMERP study has no control group, no randomization and no blinding — it measured people before and after an intervention they knew they were receiving, in a setting designed to be meaningful and supportive, without comparing them to anyone who didn't get the ceremony. That design cannot distinguish the pharmacological effect of the brew from the effect of ritual, community, expectation, therapeutic attention or simple regression toward the mean in a population selected because it was suffering acutely. The researchers also never quantified DMT or other alkaloid concentrations in the brews used — a basic pharmacological control missing from the protocol — collected no biomarkers or neuroimaging, and drew from a regionally and culturally homogeneous sample that limits how far the findings travel. Notably, the authors' own interpretation leans toward the psychodynamic scaffolding rather than the plant chemistry: they write that the integration sessions and broader clinical support "play a crucial role in treatment effectiveness," a hedge that undercuts any simple story about ayahuasca as a self-contained cure.

None of this makes the study worthless. Large observational cohorts followed for six months are unusual in psychedelic research, where most rigorous data comes from small trials that struggle to fund long follow-up. But observational data of this kind generates hypotheses; it does not test them. That distinction is where nearly all of the ayahuasca hype cycle goes wrong.

## A Sharper Instrument, Built in London

For contrast, consider a trial published just weeks later that was built to answer the causal question the Brazilian study cannot. On February 16, 2026, Nature Medicine published results from a Phase IIa trial run by Imperial College London with Cybin UK and Helus, led by Dr. David Erritzoe. Instead of a ceremonial brew, researchers gave 34 participants with moderate-to-severe treatment-resistant depression — people who had been ill for more than a decade on average and had already failed at least two prior treatments — an intravenous dose of 21.5 milligrams of synthesized DMT, ayahuasca's principal psychoactive alkaloid, delivered over ten minutes with psychotherapeutic support before and after. Crucially, the trial was randomized, double-blind and placebo-controlled.

The results were still notable: MADRS scores dropped 10.8 points more than placebo at one week and 7.4 points more at two weeks, with effects persisting at three months and, for some participants, six months. No serious adverse events were reported, and investigators saw no concerning signals around suicidality. Erritzoe described the finding, in terms reported by Imperial College, as evidence that "a single DMT experience of just around 25 minutes duration is safe, effective and durable, with effects comparable to other promising psychedelic treatments" — though he and his colleagues also flagged a lack of ethnic diversity in the sample as a real limitation.

The London trial isolates one variable — synthesized DMT alone, stripped of the ritual, the community, the ceremonial dosing schedule — and still finds a signal. That is a stronger form of evidence than the São Paulo cohort can offer, precisely because it can rule out the placebo and expectation effects that an uncontrolled study cannot. It is also, notably, not a test of ayahuasca as traditionally prepared and consumed; it tests one molecule, not a plant medicine with dozens of active compounds and a ceremonial context researchers still can't isolate experimentally.

Brazil already has a more modest precedent for controlled work. In a landmark 2014-2017 trial led by Fernanda Palhano-Fontes with teams from UFRN, USP and Unicamp, 29 patients with treatment-resistant depression — 14 given ayahuasca, 15 given placebo — were followed at Hospital Universitário Onofre Lopes in Natal. That study, published in Psychological Medicine, found significant symptom reduction within a single day that held through the seventh day. It was small, but it established ayahuasca depression research as a legitimate academic field inside Brazil, one the FAMERP team is now building on at a much larger, if methodologically looser, scale.

## The Legal Ground Underneath the Data

None of this research exists in a legal vacuum, and Brazil's regulatory posture explains why studies like FAMERP's are even possible. Ayahuasca is not a controlled substance there. Brazil's National Council on Drug Policy (CONAD) permanently removed it from the country's hallucinogen list in 1986 after a government working group reviewed its religious use, and in 2010 CONAD's Resolution No. 1 formally codified protections for ceremonial use grounded in constitutional guarantees of freedom of conscience, belief and worship. That resolution explicitly bars commercial exploitation, "drug tourism" and use outside recognized religious ritual — which means clinical and therapeutic research, like the FAMERP-IXUNI collaboration, operates in a gray zone adjacent to, but legally distinct from, the protections built for churches such as União do Vegetal, Santo Daime and Barquinha.

For patients and clinicians across Latin America watching this research, the honest summary is unglamorous: a large observational study points toward something worth investigating further, a small but rigorous London trial of an isolated compound offers firmer if narrower evidence, and a foundational Brazilian RCT from 2019 still anchors the field. None of the three, alone or together, establishes ayahuasca — the ceremonial brew, as practiced — as a proven treatment for depression. What exists is a promising, unfinished body of evidence that still needs the thing psychedelic research has struggled hardest to fund and execute: a randomized, controlled, adequately powered trial of the traditional preparation itself.
