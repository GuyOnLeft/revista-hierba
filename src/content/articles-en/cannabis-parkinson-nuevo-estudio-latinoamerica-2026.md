For the roughly one million people living with Parkinson's disease across Latin America — a number expected to climb as the region's population ages faster than almost anywhere else in the world — the gap between what patients already do and what science has actually confirmed is wide. Medical cannabis is legal in some form in Colombia, Mexico, Uruguay, Argentina, Brazil, Peru, Chile, and beyond, and anecdotal reports of relief from tremor-adjacent discomfort, insomnia, and pain circulate widely in patient forums and clinics. What has been missing is data collected under any kind of clinical structure. A newly published study out of Israel does not close that gap, but it is one of the more carefully documented attempts yet to measure what self-administered medical cannabis actually does — and does not do — for the non-motor symptoms that erode Parkinson's patients' quality of life.

## What the Researchers Actually Measured

The study, led by Omer Anis and colleagues at Sheba Medical Center's Movement Disorders Institute and the Technion in Israel, was published online in June 2026 in the peer-reviewed journal *Cannabis and Cannabinoid Research*. It was a single-center, open-label, prospective cohort study — meaning there was no placebo group and no blinding. Sixty-eight patients with Parkinson's disease began self-titrated treatment with cannabis oil extracts or inflorescence products, sourced through Israel's regulated medical cannabis system, at varying THC-to-CBD ratios. Fifty completed the three-month follow-up (mean age 65.6, 68% male); the remaining 18 dropped out, mostly lost to follow-up rather than because of adverse effects.

Researchers tracked outcomes using validated instruments: the Non-Motor Symptoms Scale (NMSS), the King's Parkinson's Disease Pain Scale (KPPS), the Parkinson's Disease Sleep Scale-2 (PDSS-2), the eight-item Parkinson's Disease Questionnaire (PDQ-8) for quality of life, and the International Prostate Symptom Score to capture nighttime urinary frequency, a notoriously disruptive but under-treated symptom in advanced Parkinson's. Israel's medical cannabis system, in place since the early 2010s, is one of the most mature regulated frameworks in the world, with licensed growers, pharmacy dispensing, and physician oversight built into a single pipeline — which is precisely what let this study track dosing and follow-up in a way that would be difficult to replicate in a market where supply chains are less consolidated.

## What the Numbers Show — and What They Don't

After three months, patients who completed the study showed statistically significant improvement on every scale researchers tracked: a 14.5-point drop in total NMSS score (p = 0.001), a 5.9-point improvement on the sleep scale (p &lt; 0.001), an 8.1-point reduction in pain scores (p = 0.004), and a modest but significant 1.5-point gain in quality of life (p = 0.040). Nighttime urinary frequency dropped by a median of half an episode per night (p = 0.016) — a small-sounding number that patients living with fragmented sleep will recognize as meaningful.

Notably, the researchers found no correlation between which cannabinoid ratio a patient used and how much they improved, suggesting either that individual titration matters more than a fixed formula, or that the study was not statistically powered to detect a dose-response pattern. That is an important caveat for any patient or clinician tempted to read this as evidence that a specific product works better than another.

None of this constitutes proof that cannabis caused the improvement. Without a placebo arm, the study cannot rule out expectation effects, natural symptom fluctuation, or the attention that comes with three months of structured monitoring — all of which are well documented to move self-reported symptom scores in Parkinson's research. The authors themselves are explicit about this, writing that their findings are feasibility and safety data first, efficacy signals second, and that randomized controlled trials — with standardized dosing, pharmacokinetic monitoring, and predefined cognitive safety assessments — are the necessary next step. A quarter of enrolled patients did not finish the study, which further limits how confidently the results generalize.

## Testimony, Evidence, and the Space Between

This distinction matters especially for a publication covering patient rights. Latin American Parkinson's patients have, for years, offered testimony that cannabis eases pain, improves sleep, or calms tremor — testimony that deserves to be taken seriously as lived experience, and that has driven much of the regional push to legalize medical access in the first place. But testimony is not the same evidentiary category as a controlled trial, and this study, however methodologically limited, sits closer to the evidence end of that spectrum than a forum post or a single physician's case notes. It gives clinicians a documented, peer-reviewed signal — with real numbers attached to real, validated scales — that self-titrated cannabis is at minimum feasible and reasonably well tolerated in a real-world Parkinson's population, even if it cannot yet say cannabis works better than watching and waiting.

Overstating what an open-label pilot shows would do a disservice to patients who are making real decisions, sometimes at real financial cost, about whether to pursue medical cannabis. Latin American medical cannabis programs vary enormously in what they require before a patient can access product — some involve a straightforward physician authorization, while others carry longer waitlists, higher out-of-pocket costs, or require navigating black-market alternatives when regulated supply is thin or expensive, particularly outside major cities. A study like this one should inform, not settle, those calculations.

## Why This Matters for Latin American Health Systems

No equivalent trial — even an open-label one — has been run with a Latin American Parkinson's cohort. That is a structural gap, not a minor omission. Cannabis chemotypes available through regional medical programs differ from the Israeli products used in this study; dosing norms, prescriber training, and insurance or public-health coverage for medical cannabis vary sharply from Bogotá to Buenos Aires to Brasília. Colombia's physician-authorization model, Argentina's Reprocann self-cultivation registry, and Mexico's slower, prescription-only pathway all regulate access differently, which means a dosing protocol validated in Tel Aviv cannot simply be copied into a Peruvian or Chilean clinic without local testing. Pharmacogenomic and metabolic differences across Latin America's genetically diverse populations are also understudied in cannabinoid research generally, a gap regional universities and public health ministries are only beginning to address.

The Pan American Health Organization has flagged neurodegenerative disease as one of the fastest-growing burdens on Latin American health budgets, driven by population aging that is outpacing the region's healthcare infrastructure. Parkinson's-specific cannabis research grounded in the region's own patient populations, growing conditions, and regulatory realities would do more for those patients than any number of imported single-arm pilot studies, however well-conducted.

For health systems already stretched by an aging population and rising neurodegenerative disease burden, the practical question is not whether patients will keep using cannabis for Parkinson's symptoms — many already do, with or without medical supervision — but whether the region will generate its own clinical evidence to guide dosing, safety monitoring, and public coverage decisions, rather than importing conclusions wholesale from a 50-patient, single-arm study conducted thousands of kilometers away. Until that evidence exists, the most responsible reading of this study is a narrow one: cannabis was tolerated, and self-reported non-motor symptoms improved in an open, uncontrolled setting. Everything beyond that is still, for now, a question rather than an answer.
