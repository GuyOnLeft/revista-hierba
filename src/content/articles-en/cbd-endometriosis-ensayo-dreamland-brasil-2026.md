---
title: "A Rigorous Brazilian Trial Found CBD Doesn't Beat Placebo for Endometriosis Pain — and That's a Useful Result"
excerpt: "The DREAMLAND trial, run by Brazil's most prolific cannabidiol research group, is a reminder that good science sometimes means reporting what doesn't work."
tag: "Science"
---

In Ribeirão Preto, a mid-sized city in inland São Paulo state, sits the research group that first described cannabidiol's calming, antipsychotic-like effects in humans back in the late 1970s and early 1980s. Four decades later, that same group — at the University of São Paulo's Ribeirão Preto Medical School (FMRP-USP) — has just published a result far less flattering to CBD's reputation: a rigorously designed clinical trial found that cannabidiol-enriched oil was no better than placebo at relieving chronic pelvic pain in women with endometriosis. The trial was stopped early, before it reached its planned sample size, because the interim data showed no meaningful benefit and more side effects in the CBD group.

For a magazine covering cannabis research from Latin America, this is not a disappointing story — it's an important one. Endometriosis affects roughly one in ten women of reproductive age worldwide, translates into painful, under-diagnosed, and under-treated chronic illness across the region, and has become one of the conditions most frequently cited by informal CBD marketing as something the compound can fix. A well-run, negative trial from one of the world's most experienced CBD research teams is exactly the kind of evidence patients and regulators need — and exactly the kind that gets less attention than a positive headline.

## A trial designed by the people who know CBD best

The study, known by the acronym DREAMLAND, was conducted entirely at FMRP-USP's Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, coordinated by Dr. Omero Benedicto Poli-Neto and involving Antonio Waldo Zuardi, José Alexandre Crippa, and Jaime Hallak — researchers who, between them, form the core of what several surveys have identified as the single most productive academic group publishing on cannabidiol worldwide. That pedigree matters for how the trial was built: this was not a wellness-industry-funded survey or an open-label pilot, but a single-center, randomized, placebo-controlled trial with participants, clinicians, and outcome assessors all blinded to group assignment — a "triple-blind" design, one of the more rigorous templates available in clinical research.

Researchers enrolled 102 women with surgically confirmed endometriosis who continued to experience significant pelvic pain despite prior surgery and ongoing hormonal therapy — a population with genuinely limited treatment options. Half were randomized to oral CBD-enriched oil, the other half to an identical-looking placebo. Dosing followed a slow escalation: starting at 10 milligrams a day in week one and rising, according to clinical response and tolerability, to a maximum of 150 milligrams a day by week nine, followed by a taper. The trial ran roughly ten weeks per participant, with pain intensity tracked on a standard visual analogue scale alongside quantitative sensory testing, psychiatric symptom measures, and quality-of-life questionnaires.

## What the interim data showed

The findings, published in January 2026 in the journal Cannabis and Cannabinoid Research, were unambiguous in one specific sense: both groups improved substantially, and CBD did not improve outcomes beyond what placebo already delivered. Roughly 40% of women in both the CBD arm and the placebo arm achieved at least a 50% reduction in pain intensity; around 60% in both groups reported at least a 30% improvement. By the end of treatment, mean pain scores were, if anything, numerically slightly lower in the placebo group than in the CBD group, though the difference was not statistically meaningful either way.

Where the two groups did diverge was in side effects. Participants taking CBD reported more mild adverse events than those on placebo — mostly gastrointestinal complaints and perceived changes in body weight — with no serious adverse events in either arm. Some secondary quality-of-life and psychological measures moved in CBD's favor, while certain physical-functioning scores favored placebo instead — a mixed, inconclusive secondary picture consistent with a trial that failed to clear its primary bar.

Because the protocol included a planned interim analysis, the trial's data safety monitors halted recruitment once it became clear that continuing to the full 102-participant target — reached, as it happened, right around that point — was unlikely to change the conclusion: no clinically relevant benefit, and a worse side-effect profile than the inert comparator.

## Reading a negative trial correctly

It would be a mistake to read this result as evidence that cannabinoids have nothing to offer chronic pain patients, or that CBD is useless. What DREAMLAND actually demonstrates is narrower and, in its way, more useful: for this specific population — women with surgically treated, hormone-refractory endometriosis pain — and this specific formulation, dose range, and duration, an oral CBD-enriched oil did not outperform a well-matched placebo. Other cannabinoid formulations, other dosing strategies, or other pain conditions could plausibly perform differently, and nothing here should be extrapolated beyond the trial's own design.

What the result does illustrate, sharply, is the size of the placebo effect in chronic pelvic pain trials — a recurring finding across pain research generally, and one that gets little attention in a wellness marketplace where CBD oils are routinely sold, including across Latin America, with implicit or explicit claims about menstrual and pelvic pain relief. A rigorous trial run by researchers with no commercial incentive to find a positive result, using real dose escalation and real blinding, found that the compound itself was doing no better than an inert oil. That is precisely the kind of finding that self-reported testimonials and open-label studies — the dominant form of "evidence" circulating in informal cannabis markets — are structurally unable to detect.

## Why it matters beyond Brazil

Endometriosis is not a minor issue for the region. Regional estimates put the number of affected women in the millions across Argentina, Colombia, Chile, and beyond, and diagnostic delay across Latin America is frequently reported to stretch toward a decade — a gap during which patients often turn to whatever is accessible, including unregulated CBD products marketed on social media as a fix for menstrual and pelvic pain. A trial of this rigor, conducted by a Brazilian institution with genuine international standing in cannabinoid science, gives regional clinicians, regulators, and patients something they rarely get: locally generated, high-quality evidence about a locally accessible product, rather than a finding imported wholesale from a market with different formulations, different regulatory context, and different patient populations.

It also reinforces something Revista Hierba's own editorial line insists on: preliminary, in-vitro, or testimonial claims about cannabis are not clinical proof, and a compound with real, well-documented pharmacological effects in other conditions — CBD's anticonvulsant activity in specific epilepsy syndromes remains one of the best-established cannabinoid findings in medicine — is not automatically effective for every condition it gets marketed for. The DREAMLAND team's own decades of foundational CBD research make them uniquely credible messengers for that distinction, precisely because they have no institutional stake in cannabis being a universal remedy.

## What comes next

The DREAMLAND investigators have not suggested this closes the door on cannabinoid research for endometriosis; if anything, they argue the opposite — that better-controlled trials, not fewer of them, are what the field needs, and that the size of the placebo response observed here should inform how future endometriosis pain trials, cannabinoid or otherwise, are designed. Other ongoing international trials, including a CBD-focused study beginning recruitment in the United Kingdom in 2026, may add further data. For now, DREAMLAND stands as a rare thing in cannabis science coverage: a rigorously negative result, from a credible source, that tells patients and clinicians something true rather than something they wanted to hear.
