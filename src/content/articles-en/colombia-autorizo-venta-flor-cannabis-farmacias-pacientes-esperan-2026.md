In Bogotá, a patient with a valid prescription for medical cannabis flower still cannot walk into a pharmacy and buy it — nine years after Colombia became one of the first Latin American countries to legalize medicinal cannabis, and nearly nine months after the government decreed that pharmacies could finally sell dried flower over the counter. The gap between the law on paper and the medicine on the shelf has become the defining story of Colombian cannabis policy in 2026, and it says as much about the region's regulatory bottlenecks as it does about cannabis itself.

On October 27, 2025, President Gustavo Petro signed Decreto 1138, which for the first time recognized dried cannabis flower as a "finished product" that licensed pharmacies could dispense under prescription, closing a legal gap that had allowed Colombia to export flower since 2021 while barring its own patients from buying it at home. The decree gave Colombia's food and drug regulator, INVIMA, until late March 2026 — five months — to publish the technical rules needed to actually implement it, according to coverage in El Tiempo and Forbes Colombia. Those rules were supposed to cover eight areas: good manufacturing practices, cannabinoid testing standards, clinical trial protocols, rules for non-psychoactive CBD products, prescription criteria, pharmacy dispensing procedures, labeling limits, and a traceability system linked to the Justice Ministry's seed-to-sale registry known as SEED.

## A Decree That "Arrives Absolutely Late"

The reaction from Colombia's own Congress was skeptical from the start. Representative Juan Carlos Losada, a Liberal Party legislator who has pushed separate legislation to regulate adult-use cannabis, told Infobae the decree "llega absolutamente tarde" — arrives absolutely late — noting it took the government roughly three years just to decide that flower counted as a finished medical product. Losada warned that signing a decree is not the same as guaranteeing access: the technical regulations INVIMA still had to write would determine whether patients could actually walk out of a pharmacy with flower in hand, or whether the promise would stall in bureaucratic limbo.

Losada also flagged a separate equity problem: cannabis-growing communities in northern Cauca, one of the regions where small growers have cultivated the plant for years, were not consulted in drafting the decree and, in his telling, feel shut out of the legal supply chain the government is now trying to formalize.

## The Missing Rulebook

Four months after INVIMA's March deadline passed, the technical regulations patients and pharmacies need have still not been fully issued, based on reporting from the health-policy bulletin Salud y Fármacos, which tracks Colombian drug regulation closely. The outlet noted in May 2026 that "state execution has demonstrated slowness and poor coordination," with observers questioning whether the rules would be finished before the end of the current government's term — a deadline that, under Colombia's single four-year presidential term, falls in early August 2026, matching Petro's original inauguration date.

Part of the problem is structural rather than specific to cannabis. INVIMA reported roughly 12,466 pending regulatory procedures across all product categories as of a May 19, 2026 cutoff — including medicines, biologics and other goods under its watch — according to figures cited by Consultorsalud and other Colombian health-industry outlets. Of that backlog, more than 3,000 filings predate 2024. The agency has since launched a contingency plan and a "regulatory confidence" pilot with peer agencies including Brazil's Anvisa, Mexico's Cofepris and Chile's ISP, aimed at leaning on other countries' prior approvals to speed its own reviews. Cannabis flower dispensing rules are one small piece of that much larger institutional traffic jam — which is precisely the patients' complaint: a medicine tied to a strict harvest and shelf-life clock is being processed on the same overloaded track as everything else INVIMA regulates.

## A Market That Never Bloomed

The stalled rollout lands on top of a medicinal cannabis market that, by most measures, has underperformed for years. Colombia legalized medicinal cannabis under Law 1787 in 2016, and the government at the time projected export revenues approaching $791 million by 2025. The real figures came in far lower: cannabis exports totaled about $10.8 million in 2023, according to an El Tiempo investigation, with a single company, Clever Leaves, accounting for roughly 63 percent of that trade, most of it shipped to Brazil, Australia and Germany.

Licensing tells a similar story of a boom that fizzled. New cultivation and manufacturing license issuance peaked at 928 in 2020 and then collapsed to 57 in 2023 and just 16 in the first seven months of 2024, per the same investigation, even as the cumulative license count across Colombia's agricultural, justice and health regulators topped 6,300. Domestic patient enrollment has not caught up either: El Tiempo counted fewer than 1,000 registered medicinal cannabis patients in Colombia, a fraction of the patient bases reported in countries with far smaller cannabis industries. Although cannabis derivatives were nominally added to Colombia's mandatory health benefits plan in 2022, few insurers actually cover them in practice, so most patients pay out of pocket — a cost barrier layered on top of the supply one.

Dr. Sandra Carrillo, president of the Colombian Medical Cannabis Association, told El Tiempo the situation for patients has "desmejorado" — deteriorated — describing disappearing certified pharmacies and supply disruptions that leave patients unable to find products with the balanced THC-to-CBD ratios their treatments require. That is the backdrop against which Decreto 1138 was sold as a fix: not a new industry subsidy, but a patch for a system that, by the regulator's own numbers, has failed most of the patients it was designed to serve.

## A Regional Contrast

Colombia's stall looks sharper set against a region that is, unevenly, moving in the other direction. In Panama, a country with a far smaller and newer medicinal cannabis framework under a 2021 law, regulators issued patient-access rules in May 2026 and a licensed product — Tilray Medical's CBD oral solution — reached shelves at the Farmacias Arrocha chain by July 9, 2026, per company and trade-press reporting. Brazil's health regulator, Anvisa, has also moved in 2026 to let patient associations cultivate cannabis directly. None of these frameworks is free of its own access and equity problems, and a single branded product landing in one pharmacy chain is a modest step, not proof of a functioning market. But the contrast underscores what Losada and Carrillo are both pointing to: in cannabis policy, the distance between a government announcement and a patient's actual ability to fill a prescription is where Latin America's reform wave keeps stalling. Colombia wrote the rule nine years ago and amended it nine months ago; whether a patient can use it is still, as of this writing, an open question.
