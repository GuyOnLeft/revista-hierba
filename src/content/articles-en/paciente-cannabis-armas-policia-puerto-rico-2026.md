---
title: "A Supreme Court Win for Cannabis Patients Runs Into Puerto Rico's Police"
excerpt: "The U.S. Supreme Court ruled that medical cannabis use alone cannot strip patients of gun rights. Puerto Rico's police are stalling on the ruling anyway, while lawmakers push a bill to codify the very exclusion the court struck down."
tag: "Gun rights"
---

In Puerto Rico, being a registered medical cannabis patient can still cost you a constitutional right that has nothing to do with your treatment. For years, islanders enrolled in the government's medicinal cannabis program have had firearm licenses denied, suspended, or revoked, and in some cases had their weapons physically seized, on the strength of a single fact in a state database: that they hold a valid cannabis card. On June 18, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled unanimously that this kind of blanket exclusion is unconstitutional. Three weeks later, the Puerto Rico Police Department has told patients, in effect, to keep waiting. The episode is small in scale but large in what it reveals: a patient registry built to protect access to medicine can just as easily become a list used to restrict rights, and that risk is not unique to Puerto Rico — it is a warning for every Latin American country now building similar rolls of cannabis patients.

## A ruling meant to settle the question, and a police force that will not apply it

The case, *United States v. Hemani*, involved Ali Danial Hemani, a Texas man prosecuted under the federal Gun Control Act for owning a legally purchased firearm while acknowledging regular marijuana use. Writing for a 9-0 court, Justice Neil Gorsuch held that habitual cannabis use alone is not enough to strip someone of Second Amendment rights absent evidence of intoxication, addiction, violence, or actual danger, according to reporting by NPR and SCOTUSblog and the Court's own opinion. The ruling was explicitly narrow — it does not touch laws targeting people who are actively intoxicated or who have a documented history of violence — but its logic landed squarely on Puerto Rico's medical cannabis program, whose tens of thousands of registered patients had long been treated by local police as presumptively disqualified from gun ownership simply for being on the list.

Instead of moving to correct that policy, the Puerto Rico Police Department signaled it would sit on the ruling. Police Superintendent Joseph González Falcón acknowledged the decision but said the agency would not apply it automatically, according to Metro Puerto Rico. Colonel Manuel De Jesús Treskow told reporters the department "must wait to change laws and regulations to establish that which you are asking about," and Legal Division Director Pedro Torres said a "thorough legal analysis" was needed before any policy shift, with the superintendent expected to weigh in "soon" — an open-ended timeline that patient advocates say functions as a denial in practice. As of the department's most recent public statements, no confiscated weapons or suspended licenses had been returned.

## Advocates push back — and lawmakers push the other way

Two organizations not normally aligned have found themselves on the same side of this fight. CODEPOLA, the Corporación para la Defensa del Poseedor de Licencia de Armas de Puerto Rico, represents licensed gun owners generally; its president, Ariel Torres Meléndez, told NotiCel and El Vocero that "the Police of Puerto Rico cannot contradict the Supreme Court's decision" and called the agency's delay "wrong and unjust," demanding an inventory of affected patients and a concrete timeline for returning seized firearms and licenses. G9 Alliance, which groups medicinal cannabis industry operators, made the patient-rights argument explicitly: executive director José Aleczer Rivera said cannabis use alone should not automatically deprive citizens of constitutional rights absent evidence of addiction, intoxication, or actual danger, and rejected the idea that a medical cannabis license can serve as a stand-in for a presumption of dangerousness, according to Revista Cronicas. CODEPOLA has also raised a quieter but consequential question: how did police identify medical cannabis patients in the first place in order to flag their gun applications, and did that cross-referencing of a health registry respect federal privacy protections such as HIPAA? Neither the police department nor the Health Department has publicly answered.

Meanwhile, Puerto Rico's legislature is moving in the opposite direction from the Supreme Court. Proyecto de la Cámara 651, which would explicitly amend the island's Firearms Law to bar anyone with an active medical cannabis license from obtaining a gun license, already passed the House of Representatives and is now before the Senate, according to Metro Puerto Rico's June 19 report. CODEPOLA and G9 Alliance both argue that codifying the exact kind of automatic, status-based exclusion the Supreme Court just struck down would be not only bad policy but very likely unconstitutional. The bill's continued advance despite the ruling suggests that political appetite for treating cannabis patients as second-class citizens has outlasted the legal argument for doing so.

## Why a Puerto Rican gun dispute matters for cannabis patients across Latin America

None of this is only about firearms. It is about what happens once a government asks patients to register in order to access medicine, and then repurposes that registry for a wholly different, punitive use. That pattern should sound familiar across the region. Argentina's REPROCANN, Peru's RENPUC, and comparable rolls being built in Colombia and Mexico all ask patients to hand over identifying information — diagnoses, addresses, cultivation details — in exchange for legal protection. The Washington Office on Latin America (WOLA) has documented that even where medicinal cannabis is nominally legal across much of the region, enforcement remains uneven and marginalized patients disproportionately absorb the consequences of ambiguous rules. Puerto Rico shows a mechanism by which that unevenness can be institutionalized: a database created for public health purposes becomes, through simple administrative inertia or legislative opportunism, a tool that a police department or a legislature can use against the very people it was meant to serve.

For Latin American patients and advocates watching from a distance, the lesson is not that registries are inherently dangerous — most functioning medicinal cannabis systems in the region depend on some form of them to grant legal cultivation limits, prescriptions, or dispensary access. The lesson is that registries need firewalls: legal guarantees that patient status cannot be shared with, or weaponized by, law enforcement or unrelated regulatory bodies, and enforceable timelines that prevent agencies from indefinitely slow-walking favorable court rulings. Puerto Rico had, on paper, a decisive Supreme Court victory for patients on June 18. Three weeks on, patients are still waiting for their weapons, their licenses, and an answer to how their private medical registration ended up on a police desk in the first place. That gap — between a right won in court and a right actually restored — is precisely the terrain where patient-rights advocacy in Latin America still has to fight hardest.
