Walk into almost any pharmacy in Mexico City, a wellness shop in Bogotá, or a licensed dispensary in Buenos Aires, and you will find rows of CBD oil droppers, gummies, and capsules promising relief from pain, anxiety, or insomnia. Most of these products deliver somewhere between a few milligrams and 175 milligrams of cannabidiol (CBD) a day. According to a new pharmacology review published this month, that dose range — the one nearly every retail CBD buyer in Latin America is actually taking — shows little consistent evidence of producing any biological effect at all.

The review, titled "Evaluating the pharmacokinetics, efficacy and safety of low-dose cannabidiol," was published online on July 19, 2026, in the British Journal of Clinical Pharmacology, a peer-reviewed journal of the British Pharmacological Society. Its authors — William George Warren, Myles Osborn, Andrew Yates, and Saoirse Elizabeth O'Sullivan — surveyed more than 50 published studies covering CBD doses of up to 2.5 milligrams per kilogram of body weight, or roughly 175 milligrams per day for an average adult. That threshold matters because it is well below the 20 mg/kg dose used in Epidiolex, the only CBD-based medicine approved by regulators worldwide, prescribed for severe childhood epilepsy syndromes like Dravet and Lennox-Gastaut.

## What the Review Actually Found

It is important to be precise about what kind of evidence this is. This is not a new clinical trial and not a formal meta-analysis with pooled statistics — it is a narrative literature review, synthesizing findings from pharmacokinetic studies, healthy-volunteer trials, and randomized controlled trials that already existed in the scientific record. That distinction matters for how much weight to give its conclusions.

Within that literature, the authors report that pharmacokinetic studies consistently show peak and total blood concentrations of CBD at low retail-style doses running five- to one-hundred-fold lower than the concentrations achieved at the approved epilepsy dose. In healthy volunteers, they found little evidence of clear physiological or neurological effects at these low exposures. And across randomized trials in patients, they found no evidence that low-dose CBD improved outcomes in Parkinson's disease, Crohn's disease, chronic pain, fibromyalgia, stress, glaucoma, or psoriasis.

Some partial signals did turn up: one trial found 160 mg improved sleep duration; another found 150 mg CBD combined with a small dose of THC shifted some markers in alcohol use disorder; an 80 mg daily dose showed modest improvement on some multiple sclerosis symptom measures. But the authors note these effects did not reproduce consistently across related outcome measures in the same or similar trials — the kind of inconsistency that, in pharmacology, is treated as a weak or unreliable signal rather than confirmed benefit.

The review also flags something that deserves separate attention: even at low milligram ranges, CBD produced measurable drug-drug interactions when combined with THC, the tricyclic antidepressant amitriptyline, or the opioid hydromorphone. That finding is significant for patients who assume that because a dose is "low" or sold without a prescription, it carries no risk of interacting with medications they already take. No serious adverse events were reported at these low doses, and the review's authors describe CBD as generally well tolerated — but "well tolerated" and "effective" are not the same claim, and the review is explicit that its evidence supports the former far more than the latter. The authors' own summary is blunt: low doses of CBD, they write, "show very little evidence of any biological effect or therapeutic value."

## A Conflict of Interest Worth Naming

Readers deserve to know something the review's abstract does not headline: its lead author, William Warren, is a Principal Scientist at Artelo Biosciences, a Nasdaq-listed pharmaceutical company developing cannabinoid-based drugs. Co-author Saoirse O'Sullivan, a cannabinoid pharmacologist formerly at the University of Nottingham, is Artelo's Vice President of Translational Sciences. Three days after the review appeared, Artelo issued a press release stating that the analysis "reinforced the case" for its own proprietary product, ART12.11 — a cocrystal combining CBD with tetramethylpyrazine that the company says shows improved bioavailability in preclinical studies, and which it is developing precisely to solve the low-exposure problem the review describes.

This does not mean the pharmacokinetic data are wrong. Peer review at a specialty pharmacology journal is a real check, and the review's central point — that retail CBD doses produce blood concentrations far below the approved therapeutic dose — is not new or fringe. A 2023 evidence evaluation by Arnold and colleagues in the journal Clinical and Translational Science, with no declared industry funding, reached a similar conclusion using an overlapping body of literature. European food-safety regulators have separately flagged large uncertainties around low-dose CBD safety and efficacy in their 2026 novel-food reassessment. Spanish science journalists, writing through the CSIC-BBVA science communication program, quoted pharmacology researchers making much the same point about the gap between OTC CBD marketing and clinical evidence.

What the conflict of interest should change is how the finding is used, not whether it is reported. A study funded or staffed by a company that sells an alternative, "improved" formulation has an obvious commercial incentive to conclude that existing retail products underperform. That is a reason for skepticism about how the finding gets marketed — not necessarily a reason to dismiss the pharmacokinetic measurements themselves, which are checkable against independent prior work and broadly line up with it.

## What It Means for Patients in the Region

For Latin American patients, the practical takeaway is narrower than either "CBD works" or "CBD is fake." Regulation of over-the-counter CBD varies sharply across the region — from Mexico's largely unregulated grey market, to Colombia's growing licensed hemp sector, to Argentina's pharmacy-dispensed oils under the REPROCANN program, to Brazil's formally prescription-gated but loosely enforced access — and in nearly all of these settings, products are sold with vague wellness claims and inconsistent labeling of actual CBD content. This review is a reminder that the milligram amounts typically found in those bottles are unlikely, on the best current evidence, to reliably treat pain, anxiety, or sleep problems in the way packaging often implies — and that even those modest doses are not automatically free of interactions with other medications. Patients taking prescription drugs, particularly antidepressants or opioids, should still tell their physician or pharmacist before adding an over-the-counter CBD product, regardless of how small the dose looks on the label.
