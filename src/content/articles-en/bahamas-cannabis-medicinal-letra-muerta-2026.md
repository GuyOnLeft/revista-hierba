On July 2, 2026, police broke into a Carmichael apartment just after 1 a.m. and found 23 grams of hydroponic marijuana in a bedroom. The occupant, a 56-year-old steel worker named Jean Robert Simbert, admitted the drugs were his — he had bought them, he told the court, to smoke with his girlfriend. He pleaded guilty. A magistrate spared him a criminal record, citing his clean history and steady job, but he still had to appear in court, plead to a drug offense, and accept a year of good behavior or a $1,500 fine to avoid it.

Nothing about that scene should have been possible in the Bahamas of 2026. Nearly two years earlier, on July 26, 2024, the country's Parliament passed and the governor-general assented to the Cannabis Act, 2024 — a law that promised to replace criminal prosecution for small-scale possession with a $250 civil fine, to open a formal path to medical cannabis for patients with conditions from epilepsy to cancer, to recognize Rastafari sacramental use, and to stand up a regulator, the Bahamas Cannabis Authority (BCA), to license the whole system. Two years on, none of that exists for the people who actually need it. What exists is the regulator's name on an office door, a licensing website that has been "almost ready" for over a year, and a criminal court docket that looks exactly as it did before the law passed.

For a magazine covering patient rights across Latin America and the Caribbean, the Bahamian case is a clean, well-documented illustration of a pattern this region knows well: a legislature votes to expand access, and then the state — through inertia, underfunding, or simple non-implementation — quietly keeps the old punitive machinery running underneath the new law's promises.

## A Law That Exists Only on Paper

The Cannabis Act, 2024 is not vague about what it intends to do. It decriminalizes possession of up to 30 grams, replacing arrest and prosecution with a fixed $250 penalty. It authorizes cannabis for medical, religious, and scientific purposes, and it creates the BCA to license cultivation, manufacturing, testing, transport, and sale — with a requirement that licensed cannabis businesses be 100% Bahamian-owned. During the 2024 parliamentary debate, the Ministry of Health described the medical provisions as covering patients with chronic pain, epilepsy, multiple sclerosis, cancer, HIV/AIDS, sickle cell disease, PTSD, glaucoma, and several other conditions.

But Bahamian law allows the government to bring a statute into force in pieces, section by section, through ministerial commencement notices in the Gazette — and only the sections establishing the BCA as an institution have ever been activated. The decriminalization threshold, the medical and religious licensing pathways, and the commercial framework all remain legally dormant. Until a commencement order switches them on, the operative law on the street is still the Dangerous Drugs Act, a statute dating to 1929, under which simple possession carries penalties of up to $50,000 or five years on summary conviction, and up to $125,000 or ten years on indictment — with possession "with intent to supply" reaching as high as $500,000 or thirty years.

## The Courts Never Got the Update

The gap between the law on paper and the law in the courtroom has been documented by local reporting for well over a year. The Tribune, one of Nassau's main daily papers, reported in September 2025 that cannabis cases were still going to trial despite the 2024 reform, and BCA chairman Dr. Lynwood Brown attributed part of the confusion to police "awareness" — officers, in effect, still enforcing a decriminalization that had not technically taken legal effect. The country's attorney general, Ryan Pinder, did not respond to questions about the enforcement gap at the time, according to that report.

Ten months later, the pattern has not changed. Court reporting from The Tribune through June and July 2026 shows a steady flow of small-scale cannabis cases moving through Magistrate's Court exactly as they would have before 2024. On June 16, 2026, Rodricka Tamika Minnis, 33, was found with 20 grams of Indian hemp — an amount that would fall under the Cannabis Act's own personal-possession threshold — and was nonetheless charged with possession with intent to supply, fined $300 by Senior Magistrate Raquel Whyms with one month in custody as the alternative. Three days earlier, Devon Brennen, 30, was fined $800 for 2.5 ounces under the same charge, plus six months of probation.

Perhaps the sharpest illustration came from Lynden Pindling International Airport. On July 8, 2026, a 35-year-old American, John Paul Ramano, arrived with 3.2 pounds of marijuana, THC vapes, and psilocybin in his luggage. According to the Tribune's account of the hearing, Ramano told the court he carried a U.S. medical cannabis card and had not realized marijuana was illegal in the Bahamas. Senior Magistrate Whyms fined him $20,000, with ten months in prison as the default. Whatever one makes of his claim of ignorance, the underlying fact is not in dispute: the Bahamas has no mechanism, two years after passing a medical cannabis law, for recognizing any patient's medical use of cannabis — foreign or Bahamian.

## Patients and Believers Left in the Same Limbo

The people the 2024 law was written for are not the tourists who make headlines when they are caught at the airport. They are Bahamian patients with the conditions the Ministry of Health itself listed during the parliamentary debate, and they are Rastafari worshippers whose sacramental use the Act was meant to formally protect for the first time in the country's history. Both groups are described in the government's own planning documents as future users of the same digital licensing platform the BCA has been building — one system meant to process medical prescriptions, religious-use registrations, and commercial licenses side by side.

That platform, according to BCA officials, has been functionally complete for months. But completion of a website is not the same as a functioning right. A patient with, say, refractory epilepsy has no legal Bahamian pathway to a cannabis-based treatment today, in July 2026, despite a law on the books that says she should. A Rastafari elder wishing to register a sacramental exemption has nowhere to file. Meanwhile, anyone actually found with cannabis, patient or not, is still processed through a colonial-era drug law with penalties designed for large-scale trafficking, applied — as the Minnis and Brennen cases show — even to quantities the new law itself considers a matter for a civil fine, not a criminal charge.

## "Measure Twice, Cut Once" — A Promise on Repeat

BCA chairman Dr. Brown has been the government's public face on the delay, and his own statements trace the slippage in real time. On June 2, 2026, he told The Tribune the licensing platform, government website, and application process would launch "before the end of June," possibly sooner, while cautioning that actual cultivation under a regulated system would still take about 18 months — meaning even a successful June launch implied no licensed dispensary product until late 2026 or into 2027 at the earliest. By July 23, 2026, that June deadline had come and gone. Asked why, Brown told the paper only that "sometimes we have to measure two, maybe three times before we cut once," pointing to unresolved internal matters at the Authority rather than any new obstacle.

There is a legitimate case for caution in standing up a new regulatory system — badly designed seed-to-sale tracking or licensing criteria can create their own problems, as patient advocates in Brazil and Chile have learned from associations and cooperatives excluded by paperwork they could not afford. But caution is not the same as absence. The Bahamas has had a law on its books for two years that names specific patients, specific diseases, and a specific religious community as rights-holders, and it has not yet built a single legal door through which any of them can walk. Until it does, the country's actual cannabis policy is not the one Parliament passed in 2024. It is the one still being enforced, case by case, in Magistrate's Court.
