title: "Colombia's Ninth Attempt to Legalize Cannabis Races a Closing Political Window"
excerpt: "For the ninth time since 2020, Colombia's Congress is trying to regulate adult-use cannabis. A bill passed its first hurdle in May — but the president-elect taking office in August has vowed to kill any legalization effort."
tag: "Policy"
---

In a region where Uruguay legalized cannabis for adults back in 2013 and Mexico's top court decriminalized personal use years ago without Congress ever building a market around it, Colombia has spent six years trying, failing, and trying again to become South America's second fully regulated adult-use market. This year the attempt carries a weight the previous eight did not: whatever Congress does next, it is doing it on borrowed time, against a country that just elected a president who has promised to shut the door the moment he takes office.

On May 12, Colombia's Chamber of Representatives cleared its First Committee unanimously — a rare show of cross-party agreement — advancing Draft Law 023 of 2025, a bill that would create a licensed, taxed, ID-checked retail market for adult-use cannabis, according to Infobae and the Chamber's own communications office. It was the ninth attempt at cannabis regulation introduced in Colombian Congress since 2020. The other eight were archived without ever becoming law, most dramatically in December 2023, when a nearly identical constitutional reform fell three votes short of the two-thirds supermajority required in the Senate's final debate, 47 in favor against a 54-vote threshold, according to Infobae's contemporaneous reporting.

## Nine tries, one deadline

Colombian bills that don't clear all required debates within two consecutive legislative sessions are automatically archived under the country's "legislative transit" rule. For Draft Law 023, that meant a hard deadline: pass a second debate in the full Chamber, then two more in the Senate, before the current session closed on June 20, 2026. Miss it, and the process would have to restart from zero in the Congress installed on July 20 — a new, more polarized legislature elected in March, split almost evenly between the governing Pacto Histórico (62 combined seats between both chambers) and the opposition Centro Democrático (45 seats), with traditional parties holding the balance of power, according to results reported by El Tiempo and CNN en Español.

Bill sponsor Alejandro Ocampo, a Pacto Histórico representative who has carried versions of this legislation across multiple sessions, framed the stakes plainly in comments carried by Marijuana Moment and Colombian outlets covering the May vote: "We're going to regulate from seed to finished product. We're going to remove marijuana from the streets so it can only be sold in places where you have to show your ID, where you need a permit." At the time of this writing, Colombian legislative trackers had not yet confirmed whether the bill cleared its remaining Chamber and Senate debates before the session closed — a genuinely open question that, more than any procedural detail, will determine whether this ninth attempt joins the other eight in the archive.

## What the bill would actually change

Unlike Colombia's existing medical cannabis framework — which already permits licensed cultivation and, since a 2025 decree, lets pharmacies dispense cannabis flower with a prescription — Draft Law 023 targets the far larger unregulated recreational market that current law leaves untouched. As approved in committee, per reporting from Marijuana Moment, Infobae and El Colombiano, it would let adults over 18 buy up to 20 grams of flower or 5 grams of concentrates per day from licensed, ID-checking retailers, subject to a 20% sales tax. Home cultivation of up to 20 plants for personal use would be legal, and registered non-profit cannabis clubs of 10 to 350 members could grow up to 200 plants to supply their membership — a model similar to Spain's and Uruguay's cannabis clubs.

The bill's most distinctive provision, and the one its sponsors describe as its social core, ties the legal market directly to Colombia's decades of armed conflict. At least 50% of legally sold cannabis flower would have to come from crops grown by ethnic and peasant associations with a documented growing tradition, and 70% of cultivation licenses would be reserved for those communities — many of them in regions where illicit coca and marijuana cultivation has long substituted for absent state investment. People convicted solely of cannabis-related offenses could request a review of their sentences under a "favorability principle," with release from incarceration possible in some cases, according to the official summary published by the Chamber of Representatives. The proposal also sets strict limits: an absolute ban on sales or supply to anyone under 18, packaging with health warnings covering roughly half the surface in red lettering on a yellow background, and buffer zones — reported variously as 100 to 200 meters, depending on the outlet — around schools, playgrounds, and religious buildings.

It is worth being precise about what this bill is not. It would not, on its own, resolve Colombia's fragmented cannabis landscape, in which medical cultivation, industrial hemp, and a still-thriving illicit market for recreational flower operate under separate, sometimes contradictory rules. Advocates who have watched eight previous attempts collapse caution against describing any single vote as decisive; Congress has approved first and even sixth-stage debates before, only to see the effort die in a later chamber.

## A political clock, not just a parliamentary one

What makes 2026 different from 2023 is not the bill's text — the core provisions are broadly similar to earlier drafts — but the government that will inherit it. On June 21, in one of the tightest presidential runoffs in recent Colombian history, right-wing lawyer Abelardo de la Espriella defeated Iván Cepeda, the Pacto Histórico's chosen successor to outgoing President Gustavo Petro, by a margin of roughly 250,000 votes out of more than 25 million cast — 49.66% to 48.70%, according to the National Electoral Council's certified count reported by El Tiempo and Infobae. De la Espriella takes office on August 7.

Where Cepeda had made cannabis legalization part of his platform and Petro spent his final months in office publicly asking Congress to "legalize marijuana and take this crop out of the hands of violence," as he put it in remarks reported by El Tiempo, de la Espriella has been categorical in the opposite direction. "There will be no room for marijuana legalization in my government," he said in comments circulated by his campaign and reported by Colombian outlets covering the race, calling cannabis "the gateway to other drugs" and describing himself as unequivocally "anti-drugs." His security platform, built around a proposed "Plan Colombia II" with expanded US and Israeli cooperation, calls for the eradication of hundreds of thousands of hectares of coca and the construction of mega-prisons modeled on El Salvador's mass-incarceration system — a security doctrine with little apparent room for a regulated cannabis retail market, let alone the record-expungement provisions in Draft Law 023.

That gives the current legislative window an unusual finality. Even setting aside the procedural deadline, a bill built around social reparation for communities affected by the armed conflict is very unlikely to find a receptive executive after August 7. Whether Congress used the weeks before that transition to push the ninth attempt further than the previous eight remains, as of this writing, the central unanswered question for patients, growers' associations, and the ethnic and peasant communities the bill was written to prioritize.

## Why it matters beyond Colombia

Colombia's cannabis debate is not just a domestic policy fight. As Latin America's most consistent site of drug-war violence over the past four decades, and as a country where illicit cannabis and coca cultivation have often been the only viable livelihood in conflict-affected territories, its choice about whether to regulate — or to double down on eradication — carries weight for how the rest of the region frames the same trade-off. Brazil has spent 2026 building a formal path for patient associations to cultivate medical cannabis; Argentina has spent the year formalizing licenses for propagation material. Colombia, alone among its neighbors, has spent six years trying to build something larger: a legal, taxed, adult-use retail market explicitly designed to move income out of illegal armed groups and into the communities that have paid the highest price for prohibition. Whether the ninth attempt succeeds where eight failed will say as much about Colombia's next four years as it will about cannabis policy itself.
