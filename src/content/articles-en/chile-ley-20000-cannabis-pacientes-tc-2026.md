---
title: "37 Chilean Lawmakers Ask Constitutional Court to Block Law That Could Criminalize Cannabis Patients"
excerpt: "Thirty-seven Chilean deputies are asking the Constitutional Court to annul a Ley 20.000 reform that replaced a quantity-based standard with a subjective one, risking criminal prosecution of medicinal cannabis patients."
tag: "Legislation"
---

Thirty-seven opposition deputies filed a constitutional challenge on April 13, 2026, asking Chile's Tribunal Constitucional to annul a reform to the country's drug law that critics say could turn medicinal cannabis patients into criminal defendants. The filing, submitted at 20:23 local time, targets a measure that took effect on March 23, 2026, and replaced a straightforward, quantity-based test for drug trafficking with a more subjective standard — one that legal advocates warn leaves cannabis users, including those who grow their own plants for medical reasons, exposed to prosecution once reserved for dealers of more dangerous substances.

The case now sits before Chile's highest constitutional body at a moment when the country's judiciary is sending a strikingly different signal: weeks after the deputies filed their challenge, the Corte Suprema issued a ruling narrowing, rather than expanding, what counts as trafficking. The two developments occupy different legal planes — one a pending constitutional review of a law still in force, the other a single case's reclassification — but together they expose a live fault line in how Chile treats people who possess cannabis for personal or medicinal use.

## From Quantity to "Potential Harm"

The reform, advanced through legislative bills known as boletines 15.347-07 and 16.430-07, rewrites the criteria prosecutors use to decide whether someone found with drugs faces personal-use penalties or trafficking-level charges. Under the previous framework, that determination turned largely on an objective measure: how much of a substance a person possessed. The new standard, as outlets including Soft Secrets have detailed, allows authorities to apply trafficking-level penalties to comparatively small quantities if the substance is deemed capable of producing "serious toxic effects or considerable damage to health" — a judgment call rather than a fixed threshold.

For cannabis, which Chile's Instituto de Salud Pública has recognized for medicinal use since 2015, that shift lands in already contested territory. The plant remains classified as "high-risk" under Decreto 867, a designation that predates and sits in tension with its medicinal recognition. Reporting by High Times, framing the reform for English-language readers, noted that health regulators now hold significant discretion over how cannabis's effects are characterized — discretion that did not exist under the old, quantity-driven system. Critics argue that discretion could be used to fold cannabis into the same aggravated-penalty category as synthetic drugs with genuinely different risk profiles.

## The Deputy Who Grows Her Own

The challenge's lead petitioner is Ana María Gazmuri, a deputy from Acción Humanista and a former actress who has spent years advocating for cannabis-policy reform in Congress. Gazmuri has been unusually direct about her own relationship to the plant, stating publicly that she cultivates and consumes cannabis herself. "Cultivo mis plantas para no alimentar las arcas del narcotráfico," she has said — "I grow my own plants so as not to feed the drug-trafficking coffers" — a framing that positions self-cultivation not as a loophole but as a deliberate alternative to the illicit market the law is ostensibly designed to fight.

Gazmuri did not wait until the reform passed to signal her objection. During the March 23 floor vote in the Chamber of Deputies, she formally registered a reserva de constitucionalidad — a procedural reservation that preserved her right to challenge the measure in court after its approval. That reservation became the seed of the April filing, which she now leads alongside a broad, cross-party coalition: fifteen deputies from Frente Amplio, including Emilia Schneider, Matías Fernández, Gonzalo Winter and Jaime Bassa; eight from the Communist Party, among them Boris Barrera and Irací Hassler; three from the Socialist Party, including Emilia Nuyado, Juan Santana and José Antonio Rivas; and additional signatories from the PPD, Partido Liberal, DC and independent benches, according to BioBioChile's account of the filing, which published the full roster of challengers.

## Six Constitutional Guarantees, One Chilling Effect

The petition, confirmed by Diario Constitucional, argues the reform violates six distinct constitutional guarantees: the right to life and physical and psychological integrity, equality before the law, proportionality, the right to privacy, the right to honor, and the inviolability of the home. Underneath the legal architecture sits a plainer claim about consequences. The deputies warn the reform could produce what they call an efecto inhibitorio — a chilling effect — on people who use cannabis for medicinal purposes, potentially driving patients to abandon treatment out of fear of prosecution, with direct harm to their physical and mental health.

"Las personas que son consumidoras... son igualmente perseguidas, detenidas y enjuiciadas," the filing states — people who are consumers are equally prosecuted, detained, and tried — despite existing legal protections meant to shield personal and medical use from criminal treatment. The claim points to a gap advocates have long identified in Chilean drug policy: protections written into law do not reliably translate into protection from police and prosecutorial discretion on the ground. For a self-cultivating patient, that gap can mean a home raid triggered by a tip or routine surveillance, a criminal investigation that damages employment and reputation regardless of whether it ends in conviction, and — for those relying on informal readings of Article 8 personal-cultivation allowances — a newly precarious legal footing for the plants they depend on.

## A Contradicting Signal from the Supreme Court

Even as the constitutional challenge moved forward, Chile's judiciary produced a ruling pulling in the opposite direction. On May 2, 2026, the Corte Suprema overturned a trafficking conviction involving 100.3 grams of cannabis — a single package, with no evidence of transactions with third parties — and reclassified the case as personal consumption. The court substituted the original prison sentence with a fine equivalent to one Unidad Tributaria Mensual, Chile's inflation-indexed tax unit, according to Diario Constitucional's coverage of the ruling and a follow-up report detailing the sentencing substitution. The court's reasoning was narrow but significant: mere possession, absent additional evidence establishing intent to traffic, is not sufficient on its own to sustain a Ley 20.000 trafficking charge.

It is important not to conflate the two developments. The Supreme Court's ruling resolves one case and does not bind future prosecutions the way a Tribunal Constitucional decision would; the legislative reform, by contrast, is already in force nationwide and will remain so unless the TC annuls it. But together they illustrate a system pulling itself in two directions at once — courts narrowing the definition of trafficking in individual cases while Congress, through the March reform, broadened the state's latitude to prosecute smaller quantities as if they were trafficking. Which signal ultimately governs depends on a ruling that, as of this writing, the Tribunal Constitucional has not yet issued, with no confirmed date for a decision.

## What Comes Next

The stakes extend beyond the courtroom. Separate polling cited in earlier coverage of Chile's broader cannabis debate found that roughly 52 percent of Chileans approve of legalizing recreational cannabis consumption, and 82 percent believe the country's drug regulations need to change — figures that suggest the punitive turn embedded in the March reform sits at odds with where public sentiment has been moving. For now, medicinal cannabis patients and self-cultivators in Chile are left in a holding pattern: a law that expanded prosecutorial reach is in effect, a Supreme Court precedent narrowing trafficking definitions exists but binds no one beyond the case it decided, and the constitutional challenge that could resolve the contradiction remains before the Tribunal Constitucional, unscheduled and unresolved.
