In Salta province, in northwestern Argentina, there is a protected area larger than Jamaica. The Reserva Provincial Los Andes covers 14,450 square kilometers — roughly 80 percent of all the protected land the province has. It encompasses high-altitude salt flats, lagoons that shelter three species of flamingo, and archaeological sites that pre-date the Inca road. In August 2018, the provincial government formally approved a management plan for it, financed by an Inter-American Development Bank loan, with three zones: a strict conservation core, a restricted-use middle ring for traditional pastoralism and low-impact tourism, and a sustainable-use zone where regulated mining could occur.

The plan was published in the official bulletin. It was never implemented.

A March 25, 2026 investigation by Mongabay Latam — reported by Andrés Bermúdez Liévano, Daniela Quintero Díaz, and Emilia Delfino as part of the eight-country "Litio en Conflicto" project coordinated through CLIP — documents what happened next. The zonification was, in the words of officials interviewed for the piece, set aside so that lithium concessions could expand without regard for the conservation core. The salars that the plan had marked as off-limits became indistinguishable, on the ground, from the salars where mining was permitted.

## The flamingos are the indicator

Flavio Moschione, a biologist with Argentina's National Parks Administration, gave Mongabay the numbers. The Andean flamingo (*Phoenicoparrus andinus*) holds Vulnerable status on the IUCN Red List. In Salar de Llullaillaco, a major reproductive colony was abandoned years ago and has not re-established. In Salar de Pastos Grandes, the count this season is eleven nests; the historical baseline was 150 to 200. In Laguna Socompa, the population has fallen below 25 birds — the habitat has degraded enough that even the flamingos that arrive do not stay.

"Lithium extraction is in itself problematic, because it requires enormous quantities of water," Moschione told Mongabay. The CONICET researcher Melisa Argento put it more bluntly: "In lithium mining, what matters most is water." A salar is not a desert. It is a wetland — a slow-circulating brine ecosystem that captures carbon, regulates regional water cycles, and supports endemic life adapted to nothing else. To pump brine out for lithium extraction is to pump the wetland itself out, and to lose what depended on it.

## The companies named, and the ones not

Mongabay names Ganfeng — the Chinese lithium giant — as the operator at Salar de Llullaillaco and Salar de Pastos Grandes. The investigation contacted Juan Gilly, Ganfeng's legal director and president of Salta's Mining Chamber, for response. None was given.

What the investigation does not fully report — and what readers in Salta, Jujuy, and Catamarca should not lose sight of — is that the salars in question are also territory. The Atacameño, Kolla, and Lickanantay communities of the Puna have been organizing against lithium expansion for years, with successful court actions in adjacent jurisdictions: in 2024 the Court of Justice of Catamarca ordered the suspension of new concessions in Salar del Hombre Muerto, citing failure to consult Indigenous peoples under ILO Convention 169. The Reserva Los Andes story is structurally the same story. The legal instruments that should have protected the territory — a management plan with binding zones, free, prior, and informed consent for any project on Indigenous land — were not absent. They were ignored.

## The frame this story belongs to

The 2026 Mongabay investigation is part of a regional pattern that Agencia Tierra Viva has documented across Argentina, Chile, and Bolivia: the "lithium triangle" is being built on a regulatory architecture that, on paper, protects ecosystems and Indigenous rights, and in practice, does not. An anonymous government official quoted in the piece summarized the institutional dynamic with unusual directness: "Within our own governments, our environmental viewpoints are seen as sabotage."

That sentence is the story. Not the absence of law. The presence of law, treated as obstacle.

For Revista Hierba, the question is not whether lithium is needed for the energy transition. It is whether the energy transition is being financed by writing off ecosystems that were already protected, and communities that were already consulted, on paper. The Reserva Provincial Los Andes was protected. The communities were consulted. Eleven flamingo nests this year is what happened next.
