Between February and April 2026, the Panti farming community, in the Pariahuanca district of Peru's Junín region, planted 2,052 *Cinchona pubescens* saplings across four sites on its land. Behind the effort were Peru's National Forestry and Wildlife Service (SERFOR), the Rotary Club Perú Sin Fronteras, and the PAIS program. In forestry terms, it's a modest action — a few hectares, a handful of organizations, one community. In historical terms, it's an attempt to give Peru back a tree that once changed global medicine and is now on the verge of vanishing from its own forests.

That tree is quina, better known internationally as cinchona or Peruvian bark. Its bark yields quinine, the compound that for more than three hundred years was the only known effective treatment for malaria — a disease that still kills hundreds of thousands of people worldwide every year. More than one Peruvian biologist has called it "the greatest contribution the country ever made to global medicine." And yet its wild populations have been in decline for decades, battered by centuries of bark extraction and by the deforestation of the Andean forests where it grows.

## Knowledge That Reached Europe After Centuries of Andean Use

The official history — the one recorded in European books — holds that quina reached Western science in 1638, through the legend of the Countess of Chinchón, wife of the Viceroy of Peru, who was cured of fevers with cinchona bark and is said to have later carried the remedy to Spain. That story gave the genus its scientific name, *Cinchona*, later coined in her honor by Carl Linnaeus.

But that European legend obscures an earlier history: Andean indigenous peoples already knew and used cinchona bark's fever-reducing properties long before any European set foot in the region. Andean ethnobotanical knowledge was not the starting point of a Western scientific discovery — it was appropriated by one. It's a pattern that repeats through much of the history of modern pharmacology, and one rarely told with that clarity in conventional accounts of quinine.

## From Colonial Remedy to Global Drug — With No Return for Its Original Owners

Through the 18th and 19th centuries, European demand for cinchona bark grew so intense that it drove systematic overextraction from Andean forests, especially in Peru, Ecuador and Bolivia. Colonial powers went as far as organizing biopiracy expeditions to smuggle seeds and live saplings out of the Andean region and establish cinchona plantations in their Asian colonies — chiefly British India and the Dutch East Indies — breaking South America's geographic monopoly on quinine production by the late 19th century.

The historical outcome is paradoxical, and it still stings: quinine became an essential drug used worldwide for generations — instrumental, among other things, in sustaining European colonial presence across tropical regions of Africa and Asia — while the tree that originated it, and the Andean knowledge that first identified its properties, were increasingly displaced within their own place of origin. No royalties, no formal recognition, no sustained chain of economic benefit ever flowed back to the Andean communities that had safeguarded that knowledge for centuries.

## A National Tree on the Brink

Today, *Cinchona* faces a new threat — this time existential. Its wild populations in Andean cloud forests, its natural habitat between roughly 1,500 and 3,000 meters of elevation, are heavily fragmented and declining, the product of centuries of unmanaged bark extraction compounded by more recent deforestation from advancing agricultural and grazing frontiers. Although quina was declared Peru's national tree in 1962 — a symbolically significant recognition — that status did not, for decades, come paired with an effective strategy to conserve its wild populations.

Facing that gap, SERFOR has begun building a National Conservation Strategy aimed at restoring 900 hectares of cinchona habitat across different regions of the country. The planting in Pariahuanca, Junín, is one of the first concrete actions under that plan — not a laboratory project or an initiative dictated from Lima, but joint work with a farming community that lives in the territory where the tree still survives.

## A Restoration That Is Also a Reckoning

What sets this effort apart from other forest-conservation projects is that it isn't only about recovering a threatened species for its ecological value — it's about restoring a historical bond between a community and a plant that was, for generations, a source of pride, of homegrown medicinal knowledge and, later, of dispossession. For the Panti community, planting quina isn't only an environmental act; it's also a way of reclaiming stewardship over a tree that world science used for centuries without that use ever translating into direct benefit for the people who first understood its healing value.

The challenge ahead is one of scale: 2,052 saplings across four sites are a start, not a solution. Restoring 900 hectares of cinchona habitat nationwide will require sustaining partnerships like this one between the state, civil organizations and local communities for years, not months. But the symbolism of the starting point — a farming community planting the tree that gave rise to one of the most important medicines in human history, on the land where that knowledge was born — is not a small thing.
