In Bogotá, lawmakers debating a stalled adult-use bill keep citing it. In Buenos Aires, deputies drafting a new legalization proposal keep citing it. In Mexico City, advocates pushing a frozen federal cannabis law keep citing it. The reference point is always the same: Uruguay, the only country in the world with more than a decade of experience running a fully regulated, nationwide cannabis market. Now, a new peer-reviewed study of that experience is landing with an uncomfortable message for the model Latin America keeps looking up to — its most distinctive feature, the cannabis social club, is quietly turning into something else.

The study, published in the *International Journal of Drug Policy* by researchers Mafalda Pardal, Rosario Queirolo, Lorena Repetto and Marta Rychert, examines what has happened to Uruguay's cannabis clubs since the country's 2013 law came into force. Uruguay was the first nationwide jurisdiction to legalize and regulate cannabis, and it did so through three parallel channels rather than one: home cultivation, authorized in August 2014; cannabis social clubs, which began registering that October; and pharmacy sales, which only started in July 2017. The clubs were conceived as the middle path — collective, non-commercial spaces where members grow cannabis together and share the harvest, modeled loosely on associative cultivation practices from Spain. More than a decade later, the researchers find, that founding idea is under strain.

## Growth on paper, drift underneath

The headline numbers look like a policy success. According to the study, the pace of new club registrations accelerated sharply in recent years, climbing from just 14 newly registered clubs in 2015 to 115 in 2024, with the largest increases concentrated in 2022 through 2024. Clubs have also spread geographically: what began as a Montevideo-and-coast phenomenon now has a registered presence in every department of the country. And the model has proven durable — relatively few clubs have shut down since the first ones opened, and collectively they continue to move a significant share of all the cannabis distributed through Uruguay's legal channels.

But the same study identifies a structural problem beneath that growth. Genuinely social, member-run clubs increasingly coexist with what the researchers call "quasi-dispensary" clubs — operations that keep the legal form of a club but function, in practice, more like a small retail outlet for a fixed membership. The paper points to informal social supply as one symptom: club members sharing cannabis with people who are not registered members, or households pooling a single membership among several consumers. There are also indications, the researchers note, of clubs selling cannabis outright to non-registered buyers, which is illegal under Uruguay's framework.

This is not an entirely new finding — Pardal and Queirolo flagged the same tension in a 2019 study titled "From Activism to Dispensaries?" based on interviews with club representatives. In that earlier fieldwork, club operators told researchers that Uruguay's own regulatory requirements were partly to blame: rules mandating reinforced security systems, banning any external signage or advertising, and restricting how clubs can present themselves to the public made it nearly impossible to run the kind of open, participatory, activist space many founders originally envisioned. Members interviewed complained that the law confined clubs to being little more than a pickup point — even when they wanted to host talks, workshops or communal events, the regulatory environment did not allow it. The 2026 study suggests that dynamic, plus waning engagement from members and club managers over time, has only deepened.

## Pharmacies are pulling ahead

The clubs' relative stagnation as a *social* model is unfolding just as Uruguay's other legal channels have shifted dramatically. According to the country's cannabis regulator, the Institute for the Regulation and Control of Cannabis (IRCCA), 120,083 people were registered across the three legal access channels as of April 2026. Of those, roughly 89,000 — about 74 percent — buy their cannabis at a pharmacy counter, making pharmacy sales by far the dominant channel. Club membership, at just under 21,000 people spread across 585 authorized clubs, is now a distant second, and home cultivation has actually fallen — from a peak of roughly 14,000 registered growers in 2022 to about 10,300 in early 2026.

Pharmacy sales themselves have kept climbing: IRCCA figures cited by the Argentine outlet El Planteo show pharmacies sold 525 kilograms of cannabis in March 2026 alone, up 51 percent from the same month a year earlier. The number of pharmacies carrying cannabis has also expanded, from roughly 40 in early 2025 to 60 by April 2026, concentrated heavily in Montevideo, Canelones and Maldonado. In other words, the channel Uruguay added last, and treated as the most conventional, commercial option, is now the one absorbing most of the country's registered consumers — while the clubs built around collective, non-profit cultivation are growing in number but shrinking in relative weight, and drifting toward the retail logic they were designed to avoid.

## Why the region is watching

None of this means Uruguay's experiment has failed — public support for regulation has climbed from roughly a quarter of the population around the time of legalization to nearly half today, according to survey data compiled by drug-policy researchers, and officials have credited the legal market with diverting a meaningful share of consumption away from illicit trafficking. But the findings complicate the way Uruguay is often invoked elsewhere in the region: as a template to copy rather than a case to study.

Colombia's stalled adult-use bill, Mexico's dormant federal cannabis law, and Argentina's various legalization proposals have all, at different points, pointed to Uruguay's club model as evidence that non-commercial, collective cultivation can coexist with a legal market. What the new research suggests is more complicated: that keeping a club model genuinely social, rather than a disguised storefront, requires regulatory choices — around security rules, advertising, permitted activities and oversight — that Uruguay itself has not fully gotten right after twelve years of trying. For a region still negotiating whether cannabis clubs, pharmacies, or some hybrid should anchor the next generation of legal markets, that is arguably the most useful, and least comfortable, lesson Uruguay has to offer.
