title: "In Peru's Yanesha Reserve, Cataloguing 120 Medicinal Plants Is a Race Against Deforestation"
excerpt: "A scientific expedition in Peru's Yanesha Reserve found a possible new frog and fish species, and catalogued over 120 medicinal and ritual plants used by Yanesha communities. Deforestation now threatens both the forest and that knowledge."
tag: "Ethnobotany"
---

In the cloud forests of Pasco, in central Peru, a monthlong scientific expedition this spring turned up two creatures new to science: a frog barely two centimeters long, from the genus Chiasmocleis, and a species of armored catfish, genus Ancistrus, pulled from the reserve's streams. Both await genetic confirmation before they can be formally described. Those discoveries made headlines. A quieter finding from the same fieldwork may matter more for the region's future: researchers working alongside Yanesha communities catalogued more than 120 species of plants used medicinally or ritually, plants that Yanesha healers have relied on for generations to treat everything from fever to snakebite.

The expedition, led by Peru's Instituto de Investigaciones de la Amazonía Peruana (IIAP) under the Ministry of Environment, ran from late April through May 2026 inside the Reserva Comunal Yanesha, a protected area of more than 34,000 hectares within the Oxapampa-Asháninka-Yánesha Biosphere Reserve, a UNESCO-recognized landscape shared by Yanesha, Asháninka, and mestizo settlers. A team of thirty to thirty-five researchers, park rangers, and community members surveyed frogs, fish, bats, dung beetles, birds, and flora across the reserve and its buffer zone. Alongside the two potential new species, they recorded more than 300 plant species and 37 species of fish, and confirmed breeding activity for the Andean cock-of-the-rock, Peru's national bird, and for Koepcke's hermit, a hummingbird found only in this stretch of Andean-Amazonian transition forest. The Yanesha people themselves are spread across a wider territory than the reserve, along the Palcazú, Pichanaz, and Pachitea river basins in Pasco, Huánuco, and Junín regions, but this particular tract has functioned as a living reservoir of both species and practice.

## A biodiversity census with a cultural layer

What distinguishes this expedition from a routine species count is the decision to fold ethnobotanical documentation into the biological survey. IIAP's team included social scientists who worked directly with Yanesha community members to record which plants are gathered, how they are prepared, and what they are used for, descriptive information, not laboratory data. IIAP director Manuel Martín Brañas noted that amphibians in particular serve as precise indicators of ecosystem degradation, a reminder that the frog and the medicine chest are, in this landscape, part of the same ledger: what disappears from the forest disappears from both.

This is not the first time outside researchers have tried to record Yanesha plant knowledge. Between 2006 and 2008, a team from France's Institut de Recherche pour le Développement, working with Peruvian institutions and three Yanesha communities, San Pedro de Pichanaz, Loma Linda, and Tsachopen, produced "Yato' ramuesh," a bilingual compendium of more than 150 plants used in Yanesha medicine, cross-referenced against their botanical names and reviewed by eight community members in a workshop before publication. That project documented not only remedies but the prayers and disease narratives that accompany their use, treating the plants as inseparable from the cosmology around them. The 2026 IIAP count of 120-plus species, gathered in a single reserve rather than across multiple communities nearly two decades later, suggests that core knowledge, at least in this area, is still actively held, a modestly encouraging sign set against a broader regional pattern of erosion.

## Where the knowledge still lives

That knowledge is not abstract. In Quillazú, near Oxapampa, a healer named Miguel Gaspar Abel, known locally as "Abel, el curandero," has practiced Amazonian plant medicine for more than 35 years. He began his apprenticeship at 14 under Yanesha masters, training to become a Pa'llerr, a Yanesha term for a naturalist healer, through a period of ascetic restriction: avoiding salt and sugar, limiting contact with the opposite sex, staying indoors during daylight. His practice today centers on bark and root preparations, alongside ceremonial rites such as a water-blessing offering. He is now one of the elders recognized by Saberes Productivos, a Peruvian state program under the Pensión 65 pension scheme that formally identifies elderly Indigenous people as holders of transmissible knowledge, an acknowledgment that this expertise is not folklore but a functioning body of practice, and, implicitly, that it is at risk of being lost.

Historically, Yanesha girls learned plant medicine through the Ponapnora, an initiation rite in which they spent weeks in the forest with mothers and grandmothers. Researchers who have studied the community describe that practice as substantially diminished today, crowded out by school schedules and the broader pull of life outside the community. That is the quieter threat behind the 120-species count: not that the plants will vanish from the forest first, but that the people who know how to use them correctly will age out before the knowledge is written down or passed on.

## What is proven, what is practiced, and what is still unknown

It would be a mistake to read a catalogue of 120 medicinal plants as 120 validated cures. Ethnobotanical documentation, including projects like "Yato' ramuesh" and the IIAP survey, records what a plant is used for and by whom, that is testimony and tradition, valuable in its own right as cultural heritage and as a map for future research, but it is not equivalent to clinical evidence. Of the many thousands of plant species documented across the Peruvian Amazon over the past decades, only a small fraction have been subjected to the kind of phytochemical or pharmacological study that would establish, for a general audience, what compounds are present, at what doses they act, and whether they are safe. Peru's own national database of medicinal plants, compiled by IIAP in prior years, exists precisely because that gap between traditional use and scientific characterization is so wide across the Amazon.

None of that diminishes the value of what was recorded in Pasco this spring. A Pa'llerr's decades of clinical-style observation, which bark reduces fever, which root calms a stomach, prepared and dosed in specific ways, constitutes real, transmissible expertise, refined through generations of trial within a single ecological and cultural context. But it is a different kind of knowledge than a peer-reviewed pharmacology study, and conflating the two, presenting traditional use as proven medicine, or dismissing it because it hasn't been tested in a lab, does a disservice to both science and the communities holding the knowledge.

## Conservation as the common thread

The reserve itself, established in 1988 as Peru's first community-managed reserve, is governed jointly by ten native communities and six associated annexes through AMARCY, the association responsible for its conservation, working alongside Sernanp, the national protected-areas service. Community members interviewed during the expedition reported the effects of deforestation they are already living with: rising local temperatures and a visible decline in wildlife, driven by agricultural expansion, illegal logging, and infrastructure pressure at the reserve's edges. Those same pressures, forest loss, migration, the pull of formal schooling and wage labor, are what erode the transmission of plant knowledge from one generation to the next.

Seen together, the frog, the catfish, and the 120 plants documented this spring are not separate stories. They are a single snapshot of a landscape whose biological and cultural inventories are shrinking under the same pressures, taken at a moment when at least part of that inventory, the Yanesha's own knowledge of what grows there and what it is for, is still possible to record.
