In November 2021, Bolivia's state medicines agency did something it had never done before: it authorized a five-year-old girl with cerebral palsy to legally use cannabis oil. The authorization, granted by the Agencia Estatal de Medicamentos y Tecnologías en Salud (AGEMED) after months of advocacy that involved the national Ombudsman's office, was explicitly "exceptional" and lasted three months. It remains, more than four years later, one of only a handful of cases in which a Bolivian patient has ever obtained legal permission to use the plant medicinally. Everyone else — children with epilepsy, cancer patients, elderly people with dementia, people living with chronic pain — has had to choose between going without, or breaking the law.

That is the backdrop against which a new bill, filed in April by a deputy from the ruling coalition, is once again asking Bolivia's Congress to do what neighboring countries did years ago: create a legal path to medicinal cannabis. It is at least the third serious attempt in a decade. None of the previous ones survived.

## The one country left behind

Some form of legal medical cannabis access now exists in Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Mexico, Paraguay, Peru and Uruguay, according to regional tracking by drug-policy researchers at the Washington Office on Latin America (WOLA) and the International Drug Policy Consortium (IDPC). Even where implementation is uneven — Peru's own patient registry shows most of its 55,000-plus enrolled users still cannot buy products through a licensed pharmacy — a legal channel exists, on paper, for a doctor to prescribe cannabis and a patient to obtain it without fear of prosecution.

Bolivia has no such channel. There is no registry, no prescription pathway, no licensed producer a family can legally buy from. A person found with cannabis oil for a sick child is, in the eyes of the law, in exactly the same position as someone caught with cocaine paste.

That is not rhetorical flourish. It is the letter of Law 1008, the Régimen de la Coca y Sustancias Controladas, passed in 1988 at the height of the U.S.-backed war on drugs and still in force today. Article 48 sets a sentence of 10 to 25 years in prison, plus fines, for anyone who "traffics" in a controlled substance — a category that includes cannabis in any form. Article 49 carves out an exception only for amounts a medical board determines correspond to 48 hours of personal consumption; anything beyond that threshold, including a dropper bottle of oil meant to last a child with epilepsy a month, is presumed to be trafficking. Unlike most of its neighbors, Bolivia's law sets no defined minimum quantity for cannabis possession, a gap that Bolivian legal researchers and outlets such as Los Tiempos have described as effectively criminalizing consumption itself, since prosecutors and judges are left to decide case by case whether an amount "exceeds" personal use.

The contrast with how Bolivian law treats coca is telling, and it matters for how this story should be read outside Bolivia. Since 2017, the Ley General de la Coca (Law 906) has protected the cultivation, trade and traditional consumption of raw coca leaf — chewing it, drinking it as mate — as part of the country's cultural and Indigenous heritage, distinguishing it from the coca paste used to produce cocaine. Cannabis, native to no Andean tradition but medicinally useful all the same, was never given a comparable carve-out. The same 1988 law that treats a sacred, constitutionally protected plant with nuance treats cannabis oil for a dying grandmother as a narcotics case.

## The clandestine pharmacy

In the absence of any legal option, Bolivian patients and families have built an improvised, underground supply chain. Reporting by the outlet Urgente.bo has documented cases including a woman with breast cancer who has produced her own cannabis oil since 2016 because she cannot obtain it any other way; a young man in a wheelchair who tried to make oil at home after watching online tutorials, with a bad batch triggering a medical crisis; and a family treating a 97-year-old grandmother's dementia symptoms with homemade cannabis butter instead of benzodiazepines.

The risks of that improvisation go beyond arrest. "Muchas veces te dicen que es aceite de marihuana y no sabés si es aceite de oliva" — "Often they tell you it's marijuana oil and you don't know if it's olive oil" — Bolivian senator Andrea Barrientos, who has pushed cannabis legislation for years, told Urgente.bo, describing a market with no quality control, no dosing standards and no recourse when a product is diluted, mislabeled or simply fake. Drug-policy researcher Patricia Chulver put the human cost more bluntly in the same reporting: families, she said, "viven con miedo, a la sombra de la ilegalidad y el prejuicio" — live with fear, in the shadow of illegality and prejudice. Some patients told reporters they buy from informal sellers advertising on social media or from herbal-medicine shops of uncertain provenance; others try to source oil through neighboring countries where medical cannabis is legal, exposing themselves to cross-border smuggling risk on top of everything else. Bolivia does not track how many people use cannabis medicinally, because doing so would require a registry that does not exist — a data gap that is itself a direct product of prohibition.

## A bill, again

On April 16, deputy Sandra Hinojosa, of the Alianza Popular bloc, filed the "Ley de Garantía del Acceso al Cannabis Medicinal y sus Derivados para la Atención Integral de la Salud" in Bolivia's Chamber of Deputies, according to reporting by the Agencia de Noticias Fides (ANF) and the outlet eju.tv. The bill, still identified in the legislature's own records as pending as of this writing, would create a new regulatory authority, ARCAME, under the Ministry of Health, to license research, cultivation, industrialization and export of medicinal cannabis; a confidential national patient and prescriber registry (RENPAC); and a requirement that dispensing happen only through licensed pharmacies against a verified prescription. It would add cannabinoid derivatives to Bolivia's list of essential medicines and prioritize state or mixed public-private production, which its sponsors argue could cut prices by as much as 70 percent compared with imported or black-market products — a claim from the bill's own text that has not been independently verified and should be read as an aspiration rather than a costed projection.

Two provisions speak most directly to patients living under today's criminal framework. The bill states there would be no criminal penalties for accredited patients and prescribing doctors acting within the new system, and — notably — it includes an amnesty clause for people already facing prosecution for possessing medicinal cannabis oil, provided there is no evidence they were selling it commercially. That single clause is an implicit acknowledgment, from inside Bolivia's own Congress, that people have been prosecuted under Law 1008 for trying to treat sick relatives.

It would not be the first time Bolivian legislators have tried this. Senator Barrientos introduced her own decriminalization-oriented bill in 2022 and 2023, drawing public support from cancer-patient organizations and a favorable, if cautious, hearing from then–Health Minister María Renee Castro, who said in 2023 that "hay mucho que aprender y debatir" — there is a lot to learn and debate — about which cannabis compounds should be recommended. That bill did not become law. Whether Hinojosa's version fares differently will depend on committees in a Congress that, as of mid-2026, has not scheduled a floor debate. For the families currently making cannabis oil in their kitchens, the outcome is not an abstract policy question. It determines whether the next exceptional authorization, if one ever comes, arrives before or after a criminal case does.
