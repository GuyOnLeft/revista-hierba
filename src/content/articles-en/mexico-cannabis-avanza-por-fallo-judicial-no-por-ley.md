Mexico's Supreme Court handed down another cannabis ruling on June 25, its latest in a string of decisions stretching back to 2018, and once again the country's marijuana policy moved forward through a courtroom rather than through Congress. The Federal Commission for Protection Against Sanitary Risk, known as COFEPRIS, followed on July 7 with a public clarification of what the ruling does and does not permit. For a region watching Mexico as a bellwether — alongside Colombia's pharmacy reforms and Uruguay's decade-old state model — the episode is a reminder that judicial patchwork is not the same thing as regulation, and that the gap between the two carries real consequences for patients and consumers alike.

## What the Court Actually Decided

The case reaching the Pleno, or full bench, of the Suprema Corte de Justicia de la Nación (SCJN) was narrow on its face. Nine people had petitioned COFEPRIS in 2022 for authorization to plant, cultivate, harvest, possess and transport cannabis and THC for personal recreational use, relying on the court's 2021 General Declaration of Unconstitutionality 1/2018, which struck down the legal provisions barring health authorities from issuing such permits. COFEPRIS initially refused, arguing the General Health Law gave it no such power. After a lower court ordered compliance, the agency issued a single joint authorization naming all nine petitioners — who then challenged that approach, arguing each of them was entitled to a separate document.

Ruling on the Recurso de Inconformidad in a plenary session, the SCJN sided with COFEPRIS's approach. According to the court's official communiqué, the justices held that "the issuance of a single administrative document did not limit or reduce the rights recognized to the beneficiary persons," and that what matters is whether each person is expressly named as a titleholder within it — not how many separate papers are printed. COFEPRIS, for its part, moved quickly to contain the ruling's scope. In a July 7 statement carried by Mexican outlets including PorEsto, the agency stressed that the decision "se limitó a revisar la legalidad del procedimiento mediante el cual se expiden permisos" — it only reviewed the legality of the permitting procedure — and does not legalize marijuana sales, distribution or any commercial cannabis activity nationwide.

## A Patchwork Built by Judges, Not Legislators

The ruling is procedural, but its context is what makes it newsworthy. Mexico has now spent more than five years — since the SCJN's 2021 unconstitutionality declaration obligated health authorities to process self-consumption permits — without a comprehensive federal cannabis law. Congress has let the issue die across three separate legislative sessions, according to reporting compiled by outlets tracking the stalled Ley General de Cannabis. The result, as Publimetro and Infobae have both documented, is a country that currently has more than 12,000 individual authorizations for self-cultivation and self-consumption in circulation, issued case by case through amparo litigation, alongside an unregulated market that supplies the vast majority of Mexican consumers.

That figure is worth sitting with. Twelve thousand authorizations is not a regulatory system; it is twelve thousand individual lawsuits, each requiring a lawyer, a filing and months of litigation to secure a right the country's highest court says already exists. In February 2026, the SCJN came within a single vote of eliminating the five-gram possession threshold that still exposes low-level users to prosecution — falling short by one justice on the eight votes required for a general binding ruling, though it did grant an individual exemption to a homeless man detained in Ciudad Juárez with just over 14 grams. Mexico's cannabis policy, in other words, is being built one amparo at a time, with each ruling binding only the litigant who filed it unless a rare supermajority converts it into general law.

## Advocates Push the World Cup Deadline

Civil society groups have tried to use a fixed calendar date to force the issue. In April, organizations including Instituto RIA and Educannabis delivered a symbolic bag of cannabis seeds to the Mexican Senate, demanding the chamber revive the shelved Ley General de Cannabis before Mexico co-hosts the 2026 World Cup with the United States and Canada. Zara Snapp, director of Instituto RIA, argued that reactivating the debate "means addressing a pending legislative debt and acknowledging an existing social reality," while Educannabis's Luis Fernando Campos framed clear rules as a matter of both public safety and formal employment. Their underlying argument is a public-health one, not a promotional one: with millions of foreign visitors arriving under varied national norms on cannabis, an ambiguous legal environment does not protect anyone — it just multiplies the discretion police and prosecutors can exercise at the border between what is tolerated and what is prosecuted.

The Senate has not moved. That inaction is precisely what channels every new cannabis question in Mexico back into the courts, which is why a procedural ruling about whether nine names can share one document became national news in the first place.

## Patients Are a Separate, and Separately Neglected, Story

It is worth being precise about who this particular ruling covers. The nine petitioners and the permit framework at issue concern recreational self-consumption, not medicinal use. Mexican patients seeking cannabis-based treatment operate under a distinct COFEPRIS pathway requiring physician prescription, one that predates and runs parallel to the amparo-driven recreational framework — and one that advocacy groups say remains slow, inconsistent and inaccessible outside major cities. Conflating the two tracks, as some celebratory social-media coverage of the June ruling has done, risks overstating what changed. No sales channel opened. No medicinal supply chain was expanded. What the court confirmed is narrower and more bureaucratic than that: a paperwork question about how the state must formally recognize a right it already, on paper, granted eight years ago.

That distinction matters for a magazine committed to separating legal fact from advocacy hope. Mexico's courts have been unusually assertive in cannabis policy since 2018, more so than almost any other legislature-averse system in the hemisphere. But an assertive judiciary is not a substitute for a health ministry with dispensing infrastructure, quality-control standards and a functioning prescription registry — the kind of system Colombia and Uruguay have spent years building through actual regulatory agencies. Until Mexico's Congress acts, patients and recreational users alike remain dependent on litigation to exercise a right the country's own Supreme Court says they already hold.
