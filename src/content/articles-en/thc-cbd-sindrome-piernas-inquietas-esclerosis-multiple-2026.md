In a survey of nearly 70,000 Mexican women, 15.6 percent met the criteria for restless legs syndrome — an irresistible urge to move the legs, worse in the evening and at rest, that wrecks sleep for years before most patients ever hear a diagnosis. Only 18 percent of those with frequent symptoms said they were receiving any treatment. The gap between how common restless legs syndrome is and how rarely it is treated is not unique to Mexico, but it sets the stage for why a small, unglamorous trial out of Madrid is worth a second look.

Published July 2, 2026 in the *Journal of Neurology*, the study tested whether a cannabis-based oromucosal spray already sold across parts of Latin America for an entirely different condition could ease restless legs syndrome. The signal was real. The trial was also tiny, uncontrolled, and heavily skewed toward one patient population — three qualifications that matter as much as the result itself.

## A drug already on the shelf, tried on a new problem

The research team, led by neurologist Laura Lillo Triguero at Hospital Universitario de Getafe in Madrid, working with colleagues from the hospital's pulmonology service and the Sleep Unit at Hospital Ruber Internacional, recruited 18 patients with restless legs syndrome. Sixteen of them had multiple sclerosis; two had the idiopathic form of the disorder, unconnected to any other neurological disease.

All 18 were given nabiximols — the THC/CBD oromucosal spray sold commercially as Sativex, delivering 2.7 mg of THC and 2.5 mg of CBD per spray — to self-administer in the evening, before their symptoms typically began. This was an exploratory, open-label trial: everyone knew they were getting the active drug, there was no placebo group, and the design cannot rule out expectation effects. The researchers followed patients for three months, with a subset continuing treatment and being reassessed at one year.

The rationale for testing a cannabinoid at all comes from basic pharmacology rather than folklore: THC and CBD are known to inhibit glutamate release in the striatum, a brain region increasingly implicated in restless legs syndrome alongside the disorder's better-known links to dopamine signaling and iron metabolism.

## What improved, and what didn't

Patients were scored on the International Restless Legs Syndrome Rating Scale (IRLS), the standard severity instrument for the condition, at baseline, one month, and three months. According to the published results, IRLS scores improved significantly at both follow-up points (p < 0.001). Actigraphy — a wrist-worn device that objectively tracks movement and sleep-wake patterns, used here in place of relying solely on patient self-report — showed a significant drop in wake-after-sleep-onset, meaning patients spent less time awake once they had fallen asleep. Sleep latency (how long it takes to fall asleep) and sleep efficiency showed little to no change, suggesting the drug's effect was concentrated on reducing nighttime awakenings caused by leg symptoms rather than on sleep onset itself.

At the one-year mark, roughly two-thirds of the patients who continued treatment maintained their improvement, according to the authors' summary of the extension data. No serious adverse events were reported in the published account, consistent with nabiximols' long safety record in its approved indication.

## Why this matters beyond a diagnosis it wasn't designed for

Restless legs syndrome is currently treated first with dopamine agonists such as pramipexole or ropinirole — but long-term dopaminergic treatment carries a well-documented risk of "augmentation," a paradoxical worsening in which symptoms appear earlier in the day, spread to other limbs, and intensify the longer a patient stays on the drug. Because of augmentation, clinical guidelines have increasingly pushed dopamine agonists to second-line status behind alpha-2-delta calcium channel ligands like pregabalin and gabapentin enacarbil — drugs that are not universally available or reimbursed across Latin American health systems.

A cannabinoid mechanism that works through glutamate rather than dopamine offers, at least in principle, a way to sidestep the augmentation problem entirely. That is the reason this small trial drew attention beyond typical cannabis-research circles: not because it proves nabiximols treats restless legs syndrome, but because it identifies a plausible alternative pathway in a disease where the standard pathway can betray patients who rely on it for years.

## What this study does not show

The limitations are not footnotes; they define what can and cannot be concluded. This was an open-label trial with no control arm, so it cannot separate a true pharmacological effect from placebo response, natural symptom fluctuation, or the attention patients receive simply by being enrolled in a study. Eighteen participants is a small sample by any standard, and 16 of them had multiple sclerosis — meaning the result speaks most directly to MS-associated restless legs syndrome, a specific subgroup, rather than to the idiopathic disorder that affects the vast majority of people with the condition. Many of these patients may already have been using nabiximols for MS-related spasticity, which the study does not fully disentangle from its restless-legs findings. The authors themselves frame the work as opening "a new line of investigation," not as clinical proof — language considerably more modest than the trial's positive headline numbers might suggest on their own.

A properly randomized, placebo-controlled trial in a broader restless legs syndrome population — including patients without multiple sclerosis — would be needed before this could shift prescribing guidelines anywhere.

## The Latin American angle

What makes this finding practically relevant for the region, rather than just scientifically interesting, is that nabiximols is not a hypothetical drug awaiting import authorization in Latin America — it is already registered for multiple sclerosis-related spasticity in Colombia, where INVIMA approved it for distribution by Laboratorios Biopas, and in Chile, where it was the first cannabis-derived medicine approved by the Instituto de Salud Pública. Brazil has granted more recent registration as well, while Argentina currently allows access only through compassionate-use channels, and Mexico was explicitly excluded from the original regional distribution agreement.

That existing regulatory footprint means neurologists in at least a few Latin American countries who already prescribe nabiximols for MS spasticity are, in effect, in a position to observe — cautiously, and without any change to approved indications — whether their patients with coexisting restless legs syndrome report the same benefit seen in Madrid. It does not license off-label prescribing for restless legs syndrome on the strength of an 18-patient open-label trial, but it does mean that if larger controlled studies eventually confirm the signal, the region would not be starting from zero on access. For a disease that goes undiagnosed and untreated in the vast majority of cases across Latin America, according to available prevalence data, any legitimate new therapeutic lead is worth tracking carefully — and reporting precisely for what it is: promising, small, and unproven.
