---
title: "Paraguay Sentences Cannabis Oil Producer to 10 Years as Legal Access Reaches Only 107 Patients"
excerpt: "A Paraguayan court sentenced medical cannabis oil producer Juan Carlos Cabezudo to ten years in prison, even as the country's official patient registry counts just 107 people nationwide."
tag: "Criminalization"
---

In Luque, a working-class suburb of Asunción, a small greenhouse once produced an oil that traveled through Paraguay in ways the state never managed to replicate: for free, or nearly so, to children with refractory epilepsy whose families had nowhere else to turn. On June 25, 2026, a Paraguayan criminal court sentenced the man who ran that greenhouse, Juan Carlos Cabezudo, to ten years in prison. The verdict lands in a country where, according to the National Registry of Cannabis Derivative Product Users, only 107 patients have managed to enroll in the legal system meant to serve them.

Cabezudo is a lawyer, businessman and one of the founders of the Paraguayan Chamber of Industrial Cannabis. Through his company, known publicly as Granja Madre (also registered as Huerta Madre), he cultivated cannabis and produced an artisanal oil sold under the brand "Kokuesero" — priced between 150,000 and 250,000 guaraníes (roughly $22 to $37) — while, according to reporting at the time, distributing about 80 percent of the output free of charge to patients and their families. On May 21, 2021, agents from Paraguay's National Anti-Drug Secretariat (SENAD) raided his facilities in Luque and Areguá, seizing plants, precision scales and vials of oil. He was charged with illicit possession, cultivation and commercialization of marijuana and its derivatives.

## A five-year path to ten years in prison

The case took an unusually long and uneven road through Paraguay's justice system. Cabezudo spent months under house arrest before being briefly detained again in late 2021 for leaving Asunción without authorization. In March 2022, a Penal Court of Guarantees in Luque granted him a provisional dismissal, lifting precautionary measures and — according to his lawyer at the time — opening the door for a broader debate about aligning drug legislation with medical reality. That debate never produced a change in the law. Instead, the case against Cabezudo continued, and more than five years after the initial raid, a Tribunal de Sentencia in Fernando de la Mora, composed of judges Gloria Garay, Leticia Frachi and Ana Carolina Silveira, convicted him and imposed the maximum available sentence.

His defense attorney, Juan Sosa Bareiro, announced he would appeal, arguing the conviction was unjust because Cabezudo had promoted therapeutic cannabis use "publicly and transparently" for years and had allowed authorities to inspect his cultivation sites rather than concealing them. Prosecutors and SENAD, for their part, pointed to evidence that the plants exceeded THC levels consistent with medicinal-only production and that the operation lacked the health authorizations required even under Paraguay's own cannabis law.

Cabezudo is not the first person prosecuted in Paraguay for producing cannabis oil outside the formal licensing system. Édgar Martínez Sacoman, convicted twice for home-based oil production, received a five-year sentence in 2019 that drew national attention to the same contradiction now resurfacing with Cabezudo's case: patients need the oil, the legal channel barely reaches anyone, and the people who step into that gap face prison.

## A law that exists mostly on paper

Paraguay is often cited among the Latin American countries with a medical cannabis framework already in place. Law 6007, passed in 2017, created PROINCUMEC, the national program for the medical and scientific study of cannabis, and the country issued its first cultivation licenses in February 2020; roughly a dozen companies now hold authorization to produce medicinal cannabis commercially. In late 2025 and early 2026, the health regulator Dinavisa updated the rules again through Resolution 488/2025, opening the door for prescription sales of cannabis-derived medicines through authorized pharmacies under quadruplicate prescription controls.

On paper, the system looks increasingly complete. In practice, it reaches almost no one. As of December 2025, the official patient registry counted just 107 people nationwide — 103 living with refractory epilepsy and four with multiple sclerosis — served by only ten doctors authorized to prescribe cannabis-based treatment. The medications available through that legal channel cost roughly 1.8 million guaraníes (about $240) for epilepsy treatment and 1.3 million guaraníes (about $175) for cancer-related prescriptions, in a country where, according to reporting on the rollout of the new pharmacy rules, seven in ten people have no medical insurance at all. For most Paraguayan families, that price tag alone puts the legal option out of reach, regardless of how many resolutions the health ministry publishes.

That gap is precisely where Granja Madre operated. Mamá Cultiva Paraguay, the grassroots organization of mothers who produce cannabis oil for their children, issued a statement of solidarity with Cabezudo when he was first detained in 2021, a message that reads, in hindsight, as a preview of the argument his case has forced back into public view. "While the Ministry of Health and the Executive closed the door on us, homemade artisanal oil has saved our children and our sick," the group said at the time, adding: "Maybe for many it's illegal, but it's more illegal to watch our children, parents, siblings and loved ones suffer and do nothing." The organization has since renewed its call for the state to stop prosecuting people for what it describes as "planting hope for health," arguing that Law 6007 remains largely "letra muerta" — a dead letter — for low-income patients who cannot navigate, or afford, the formal system.

## What the case exposes

The tension in Cabezudo's prosecution is not simply about one grower's legal missteps. It is about what happens in the space between a law that recognizes a medical right and a state that has built almost no functioning path to exercise it. Paraguay is not unique in this regard — across Latin America, medical cannabis statutes have often outpaced the administrative infrastructure needed to make them real for patients rather than for pharmaceutical importers and licensed corporations. But the ten-year sentence against Cabezudo is a stark marker of how severely that gap can be punished when it is private citizens, rather than institutions, who try to close it.

For patient-rights advocates, the case also raises a due-process concern independent of the underlying cannabis policy debate: a five-year gap between an initial raid, a provisional dismissal that suggested the matter was largely resolved, and a maximum sentence handed down in 2026 reflects a judicial process that left Cabezudo — and, by extension, other Paraguayans considering similar advocacy — in prolonged legal limbo. Mamá Cultiva and other advocates argue that this kind of prosecutorial persistence functions as a deterrent against exactly the kind of grassroots, low-cost production that has filled the gap left by an underused formal registry.

The appeal Sosa Bareiro has promised will now test whether Paraguay's appellate courts see Cabezudo's public, undisguised advocacy as a mitigating factor — or whether the country's courts continue treating patient-access improvisation as no different from ordinary drug trafficking. Either way, the outcome will shape how the next Paraguayan family or activist who tries to fill the space between the law and the pharmacy calculates the risk of doing so.
