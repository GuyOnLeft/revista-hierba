In the Peruvian Amazon region of Loreto, harvesters still slash the bark of a slender tree and watch it bleed. The sap that runs out is a deep, viscous red — "sangre de grado" to Peruvians, "sangre de drago" to Ecuadorians and Colombians, dragon's blood to everyone else. For generations, communities along the Ucayali and Napo rivers have used it to close wounds, calm intestinal ailments, and treat skin infections. Today that same red latex, drawn from the tree *Croton lechleri*, sits in two very different files a world apart: the U.S. Food and Drug Administration's drug registry, where a purified extract of it is sold as a prescription medicine, and Peru's national registry of biopiracy cases, where it is the single most frequently misappropriated biological resource the country has recorded.

That contradiction — a real, rigorously tested pharmaceutical success story built on indigenous knowledge, running alongside an unresolved account of who gets paid for it — makes *Croton lechleri* one of the clearest test cases anywhere for what ethical bioprospecting is supposed to look like, and how far the current system still falls short.

## From folk remedy to FDA approval

The drug is called crofelemer, and its path to a pharmacy shelf took roughly a quarter-century, according to a detailed account published by Atlas Obscura. The story starts with Shaman Pharmaceuticals, a California startup founded in the late 1980s by entrepreneur Lisa Conte, which sent ethnobotanist Steven King into the Amazon and other tropical forests to work directly with traditional healers and assemble a library of thousands of plant specimens screened for therapeutic activity. Croton lechleri's sap, already known throughout Amazonian folk medicine as an anti-diarrheal and wound treatment, stood out early as a promising lead.

Shaman Pharmaceuticals never survived to bring the drug to market. The company collapsed into Chapter 11 bankruptcy in the early 2000s, at a moment when the broader "bioprospecting" model it represented — for-profit drug discovery built on indigenous ethnobotanical knowledge — had fallen out of favor amid criticism that communities were being mined for information without commensurate return. Conte subsequently founded a new company, Napo Pharmaceuticals, which acquired Shaman's compound library, including crofelemer, and carried the drug through the FDA approval process. On December 31, 2012, the agency approved crofelemer for relief of noninfectious diarrhea in HIV-positive patients on antiretroviral therapy — only the second botanical prescription drug ever approved in the United States, and the first administered orally. It launched first under the brand Fulyzaq, then was rebranded Mytesi in 2016 by Napo Pharmaceuticals, now a subsidiary of the Nasdaq-listed Jaguar Health.

## What the chemistry actually shows

Unlike many "miracle plant" stories, the pharmacology behind sangre de grado holds up under scrutiny — which is precisely why it belongs in a conversation about rigor rather than romance. Crofelemer is not the raw sap; it is a purified, standardized mixture of proanthocyanidin oligomers extracted from the latex. Pharmacological studies published in the peer-reviewed literature and summarized in drug monographs describe crofelemer as a dual inhibitor of two distinct chloride channels in the lining of the small intestine: the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) and calcium-activated chloride channels (CaCC). By partially blocking both, the compound reduces the excessive secretion of chloride ions and water into the gut that drives secretory diarrhea, without slowing intestinal motility the way opioid-based antidiarrheals do.

That mechanism explains why crofelemer's clinical use has expanded well beyond its original HIV indication. The FDA has granted orphan drug designations for crofelemer in short bowel syndrome and, more recently, in microvillus inclusion disease, a rare and severe congenital intestinal disorder that mostly affects infants. In May 2026, Jaguar Health said its ongoing ethnobotanical research collaboration continues to inform crofelemer's development for these genetically defined pediatric conditions, according to a company announcement — a reminder that the compound's scientific story is still being written more than a decade after its first approval.

## Who actually benefits

Here the narrative gets harder to romanticize. When Shaman Pharmaceuticals was founded, it also created a nonprofit, the Healing Forest Conservancy, meant to design a mechanism for returning some commercial benefit to the indigenous communities and countries whose knowledge underpinned its drug candidates — an early, imperfect attempt to answer a question international law was also grappling with at the time. Napo Pharmaceuticals says it continues that approach today, describing a "sustainable" and "fair trade" harvesting arrangement carried out with 19 distinct indigenous groups across more than 30 locations in the central and northern Peruvian Amazon, according to company materials. In January 2026, Napo also launched a copay savings and patient assistance program intended to widen access to Mytesi within the U.S. health system, where list prices for specialty botanical drugs routinely run far beyond what most patients can pay directly.

But that self-reported benefit-sharing arrangement exists alongside a separate, government-run accounting that tells a less settled story. Peru's National Commission against Biopiracy (CNB), chaired by the intellectual-property agency Indecopi, tracks patent applications filed anywhere in the world that draw on Peruvian genetic resources or associated traditional knowledge without documented authorization. As of a 2022 report cited by the Peruvian outlet Servindi, the commission had identified 246 such cases affecting 41 Peruvian resources since the year 2000 — and sangre de grado, under its scientific name Croton lechleri, accounted for more of those cases, 66, than any other single species, ahead of maca and cashew. A more recent Servindi report from May 2026 states that Peru's commission has since helped block or roll back 180 biopiracy attempts in patent offices worldwide over the past two decades, again naming sangre de grado alongside maca and sacha inchi as among the country's most contested biological exports.

Indecopi's tally is not a judgment on Napo Pharmaceuticals specifically — the commission's cases span many companies, countries, and decades, and crofelemer's benefit-sharing arrangement with harvester communities is, by most public accounts, more structured than the norm for the industry. But the two numbers sitting side by side — a single approved drug generating a stream of U.S. prescription revenue, and 66 separate patent claims flagged by Peru's own government as improperly built on the same plant's traditional knowledge — is the honest measure of where bioprospecting has landed, roughly thirty years after the debate began. Real medicine came out of the Amazon. Whether the value it generates flows back to the rivers it came from remains, case by case, an open question.
