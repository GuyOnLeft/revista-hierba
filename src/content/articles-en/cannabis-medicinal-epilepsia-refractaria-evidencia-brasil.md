title: "Real-World Evidence From Brazil's Public CBD Program Backs Treatment for Refractory Epilepsy — But Access Is the Catch"
excerpt: "A peer-reviewed study of 91 patients in Brazil's public health system found strong seizure reduction linked to sustained CBD access, and equally strong evidence that stock-outs undo the benefit."
tag: "Research"
---

For thousands of families across Latin America who spent years fighting court battles for the right to give their children cannabis oil, a new peer-reviewed study offers something rarer than another testimonial: systematic, real-world data from inside a public health system. Published on July 8, 2026, in *Clinical Therapeutics*, the study followed patients with treatment-resistant epilepsy enrolled in the Canabidiol–SUS Project, a program run by the Health Department of Brazil's Federal District (SES-DF) that has provided cannabidiol-based products to patients through the public health system since the mid-2010s. The finding that will matter most to patients is not just that CBD appeared to help — it's that how long, and how reliably, someone could keep taking it determined whether it helped at all.

## What the study found

The research team — led by Mariana Dias Lula and Cristina Mariano Ruas of the Universidade Federal de Minas Gerais, working with colleagues at Brazil's health regulatory agency ANVISA and Université de Bordeaux — conducted a cross-sectional study of patients registered in the program between 2017 and 2022. Between October 2023 and February 2024, the team combined medical-record review with structured telephone interviews, mostly with caregivers or guardians (89% of respondents), since most patients in the program are children and adolescents with severe epilepsy syndromes.

Among the 91 patients who had actually used a cannabis-based product, 70.3% reported a seizure reduction of 50% or more — the threshold conventionally used in epilepsy research to define a meaningful clinical response. That number, on its own, is consistent with what smaller observational studies and open-label trials have found elsewhere. What stands out is the split within that group: among patients who were still actively using CBD at the time of the interview, 94.7% reported that level of improvement, compared with just 57.1% among those who had stopped. Statistically, the researchers found that more than 12 months of continuous treatment was strongly associated with a favorable response — patients who reached that duration threshold were reported to have dramatically higher odds of a meaningful seizure reduction than those with shorter or interrupted courses.

Most patients used CBD isolate (73.6%), with a smaller share (18.7%) using formulations combining CBD with other cannabinoids. Adverse events were reported by just over half of patients (51.6%) — sleep disturbances (28.6%), mood changes (24.2%), and gastrointestinal symptoms (19.8%) were the most common. None of these categories, as described in the study, appear to represent the kind of severe, treatment-limiting toxicity that would raise safety alarms, though the researchers were explicit that ongoing pharmacovigilance and better product standardization are still needed.

## Access, not biology, decided the outcome

The more consequential finding — and the one with the clearest policy implications — is what determined whether a patient stayed on treatment long enough to benefit. A companion analysis from the same research project, examining how 101 registered patients actually obtained their CBD products, found that 58.4% of everyone enrolled in the program never managed to obtain the product through the public system at all, despite 80.2% of formal requests being approved on paper. Among the patients who did use CBD, only 46.1% received it through the public system; 69.2% ended up purchasing it privately out of pocket, and 31.9% obtained it only after going to court. Of the patients who discontinued treatment, two-thirds cited product shortages at the health department or their own inability to keep paying for it privately as the reason.

Put together, the two studies describe a therapy that works reasonably well when patients can actually take it consistently — and a public system that, despite formally offering it for free, routinely fails to deliver the product to the people it approved. The gap between "approved for dispensation" and "product in hand" is where the clinical benefit quietly disappears for a large share of patients. This is not a story about whether CBD works; the researchers are careful to frame their result as "perceived effectiveness," based on self-report and caregiver report rather than blinded seizure counts. It is a story about a health system whose stock-outs may be doing as much to determine outcomes as the compound itself.

## What this evidence is — and isn't

It's worth being precise about the limits of what was measured. This is real-world observational data, not a randomized controlled trial: there is no placebo arm, outcomes were self-reported by patients or their caregivers rather than measured through seizure diaries or EEG monitoring, and the products used varied in formulation and dose across the cohort. The researchers themselves flagged the cross-sectional design, the reliance on retrospective recall, and the heterogeneity of the products as limitations. Reported seizure reduction in an unblinded, caregiver-reported study is a different kind of evidence than what a phase 3 trial of a standardized pharmaceutical formulation (like the CBD-based Epidiolex, already approved in several countries for specific severe epilepsy syndromes) would produce. What this study adds is not proof of a new mechanism or a new drug — it's a rare, systematic look at what happens to cannabis-based epilepsy treatment once it leaves the clinical trial and has to survive contact with a real public bureaucracy, over years, for real families.

## Why this matters beyond Brazil

The Pan American Health Organization has estimated that more than half of people living with epilepsy in Latin America and the Caribbean do not receive adequate treatment — a treatment gap that, in some lower-income areas of the region, reaches 70%. Brazil's judicial history with cannabis-based epilepsy treatment is not unique in the region: in Mexico, a mother's 2019 legal challenge on behalf of her son eventually reached the Supreme Court, which in 2021 ordered the national health institute to provide cannabis-derived medication; Mexico's drug regulator has since approved only a handful of import permits for such products. Across Latin America, patient associations and families have repeatedly had to use courts, rather than pharmacies, as the point of access.

That is exactly why this study matters beyond its home country. It offers something other Latin American health systems considering similar cannabis access programs rarely get: a documented account of how a formally approved benefit performs once implemented — including the specific administrative failure points, like dispensation delays and stock-outs, that undercut its clinical promise. A public program that grants access on paper but not in practice does not just disappoint patients; according to this data, it may be actively suppressing the treatment effect that the underlying compound is capable of producing. For health officials elsewhere in the region weighing whether and how to fund CBD access through public systems, the lesson is not "does it work" so much as "can you actually keep it in stock."
