---
title: "Latin America Grows the Cannabis Germany Wants — But Can No Longer Sell It There Directly"
excerpt: "Uruguay and Colombia have doubled their cannabis export volumes, yet their direct shipments to Germany, Europe's largest medical market, have collapsed. A pharmaceutical certification wall is turning them into raw-material suppliers."
tag: "Trade"
---

Latin America's cannabis industry has spent the past two years celebrating export numbers: Uruguay's shipments abroad doubled between 2023 and 2025, Colombia now holds authorization to send product to fourteen countries across five continents, and both nations are frequently described in trade press as the future suppliers of Europe's booming medical cannabis market. What that narrative tends to skip is what happened to those countries' direct trade with Germany, the market that actually matters most. It collapsed.

According to trade data reported by the industry outlet Cannamonitor, Uruguay's direct cannabis shipments to Germany fell from 1.7 tonnes in late 2024 to close to zero by the second quarter of 2025. South Africa, a comparable emerging supplier, recorded zero direct exports to Germany in both the first and second quarters of 2025. Colombia held on to only marginal volumes, shipping just 0.2 tonnes directly in the second quarter. None of this happened because German demand dried up — quite the opposite. Germany's medical cannabis imports rose to roughly 200 tonnes in 2025, an increase of about 57% following an expansion of import quotas by the country's federal drug regulator, BfArM, according to reporting by Cannabis-Startups. A separate industry estimate cited by GrowerIQ put Germany's domestic medical cannabis consumption at 142 tonnes in just the first nine months of 2025. The German market is not shrinking. Latin America's direct share of it is.

## The Certification Wall

The reason comes down to a single regulatory requirement: EU-GMP, the European Union's pharmaceutical-grade manufacturing standard, codified in EudraLex Volume 4 and, for cannabis specifically, its herbal-medicine and computerized-systems annexes. Selling cannabis flower or extract into the German market as a finished, sellable product effectively requires either an EU-GMP certificate issued by a European national regulator, or written confirmation, shipment by shipment, from the exporting country's own competent authority — a slower and less scalable path. Colombia, Uruguay and Argentina are not on the European Commission's Article 111b "white list" of countries exempted from that per-shipment paperwork, unlike Canada, Australia, or Switzerland, according to GrowerIQ's certification guide. That leaves Latin American exporters navigating a process that the guide describes as taking twelve to twenty-four months from initial gap analysis to certificate in hand: a months-long remediation of facilities and documentation, an on-site inspection by a European authority, and the real possibility of a formal non-compliance finding at the end of it — as happened to two South African facilities inspected by Malta's Medicines Authority in April 2025.

Germany's own regulator, BfArM, has become the dominant inspecting authority for producers in Colombia, South Africa and Canada seeking direct certification, but its capacity to conduct overseas facility inspections is limited relative to the number of producers seeking a foothold. Portugal's Infarmed and Denmark's DKMA offer alternative inspection routes, but the bottleneck is structural, not a matter of any single country trying harder.

## Who Captures the Value Instead

Rather than exit the German market, Uruguayan, Colombian and South African cannabis has mostly kept moving there — just indirectly. Product now flows through EU-GMP certified processors already established inside the European Union, which import raw or semi-processed cannabis, finish it to pharmaceutical standard on their own certified lines, and resell it into Germany under their own certification. Cannamonitor's reporting names Portugal's Agrivabe and Spain's Linneo Health and Medalchemy among the intermediaries handling this role, describing Latin America's contribution to the finished German product as "more symbolic than material" once it passes through a European processor's books. Those companies capture the margin that comes with pharmaceutical-grade finishing and compliance — value that would otherwise accrue to the growers themselves if they held their own certification.

Uruguay's own export statistics for 2025, reviewed by the financial outlet El Cronista, illustrate the pattern from the inside. The country exported 45 tonnes of cannabis-related product that year — nearly double the volume shipped in 2023 — but total export revenue actually fell, from roughly $3 million in 2023 to $2.6 million in 2025. Direct sales credited to Germany amounted to only about $100,000 of that total, a rounding error next to the $1.1 million earned from Switzerland or the $813,000 from Portugal — the country now functioning as Uruguay's principal logistics gateway into the EU rather than as an end market in its own right. More of Uruguay's growing export volume is also shifting toward low-margin raw seed rather than finished, higher-value flower or medicine, according to the same reporting.

## A Narrower Kind of Opportunity

None of this means Latin America's cannabis export ambitions are collapsing — Colombia, in particular, continues to add new destination markets and cites genuine structural advantages, including year-round cultivation and lower production costs than Canadian or European growers, according to Cannabis-Startups' reporting on the country's export strategy. Discussion at Cannabis Europa's London conference in late May, recapped by the outlet El Planteo, centered on precisely this dynamic: European buyers increasingly want pharmaceutical-grade, EU-GMP-verified supply, and Latin American producers who can meet that bar — rather than simply grow cannabis cheaply — are the ones positioned to benefit. As the outlet put it, the next phase of the global cannabis trade may not be primarily about which countries have legalized cannabis, but about which producers can prove, on paper and under inspection, that they can manufacture it to pharmaceutical standard.

That reframing matters for how the region's export growth should be read. Government press releases and trade fairs in Bogotá, Montevideo and Buenos Aires continue to tout rising tonnage as evidence of an industry coming into its own. The underlying trade data suggests something more specific and less triumphant: Latin American cannabis is increasingly present in Europe's biggest market, but increasingly as an input controlled by someone else's certificate, not as a finished product sold under its own name.
