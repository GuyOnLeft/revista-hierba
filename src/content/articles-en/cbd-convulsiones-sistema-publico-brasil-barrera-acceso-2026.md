title: "Cannabidiol Cut Seizures in Brazil's Public Health System — but Access Remained the Real Barrier"
excerpt: "A study within Brazil's public health system found that 70 percent of refractory epilepsy patients who accessed CBD-based products cut their seizures in half — but many enrolled patients never received the treatment at all."
tag: "Research"
---

When Brazilian researchers finally sat down to ask patients in the Federal District's public cannabidiol program a simple question — did this actually help you? — the answer, for most who could get the product, was yes. A new real-world study of 91 patients with severe, drug-resistant epilepsy enrolled in the "Canabidiol-SUS" program found that 70.3% reported at least a 50% reduction in seizure frequency, and among those still actively using CBD-based products at the time of the interview, that figure rose to 94.7%. But the same study, published in the journal *Clinical Therapeutics*, delivers a less encouraging finding alongside the hopeful one: access to the program, not the drug's apparent effect, was the biggest obstacle patients faced. For a region where public cannabis-derived medicine programs remain rare and epilepsy care is frequently rationed through courtrooms rather than clinics, that combination — real benefit, real barriers — is the more important story.

## What Brazilian Researchers Found

The study, led by Mariana Dias Lula and colleagues at the Universidade Federal de Minas Gerais, in collaboration with researchers linked to Brazil's health regulatory agency, Anvisa, and the Université de Bordeaux, tracked patients enrolled between 2017 and 2022 in a Federal District health department program that dispenses CBD-based products to people with refractory epilepsy — seizures that persist despite adequate trials of standard anti-seizure medications. Researchers conducted structured interviews between October 2023 and February 2024; because most participants were children or adolescents, 89% of those interviews were completed by parents or legal guardians reporting on the patient's behalf.

Beyond the headline seizure-reduction figures, a few details stand out. Patients who had used cannabis-based products for more than 12 months were dramatically more likely to report benefit — the odds ratio for effectiveness in this group was 23.79, suggesting either a genuine cumulative effect or that patients who saw no benefit dropped out earlier. Adverse events were common: 51.6% of patients reported at least one, with sleep disturbances the most frequent complaint (28.6%). A small but clinically important minority — 4.4% — reported their seizures got worse, not better. Most patients used CBD isolate products (73.6%), while about a fifth used formulations combining CBD with other cannabinoids, reflecting Brazil's patchwork market of compounded and imported products rather than a single standardized medicine.

## Real-World Data Is Not the Same as a Clinical Trial

It matters that this is what researchers call "real-world evidence," not a randomized controlled trial, and the authors are explicit about that limitation. There was no placebo arm, no blinding, and outcomes relied on caregivers' and patients' own recollection of seizure frequency rather than seizure diaries verified against EEG or medical records. The products used were not uniform — dosing, cannabinoid ratios, and manufacturing quality varied across the "CBD isolate" and "CBD with other cannabinoids" categories, which the authors themselves flag as a source of heterogeneity.

That distinction matters because it echoes a pattern seen elsewhere in cannabidiol research: open-label and observational studies of CBD for epilepsy tend to report considerably higher response rates than the placebo-controlled trials that led to CBD's approval for Dravet and Lennox-Gastaut syndromes, where blinded trials — including the pivotal Dravet syndrome trial published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* — found roughly 40% median seizure reduction against a meaningful placebo response of its own. Caregiver-reported outcomes are also vulnerable to expectation and reporting bias, particularly in a program patients had to actively seek out and often pay for. None of this means the Federal District findings are wrong; it means they should be read as a signal worth investigating further with more rigorous designs, not as proof of a specific effect size.

## The Access Gap Is the Real Finding

The more striking result may be a companion analysis from the same research group, presented at a health-technology conference, examining a related cohort of 101 patients in the same Federal District program. It found that although 80.2% of requests for CBD products were formally authorized, 58.4% of enrolled patients never actually received the product through the public health system. Nearly seven in ten of those who did use CBD paid for it out of pocket rather than receiving it free through the program that was supposed to provide it, and two-thirds of patients who discontinued treatment cited product shortages or their own financial limits as the reason.

This gap between legal authorization and actual delivery is not new in Brazil. A separate 2023 study in *Cadernos de Saúde Pública*, led by some of the same researchers, examined more than 1,100 lawsuits filed between 2019 and 2022 by patients seeking court orders to force the public system to provide cannabidiol — nearly half of them for epilepsy. Anvisa has since loosened some rules, including a 2026 resolution expanding pathways for compounding pharmacies to prepare CBD formulations, but this new data suggests that formal eligibility for a public program does not translate reliably into a patient actually holding the medication.

## What It Means for Patients Across Latin America

Brazil is one of the few countries in the region running a government-administered dispensing program for cannabis-derived medicine at all, which is precisely why this data carries weight beyond its borders. Argentina's REPROCANN registry, Colombia's prescription framework, and Mexico's evolving cannabis rules each grant some form of legal access, but none functions as a supply chain the way Brazil's SUS-linked program aspires to. Most Latin American patients with refractory epilepsy who want to try CBD still face the same combination documented here: a legal green light and a practical red one, often bridged only by private purchase, importation paperwork, or a judge's order.

The clinical message is cautiously encouraging: for a subset of Brazilian children and adults with the hardest-to-treat epilepsies, cannabis-based products delivered meaningful, caregiver-perceived relief, and did so within a real public program rather than a boutique private clinic. But this study should not be mistaken for proof that CBD outperforms established anti-seizure medications, nor extrapolated to other conditions. Latin America still lacks its own blinded, placebo-controlled trials of cannabis-based epilepsy treatment — the kind of study that could confirm or temper what this real-world data suggests. Until then, the most concrete, actionable finding here is not about pharmacology at all: a public program only helps the patients who can actually get what it promises them, and for more than half the cohort studied, that promise went unmet.
