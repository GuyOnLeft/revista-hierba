In 1989, researchers working around La Chorrera, in Colombia's Amazonas department, catalogued 51 distinct varieties of yuca — cassava — grown by Uitoto families in their chagras, the mixed forest gardens that double as pharmacy, pantry, and classroom. A recent survey in the same territory found 18. Two-thirds of that varietal memory, encoded in seeds, planting calendars, and the vocabulary needed to tell one root from another, is simply gone. It is the kind of quiet, undramatic loss that rarely makes headlines, which is exactly why a new study published July 8 in the journal Nature matters: it is the first attempt to put a number on how much of this is happening across the entire Amazon basin, and how much more is coming.

The paper, "The Forest of Knowledge Under Global Change," led by ethnobotanist Rodrigo Cámara-Leret of the University of Zurich, with Jordi Bascompte (also at Zurich) and Patrick Roehrdanz of Conservation International, is not a story about one plant. It is a story about an entire knowledge system — the accumulated, orally transmitted expertise of more than 400 Indigenous groups speaking 82 languages — and about what climate change stands to do to it. For a magazine section built on the premise that the plants that heal deserve the same scrutiny as any other subject of science, it is hard to find a more directly relevant piece of research.

## A Third of the Forest's Pharmacy at Risk

The team built the largest dataset of its kind: 90,536 individual use-records drawn from 700 references spanning five centuries, from 16th-century chronicles to contemporary ethnobotanical surveys. Cross-referencing that record against distribution models for 8,429 plant species, they found that Amazonian societies use at least 5,796 native species — more than a third of the basin's known flora, comparing 4,509 utilized species against 3,920 that show no documented use at all.

Running those species through three IPCC climate scenarios, the researchers project that Indigenous communities could lose 28% to 34% of the plant species they currently rely on by 2060–2080, with associated ecosystem services — food, medicine, construction materials, ritual objects — shrinking by 18% to 23%. Layer in the parallel, well-documented erosion of Indigenous languages, and the study estimates the Amazon's combined biocultural heritage — the living link between species and the people who know what to do with them — could contract by roughly 26% by century's end.

"The plants that Indigenous communities rely on could be decimated more severely than previously thought," Cámara-Leret said in comments distributed by the University of Zurich. "The climate tipping point for Amazonia will not only impact biological diversity but cascade across the unique cultural heritage of the biome." Roehrdanz, whose Moore Center for Science at Conservation International supplied the climate modeling, framed the finding as a warning against treating biodiversity loss and cultural loss as separate problems: "The future of biodiversity and the future of Indigenous knowledge are deeply connected, and neither can be fully understood in isolation."

## Coca, Tobacco, and the Mambeadero

What makes the study land differently from a standard deforestation report is its insistence that plant knowledge is not a static inventory but a living practice, tied to specific places of transmission. In much of the Colombian, Peruvian, and Brazilian Amazon, that place is the mambeadero — the space, often inside a maloca, where coca leaf is toasted and ground into mambe and tobacco is prepared as ambil, and where elders pass botanical, medicinal, and cosmological knowledge to younger generations through the shared, ritualized use of both plants. Lose the plants, or lose fluent speakers of the languages in which that knowledge is encoded, and the mambeadero itself becomes harder to sustain — which is the mechanism the study is trying to quantify rather than merely describe.

Researchers from Colombia's Instituto Sinchi, which studies the Colombian Amazon and contributed local fieldwork referenced around the study's release, described the stakes in blunt practical terms. "The loss of these fundamental plants would affect medicinal knowledge, cultivation methods, diets that guarantee food security, and the economic opportunities that currently exist around them," Sinchi researcher José Guerrero Rolón told the Colombian newspaper El Tiempo, which covered the study with reporting grounded in the La Chorrera case and interviews with Uitoto researcher Delio Mendoza Hernández. That reporting also noted roughly 60 Indigenous peoples inhabit the Colombian Amazon alone, each maintaining its own subset of this botanical vocabulary — meaning the erosion documented in one territory is not necessarily replicated, or recoverable, in the next.

## What the Models Can't Fully Capture

The study's authors are careful to frame their numbers as a floor, not a ceiling, on what is at stake. Cacao is one of their sharper examples: wild Amazonian cacao populations carry genetic resistance to witches' broom disease, a fungal blight that has devastated commercial cacao yields elsewhere in the hemisphere — resistance genes that plant breeders have already drawn on and that depend on wild populations surviving in situ. Peach palm, a staple starch across much of the western Amazon known regionally as chontaduro or pupunha, remains, in the researchers' assessment, strikingly under-studied relative to its potential as a climate-resilient crop. And the collaborative model the study's authors point to as a way forward — not extraction, but partnership — has already produced results: participatory fieldwork with the Cacua people in the Colombian-Brazilian border region led to the formal scientific description of a previously unknown canopy palm species, discovered because Cacua collaborators knew exactly where to look for a plant that botanists, working alone, had missed for decades.

That example cuts against a temptation this kind of story invites: treating traditional knowledge as either mystical wisdom to be romanticized or raw data to be mined. The Nature paper resists both. It treats Indigenous botanical knowledge as what it is — a rigorously accumulated, testable body of empirical observation, built over centuries of trial, error, and transmission, that happens to be encoded in oral tradition rather than journal articles. The 26% projected contraction in biocultural heritage is not a metaphor. It is a measurable reduction in humanity's working knowledge of a flora that has already yielded quinine, curare-derived muscle relaxants, and the leads behind a meaningful share of modern pharmacology — with an unknown fraction of its therapeutic potential still undescribed, embedded in vocabularies and practices that this study suggests are eroding faster than previously assumed.

For a Latin American readership, the finding is not abstract. It concerns forests, languages, and gardens inside the region's own borders, tended by communities whose knowledge systems are rarely treated with the same institutional weight as the laboratories now racing to catalogue what those systems already know.
