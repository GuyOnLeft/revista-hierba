Ask any of the many women across Latin America living with fibromyalgia how long it took to get a diagnosis, and the answer is rarely quick. A survey of nearly 900 patients and more than 1,800 physicians in Argentina, Brazil, and Mexico found that Latin American patients waited an average of 42 months between the onset of symptoms and a formal diagnosis — more than eleven months longer than patients surveyed in Europe. Once diagnosed, the treatment menu is thin: antidepressants, anticonvulsants, graded exercise, physical therapy, and, for a small and growing number of patients in countries like Argentina, medical cannabis obtained through programs such as REPROCANN. Until now, the clinical evidence behind that last option has been thinner than patients might hope. A new placebo-controlled trial, published in the journal *Pain Research and Management*, adds a rare piece of rigorous data to the picture — with real caveats attached.

## A Small, Careful Trial

The study was led by Inna Kurlyandchik together with colleagues Janet Schloss, Evelin Tiralongo, Jennifer Ng, Gerald Tracey, and Romy Lauche, researchers based at Southern Cross University's natural medicine research unit, Griffith University, and Gold Coast University Hospital in Australia. It was designed, first and foremost, as a feasibility and safety study: could a trial of this kind even be run, and would the treatment be tolerated? Efficacy was treated as a secondary, preliminary question — a distinction the authors are careful to preserve in their own conclusions.

Researchers recruited adults who met the 2016 American College of Rheumatology diagnostic criteria for fibromyalgia. Recruitment ran from August 2023 through January 2024, with treatment and follow-up concluding by mid-2024; the paper appeared in print in 2026. The trial had originally aimed to enroll 36 participants but closed with 24, all of them women — a recruitment shortfall the authors attribute in part to Australia's legal and logistical barriers to cannabis research, and one that limits how far the findings can be generalized, including to men with fibromyalgia.

Participants were randomly assigned, under double-blind conditions, to receive either a plant-derived oil containing equal concentrations of THC and CBD (10 mg/mL of each) or a matched placebo oil. Dosing followed a four-week titration period, allowing participants to gradually find a tolerable dose, followed by twelve weeks at a stable dose — sixteen weeks of treatment in total.

## Pain, Sleep, and What Moved

On the trial's primary aims, the results were encouraging: retention was high, with 22 of the 24 participants completing the study, and adherence exceeded 90 percent of prescribed doses in both arms. Adverse events were common but mild — somnolence, dizziness, fatigue, nausea, and dry mouth were the most frequently reported — and no serious adverse events occurred in either group.

On the secondary, efficacy-related outcomes, the differences between groups were larger than a strict feasibility trial might have anticipated. Seventy percent of participants receiving the THC:CBD oil achieved a clinically meaningful reduction in pain — defined as at least a 30 percent drop in average daily pain scores — compared with 20 percent of the placebo group after the titration phase and 40 percent by week twelve. On the Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire, a standard measure of how the condition affects daily functioning, 40 percent of the treatment group showed a clinically meaningful improvement, compared with 10 percent on placebo. Participants receiving cannabis also reported improved sleep quality, reduced bodily pain, and better social functioning relative to those on placebo. Notably, the oil did not produce statistically meaningful improvements in fatigue, anxiety, or depression — a reminder that fibromyalgia's symptom cluster does not respond uniformly to any single intervention.

## What This Trial Does Not Show

The authors themselves are explicit about the limits of what they found. With only 24 participants, the confidence intervals around every effect estimate are wide, and the study was not statistically powered to detect efficacy with certainty — it was built to test whether a larger trial was feasible, not to settle the question of whether THC:CBD oil works for fibromyalgia. The all-female sample, a consequence of who enrolled rather than a deliberate design choice, means the results cannot yet be extended to men, who represent a minority but not a negligible share of fibromyalgia patients globally.

That caution fits into a broader pattern in the cannabis-and-chronic-pain literature. An updated systematic review published in the *Annals of Internal Medicine* in 2026 found that evidence for cannabis-based products remains strongest for neuropathic pain and multiple sclerosis-related spasticity, while results for fibromyalgia, osteoarthritis, and other musculoskeletal pain conditions have historically been inconsistent across studies — some finding modest benefit, others finding none. Against that backdrop, a well-designed, placebo-controlled trial with a clear, prespecified pain outcome is a meaningful, if incremental, contribution — precisely because so much of the existing fibromyalgia-and-cannabis literature has relied on observational surveys and patient-reported data rather than randomized comparisons.

The authors' own conclusion is measured: the intervention was feasible and well tolerated, the preliminary efficacy signals were promising enough to justify further investigation, and a larger, adequately powered trial — one with enough participants of both sexes to detect a real effect with confidence — is the necessary next step.

## Why It Matters in Latin America

Fibromyalgia disproportionately affects women everywhere, with global estimates putting female prevalence at roughly 4.2 percent, compared with 1.4 percent in men. A population-based study of chronic pain in Brazil found fibromyalgia prevalence of around 2 percent, with women affected at a ratio of roughly 5.5 to 1 over men — figures broadly consistent with patterns reported elsewhere in the region. Combined with the diagnostic delays documented across Argentina, Brazil, and Mexico, the disease represents a substantial and under-addressed source of chronic pain for women in Latin America, many of whom have already cycled through years of medications with limited relief before anyone raises the possibility of cannabis-based treatment.

That is precisely why the quality of evidence matters. In Argentina, patients with fibromyalgia can already access medical cannabis through the REPROCANN registry, and clinicians elsewhere in the region increasingly field questions about it — but most public health systems in Latin America have no formal pathway for prescribing cannabis specifically for fibromyalgia, and coverage, where it exists, is inconsistent and often out of pocket. Decisions about whether to expand or formalize that access, in Argentina or anywhere else, should rest on trials designed to withstand scrutiny — not only on patient testimony, however sincere, or industry-funded claims. This pilot trial does not resolve that question. But it is the kind of study that needs to be replicated at scale, with larger and more diverse samples, before medical cannabis for fibromyalgia can be recommended with real confidence rather than cautious hope.
