When a patient with advanced cancer in Bogotá, Mexico City or São Paulo asks a doctor whether cannabis oil will ease their pain, nausea, insomnia or anxiety, the honest answer today rests less on placebo-controlled evidence than on prescription registries and accumulated clinical experience. The most rigorous trial yet to test that claim — randomized, placebo-controlled, triple-blind — was run entirely in Canada, and Latin America has nothing equivalent. A preprint posted June 2, 2026 on the medRxiv repository, drawing on data from eight Canadian cancer centers, offers the best-designed evaluation so far of an assumption repeated across the region's medical-cannabis clinics: that cannabis extracts relieve cancer-related symptoms. The results are real, but they are also more modest and more nuanced than the headlines already circulating in Spanish-language cannabis media suggest.

## What the trial actually did

The study, registered as NCT03948074 on ClinicalTrials.gov under the name CAFCARS ("Cannabis For Cancer-Related Symptoms"), enrolled 89 clinically stable but symptomatic cancer outpatients treated at sites including BC Cancer, the University of British Columbia, the University of Manitoba, the University of Ottawa and Queen's University. Instead of a conventional two-arm design, the researchers used an "aggregate n-of-1" format: each participant tried, in randomized sequence and blinded to which one they were receiving at any given time, three sublingual extracts — one THC-dominant, one CBD-dominant, and a balanced 1:1 blend — plus a placebo, in four-day cycles per formulation, repeated for up to three cycles (16 to 48 days total). By having each patient serve as their own control, the design tries to capture something conventional trials tend to average away: how much cannabis response varies from one person to the next.

The primary outcome was defined as at least a 1.4-point (20%) improvement on a seven-point Patient Global Impression of Change (PGIC) scale for at least one active extract over placebo. That threshold was met in 50 of the 89 participants with complete data — 56% — with a p-value below 0.001, indicating the aggregate finding is statistically robust rather than a product of chance.

## Real relief, but no single winner

This is where enthusiasm needs a check before it turns into a promise. All three active extracts outperformed placebo on average, but none was clearly superior across the full patient group: response rates were 50% among those whose primary symptom was pain, 47% among those with sleep disturbance, and 60% among those prioritizing anxiety. According to coverage of the preprint by the specialty outlet The Marijuana Herald and the Spanish-language site Cannabis Magazine, improvements in sleep, anxiety and daytime tiredness contributed most to the overall benefit. Mild adverse effects — drowsiness, dizziness — were common across all conditions, including placebo, and typically resolved quickly; moderate-to-severe effects were rare, though more associated with THC-containing formulations.

The authors' own framing, as reflected in that coverage, is not "cannabis works for cancer" but something narrower and clinically more useful: response appears to depend on individual patient profile, which opens the door to personalizing which extract to try first rather than defaulting to a single formulation for everyone. That is a genuinely useful clinical conclusion — but it falls well short of establishing cannabis as a standard treatment with predictable efficacy.

## The Latin American gap

No Latin American center took part in this trial, and the region has no study of equivalent design — randomized, blinded, placebo-controlled — on cannabis and cancer-related symptoms. What does exist is a different kind of evidence: a retrospective cohort study published in 2022 in Frontiers in Pain Research examined 2,112 chronic pain patients in Colombia treated with two oral cannabis formulations (a CBD-dominant one, used in 59.5% of prescriptions, and a balanced THC/CBD one, in 39.8%). Of those patients, 92.7% reported clinical improvement, with an average pain reduction of 54.7%, and mild adverse effects — drowsiness, dizziness, dry mouth — in a minority of cases. That is valuable real-world data, drawn from thousands of patients over meaningful follow-up periods, but it is methodologically a different animal: without a placebo arm, blinding, or randomization, it cannot isolate how much of the reported relief comes from the drug itself versus other factors, including placebo response — which, in the Canadian trial itself, turned out to be far from negligible.

This is not an academic technicality. It defines how far the available evidence can actually stretch to justify clinical decisions or public policy in countries such as Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, Peru or Colombia itself, where medical-cannabis regulatory frameworks have existed for several years but are rarely matched by the research infrastructure — ethics committees experienced with blinded trials, public funding, multicenter networks — needed to produce data of the caliber CAFCARS just generated.

## What this means for patients and doctors in the region

The reasonable conclusion — and the one this publication's editorial standards require — is not that cannabis "cures" or "treats" cancer. The Canadian trial did not study the tumor; it studied symptoms associated with the disease and its treatment. Nor is the finding settled: this is a preprint, not yet peer-reviewed, with a modest sample of 89 people, and a design that, while rigorous, does not substitute for independent replication. What it does offer is a clinically honest signal: for roughly half of patients with advanced cancer, some cannabis extract — not necessarily the same one for everyone — appears to provide relief superior to placebo for sleep, anxiety and pain, with a manageable short-term safety profile.

For Latin American patients already using medical cannabis under prescription, the takeaway is not to abandon what is working, but to understand its evidentiary limits: cohort data like Colombia's supports clinical experience, it does not substitute for scientific certainty. And for the region's health systems and regulators, the Canadian trial leaves a clear item of unfinished business: without trials of their own — accounting for local populations, genetics, and use patterns — decisions about cannabis in oncology across Latin America will keep being made, largely, on borrowed evidence.
