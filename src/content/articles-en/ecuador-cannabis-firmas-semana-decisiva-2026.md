---
title: "Ecuador Against the Clock: Cannabis Reform Bill Faces Its Final Week for Signatures"
excerpt: "Ecuador is in the final week of a 90-day deadline to gather 34,136 signatures for a patient- and doctor-backed bill on medical cannabis access and home cultivation."
tag: "Legislation"
---

In Quito this week, a small team of volunteers is doing something that will feel familiar to cannabis patients across Latin America: racing a bureaucratic clock to prove that enough citizens want a law that already exists on paper. Ecuador's proposed cannabis regulation bill is not stalled in a legislative committee or awaiting a judge's signature, as similar reforms have in Mexico, Brazil and Colombia. It is stalled at a signature-counting machine, five months after it cleared its first legislative hurdle, and organizers say they are now in the final days of a 90-day extension to prove their support is real. For a region where patient access to cannabis-derived medicine has advanced mostly through courtrooms and executive decrees, Ecuador's experiment with direct democracy is a rare test of whether citizens, not judges, can be the ones to force reform.

## A law born from patients, not politicians

The bill — the Ley Orgánica para la Regulación Integral del Uso Responsable de Cannabis en Ecuador — did not originate in the National Assembly. It began as a "popular normative initiative," a constitutional mechanism that lets citizens draft and submit legislation directly, provided they can prove sufficient public backing. The Red de Acción Cannábica del Ecuador (Redcan), a network of cannabis organizations and patient advocates, filed the proposal on August 21, 2025, through common proponent Carlos Andrés Albuja Terán, delivering close to 20,000 signatures gathered by volunteers across the country.

Medical professionals were central to building the case. Doctors from the Fundación Ecuatoriana de Medicina Cannábica (FEMCA), including vice president Cristina Martínez and cannabis specialist Isabel Espinosa, argued publicly that patients "have the right to choose what medicine they are treated with," according to Edición Médica, and that Ecuador's existing rules — which allow only low-THC CBD products — leave out people whose conditions require stronger formulations. The bill's medical provisions specifically target joint and post-surgical pain, cancer-related symptoms, neurodegenerative diseases, epilepsy, nausea and vomiting linked to HIV treatment, fibromyalgia and spasticity, with access tied to a prescription and a patient registry rather than open retail sale.

Beyond medicine, the bill would let adults 18 and older cultivate up to 30 plants at home for personal, non-commercial use, and would create a new autonomous agency, the Instituto Nacional de Regulación del Cannabis, to oversee licensing across the entire chain — from cultivation to dispensaries, cannabis clubs and medical shops. Organizers frame it explicitly as a public-safety measure as much as a health one, arguing that a regulated market would undercut illegal trafficking profits and let the state control product quality, rather than leaving consumers dependent on the black market.

On April 7, 2026, the Assembly's Qualifying Commission — chaired by assemblymember Jhajaira Urresta — found the initiative met the constitutional admissibility requirements, and the full plenary voted 142 in favor to formally admit it for processing, according to the National Assembly's own records. That vote did not approve the law itself; it simply opened the next stage, in which the National Electoral Council (Consejo Nacional Electoral, CNE) must independently verify that the signatures backing the initiative are genuine and sufficient.

## The shortfall that reset the clock

That verification did not go the organizers' way. On April 25, 2026, the CNE's five-member board — council president Diana Atamaint and councilors José Merino, Elena Nájera, José Cabrera and Enrique Pita — voted unanimously to approve a technical report finding that only 11,134 of the submitted signatures were valid, according to reporting by El Universo, Primicias and El Comercio. The legal threshold is 0.25% of Ecuador's national electoral registry, which the CNE calculated at 34,136 valid signatures. The initiative was therefore short by roughly 23,000 signatures — a shortfall of about two-thirds of the requirement.

"La legitimación ciudadana debe contar con no menos del 0,25% de los registros del padrón electoral," councilor Enrique Pita said in explaining the requirement, according to El Universo's account of the session — a formula that translates to needing broad, geographically distributed support rather than a concentrated push in any one city. Rather than dismissing the initiative outright, the council granted Albuja Terán a 90-day extension, counted from the date of formal notification, to collect and submit the missing signatures, a process confirmed by the Spanish wire agency EFE in its report carried by SWI swissinfo.ch. That window is what has now brought the campaign to its final stretch in mid-to-late July.

## A campaign in its "final week"

According to Redcan's own public updates, checked in mid-July, the renewed signature drive has collected more than 28,000 signatures during the extension period, with volunteers mobilized across Ecuador's 24 provinces and organizers describing the current stretch as the campaign's "final week" before the CNE's deadline closes. That figure, however, comes with an important caveat: it reflects signatures collected by the campaign, not signatures certified by the CNE. The council's April ruling is a reminder of how large that gap can be — of an earlier batch of roughly 20,000 signatures submitted in August 2025, only 11,134, or a little over half, survived verification. If a similar attrition rate applies this time, a raw count near 28,000 would not guarantee that the certified total clears 34,136. The only verified number the public currently has is the one the CNE published in April; the final, audited count will not be known until the council rules again after the extension closes.

If the organizers do clear the threshold, the bill does not automatically become law. Under the process described by the National Assembly and confirmed by EFE, the initiative returns to the legislature, which then has a defined period — reported as 180 days by the wire service — to debate the proposal in committee and in two plenary rounds, with Redcan's representatives entitled to participate directly in those debates. If the signature drive fails again, organizers would likely have to restart the entire popular-initiative process from the beginning, since none of the sources consulted describe a further extension beyond the 90 days already granted.

## Why it matters beyond Ecuador

Ecuador's cannabis debate sits inside a broader Latin American pattern in which patients have often won ground faster through courts than through legislatures — as in Brazil, where a Superior Tribunal de Justiça ruling forced regulators to act, or in Mexico, where the Supreme Court, not Congress, has driven every major change to personal cannabis rules. Ecuador's organizers are deliberately testing a different, slower and arguably more fragile path: asking ordinary citizens to sign their names, province by province, to force their own Assembly to debate the issue. The bill's home-cultivation cap of 30 plants echoes provisions already in place in Argentina's REPROCANN program and in Uruguay's cannabis clubs, suggesting organizers are borrowing deliberately from neighboring frameworks rather than proposing something untested in the region.

For now, the outcome hinges on a number: how many of the signatures gathered in the past 90 days will actually survive the CNE's verification process. Ecuador's patients, and the doctors who have spent a year building the medical case for them, will know only when the council rules again — a decision expected in the days immediately following the deadline closing this month.
