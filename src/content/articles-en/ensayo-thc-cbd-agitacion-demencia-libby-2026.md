Nearly nine out of ten patients with advanced dementia and severe agitation improved after receiving a purified oral THC-CBD formulation, according to results from the LiBBY trial presented July 14 at the Alzheimer's Association International Conference in London. It is, by the researchers' own description, the highest response rate ever recorded in a dementia clinical trial. The finding arrives as Latin America faces accelerated population aging and a supply of palliative care still insufficient to meet the projected rise in dementia cases.

Agitation — restlessness, aggression, shouting, resistance to care — is one of the most distressing symptoms of advanced-stage dementia, for patients and caregivers alike. Currently available drugs, mainly antipsychotics, have limited efficacy and carry serious risks in older adults, including increased mortality. That lack of safe options is the clinical problem the LiBBY trial — short for "Life's End Benefits of cannaBidiol and tetrahYdrocannabinol" — set out to address.

## The trial design

LiBBY was a Phase II, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, funded by the National Institute on Aging (NIA, part of the U.S. National Institutes of Health) and coordinated by the Alzheimer's Clinical Trial Consortium (ACTC), an academic network led by the University of Southern California's Alzheimer's Therapeutic Research Institute, with participation from the Medical University of South Carolina and Georgetown University.

The trial enrolled 120 people with Alzheimer's disease or other forms of dementia who were hospice-eligible, with a mean age of 80 and documented agitation. The study ran across ten U.S. medical centers, with visits conducted in participants' own homes or residences — a design meant to avoid requiring travel from people in a terminal stage of illness. Half the participants received an oral suspension of purified THC and CBD in calibrated ratios, dissolved in a fast-acting oil; the other half received an identical-appearing placebo. Neither patients, caregivers, nor clinical evaluators knew which group each participant was assigned to.

Agitation was measured using the Cohen-Mansfield Agitation Inventory, a 29-item behavioral scale rated on a seven-point frequency score, widely used in geriatric research. The primary outcome was assessed at two weeks, with additional follow-up through twelve weeks.

## Results: a response rarely seen

At two weeks, the group receiving THC-CBD showed a 6.27-point greater average reduction in agitation scores than the placebo group. Measured by clinical global impression of change — a clinician-rated assessment of improvement — 83.9% of those on active treatment showed improvement, compared with 30.5% in the placebo group. At twelve weeks, the gap held: 87.2% versus 23.6%.

Adverse events — mainly infections and gastrointestinal conditions, common in this older, clinically frail population — were similar between groups (46.7% in the treatment group versus 42.4% in the placebo group), suggesting the safety profile was not meaningfully compromised by the active treatment.

"These trial results were extremely impressive and showed a level of response not seen before in clinical trials related to dementia," said Jacobo Mintzer of the Medical University of South Carolina, the trial's co-lead investigator. "Rarely do we see close to 90% of patients in a trial respond positively," he added. Brigid Reynolds of Georgetown University, co-lead investigator, underscored the scale of the problem the trial addresses: "Agitation affects many people with late-stage dementia, causing symptoms such as restlessness, aggression and emotional distress."

## What this study does not yet say

It's worth being precise about the limits of what was announced. First, the results were presented at a scientific conference, not yet published in a peer-reviewed journal; the figures released — including the 6.27-point reduction and the response rates — come from institutional press releases and the conference presentation itself, not from a publicly available article with full statistical methodology, confidence intervals, and detailed p-values. That does not invalidate the finding, but it does call for caution until the study completes peer review.

Second, and this was explicitly flagged by the researchers themselves: the formulation used in the trial is a purified pharmaceutical product, with exact THC-to-CBD ratios and clinical-grade quality control. "People should not assume that products available at dispensaries or online are equivalent to what was studied in this trial," Reynolds warned. This is a crucial distinction: the result is not evidence that any cannabis product reduces agitation in dementia, but that this specific formulation, in this specific design, did.

Third, the population studied — people eligible for hospice care, in a terminal stage of illness — is a particular subgroup within the broader universe of dementia patients. Extending the conclusion to people with earlier-stage dementia would require additional studies. And while follow-up extended to twelve weeks, that remains relatively short against the course of a progressive neurodegenerative disease.

## What it means for Latin America

The LiBBY trial was conducted entirely in the United States, with no research sites in Latin America. But the problem it addresses — the lack of safe treatments for agitation in advanced dementia — is, if anything, more acute in the region.

According to guidance published in 2026 by the Pan American Health Organization on long-term care, roughly 14.4% of people over 65 in Latin America and the Caribbean — about eight million people — already require this type of care, a share that could reach 16% by 2050. PAHO has also noted that the accelerated pace of population aging in the region, combined with unequal access to health systems, helps explain the rising prevalence of dementia observed in countries such as Peru, Mexico, and Puerto Rico. That demographic pressure compounds limited coverage of specialized services and a scarce, undervalued caregiving workforce.

Against that backdrop, evidence on cannabinoids for the neuropsychiatric symptoms of dementia is not entirely new in the regional literature: a systematic review published in Revista Eugenio Espejo, with authors linked to Ecuador's Ministry of Public Health, had already found in 2023 a reduction in neuropsychiatric symptoms in five of seven clinical trials analyzed, though it warned about the lack of dose standardization across studies. LiBBY, by virtue of its sample size, placebo-controlled design, and the magnitude of the reported effect, represents the most robust study to date in this line of research.

The challenge for the region is not only scientific but regulatory and about access. Countries such as Colombia are advancing regulatory frameworks — Decree 1138 of 2025 instructs INVIMA to define quality standards, cannabinoid standardization, and prescription criteria for medical cannabis — but none yet has a pharmaceutical product equivalent to the one used in LiBBY, with fixed THC-to-CBD ratios and clinical-grade quality control, available for dementia patients. If these results hold up in peer-reviewed publication and eventually in Phase III trials, the question Latin American health systems will face is how to translate a U.S. clinical-laboratory finding into a regulated, affordable, and accessible product for a population aging faster than its care systems are adapting.
