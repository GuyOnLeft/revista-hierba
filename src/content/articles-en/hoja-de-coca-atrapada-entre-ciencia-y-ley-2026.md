title: "Coca Leaf, Caught Between Science and UN Law"

---

In the highlands of Bolivia, Peru, Colombia and Ecuador, millions of people still start the workday the way their ancestors did eight millennia ago: tucking a handful of coca leaves into one cheek, adding a pinch of alkaline ash or lime paste, and letting the leaf slowly release its mild stimulant compounds. The practice, known as acullico, mambeo or chacchado depending on the country, is older than the Inca empire and remains central to Andean and Amazonian life — a mild stimulant against fatigue, hunger and altitude sickness, a social ritual, and an offering to the earth, Pachamama. Yet under international law, the leaf that makes this possible is still classified alongside heroin and fentanyl. In December 2025, the World Health Organization had a rare chance to change that — and largely declined to.

The WHO's Expert Committee on Drug Dependence (ECDD) completed, in October 2025, the first "critical review" of coca leaf since the plant was placed under Schedule I of the 1961 Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs more than seventy years ago. The review had been requested in 2023 by Bolivia, a country where coca cultivation and consumption are constitutionally protected, and backed by Colombia. Both governments argued that grouping a leaf chewed daily by millions of indigenous and mestizo Andeans with cocaine itself was scientifically indefensible and rooted in colonial-era prejudice. On December 10, 2025, the WHO announced its committee's conclusion: coca leaf would stay in Schedule I.

## A review seventy-five years in the making

According to the WHO's own announcement, the ECDD's reasoning rested on the fact that cocaine "can be easily and profitably extracted from coca leaf," combined with concern over a sharp rise in global coca cultivation and cocaine production in recent years. The committee recommended maintaining strict international controls while preserving existing exemptions that let producing countries permit traditional and cultural uses domestically.

Because the WHO did not recommend a change in classification, the matter did not require a vote when the UN Commission on Narcotic Drugs (CND) convened its 69th session in Vienna in March 2026 — under UN treaty procedure, only proposals to change a substance's scheduling go to a vote. The critical review, which took more than two years and generated multiple technical reports and consultations, closed without altering coca's legal status anywhere in the world.

Analysts at the Washington Office on Latin America (WOLA), which has tracked the process closely through its "Coca Chronicles" series, describe the committee's central argument as a "convertibility principle": because cocaine can be extracted from the leaf, coca itself is treated as a controlled substance regardless of the risk posed by the leaf in its natural form. WOLA's analysis notes this reasoning found no evidence that traditional coca use poses a public-health harm, yet leaned on the theoretical risk of extraction to justify the status quo — a legal interpretation critics say lacks solid grounding in the treaty's own text.

## What the science actually says

Editorial rigor requires separating what is established from what is contested. The undisputed chemistry: dried coca leaf contains roughly one percent or less of the cocaine alkaloid by weight, alongside other alkaloids, and a nutrient profile that Andean nutrition researchers have long highlighted — notable levels of calcium, iron, fiber and B vitamins, which is part of why coca-leaf flour and coca tea have been studied in Colombia and Bolivia as potential foods against regional malnutrition rather than as narcotics. Colombia's Ministry of Science, Technology and Innovation (Minciencias) has funded research into these licit product lines — flour, fresh leaf, tea — precisely to build an evidence base for uses distinct from cocaine.

What remains genuinely underexplored, and the WHO committee said as much, is the clinical evidence on the leaf's therapeutic potential beyond traditional use — appetite suppression, altitude adaptation, and mild stimulant effects are well documented anecdotally and through ethnobotanical literature, but rigorous trial data is thin. The committee itself acknowledged the leaf's "cultural and therapeutic significance for Indigenous peoples" and stated that traditional use "does not appear to pose a particularly serious public health risk" — an unusually direct acknowledgment embedded in a decision that nonetheless kept the leaf under the strictest control category. That tension between acknowledged low risk and retained maximum control is the crux of the criticism from drug-policy researchers, including Marie Nougier of the International Drug Policy Consortium (IDPC), who called the review "a key opportunity to correct the historical wrongs of the past" that ultimately fell short.

## Indigenous voices, not a single verdict

It would be a mistake to present this as indigenous communities uniformly demanding descheduling and a WHO uniformly resisting them; the record is more complicated, and flattening it would fall into the same romanticism the topic invites. Former Bolivian vice-president David Choquehuanca, an Aymara leader who has long championed coca's legal recognition, condemned the outcome bluntly: "For six decades we have had our sovereignty as indigenous peoples violated by the United Nations' international prohibition on the coca leaf. For six decades we have suffered discrimination against our legitimate rights to use our sacred coca leaf for nutrition, healing and rituals."

But reporting by WOLA and the Transnational Institute (TNI) on consultations tied to the review found that many indigenous communities in Peru's Amazonian frontier, in particular, are wary of full descheduling — not because they doubt coca's cultural legitimacy, but because in parts of their territory coca cultivation has expanded alongside drug trafficking, displacing subsistence agriculture and fueling violence on indigenous lands. Their concern is less about the leaf itself than about who would profit from a liberalized, potentially commodified coca market — whether local growers and indigenous associations, or agribusiness and intermediaries disconnected from the traditions the plant represents. That divide — between the Andean altiplano, where coca has been legally cultivated and consumed for generations under state-registered quotas, and Amazonian frontier zones grappling with illicit expansion — is central to understanding why "coca policy" cannot be reduced to a single indigenous position.

## What comes next

For now, the practical reality on the ground has changed little. In Bolivia's Cochabamba tropics, one of the country's two legally recognized coca-growing regions, growers reported in July 2026 that increased cultivation and expanded "free sale" channels had driven prices down by nearly half compared to three years earlier — a market dynamic unrelated to the WHO's scheduling decision but a reminder that coca's economics remain volatile and consequential for the families who depend on it. Colombia, meanwhile, continues to lead global coca cultivation even as it reported a 56.9 percent reduction in crops in a January 2026 government count, part of an entirely separate policy track aimed at illicit cultivation rather than traditional use.

Advocates including IDPC and WOLA say the path forward now runs through mechanisms other than the WHO review: national governments invoking human-rights obligations before UN bodies, potential treaty reservations by sympathetic states, and calls for an independent technical panel to examine inconsistencies across the three UN drug conventions. None of that promises a quick resolution. What the coca review does confirm, seventy-five years on, is that the gap between a plant's documented chemistry and cultural role, and the legal architecture built around it in the mid-twentieth century, remains one of the starkest unresolved questions in Latin American drug and indigenous-rights policy.
