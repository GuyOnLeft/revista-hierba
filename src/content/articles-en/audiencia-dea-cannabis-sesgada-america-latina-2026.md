In Bogotá and Montevideo, where medical cannabis exporters spent much of the past decade building pharmaceutical-grade supply chains for a global market that barely existed, executives are now watching what happens inside an administrative courtroom in Arlington, Virginia. There, a U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration judge is presiding over a hearing that could reshape the world's largest cannabis market. The catch, according to critics inside the industry and reform groups alike, is that almost no one testifying in that hearing wants things to change.

## A Hearing Stacked Against Reform

The hearing opened on June 29, 2026, at DEA headquarters in Arlington and, according to the agency's own press release, must conclude no later than July 15. It is presided over by Chief Administrative Law Judge Derek C. Julius, and its formal purpose is to receive factual evidence and expert opinion on whether marijuana should move from Schedule I to Schedule III of the Controlled Substances Act.

The problem, documented by Marijuana Moment and by NORML, is that the DEA announced on June 18 that it had selected seven "designated parties" to present testimony — and all seven oppose broader rescheduling. Reform-minded groups, including NORML, the National Cannabis Industry Association (NCIA), the Drug Policy Alliance, Students for Sensible Drug Policy and several medical organizations, asked to participate and were turned down. NORML board chair Joseph Bondy called the process unfair, arguing, per the organization's own statement, that "a fair administrative process should include the voices of those most directly affected by federal cannabis policy."

Cat Packer, who directs drug markets and legal regulation at the Drug Policy Alliance and previously served as Los Angeles's first cannabis regulation director, wrote in an op-ed published by Marijuana Moment that there was no livestream or public audio feed of the hearing, that public attendance was limited and first-come, first-served, and that she herself was denied entry on the third day under an unexplained restriction. Packer also noted that the early days of testimony included "no discussion of the lasting harms of marijuana criminalization itself" — arrests, convictions, incarceration — and drew an editorially important distinction: "this is not about recreational cannabis. This is about regulation, not legalization." That's worth sitting with: what is being argued in Arlington is, at best, a technical scheduling adjustment, not legalization.

## From an Executive Order to a Narrow Rule

Understanding the hearing requires separating three distinct moments that are easy to conflate. On December 18, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order aimed at expanding medical marijuana and cannabidiol research. On April 23, 2026, the Justice Department and DEA responded with a final order moving exactly two narrow categories to Schedule III: FDA-approved drug products containing marijuana, and marijuana produced under state medical licenses for manufacturing, distribution or dispensing for medical purposes only, according to a legal analysis published by the law firm Foley & Lardner. That order explicitly left out adult-use marijuana — which remains Schedule I even in states where it's legal — along with synthetic THC, hemp and unlicensed activity.

The hearing running through July 15 is the mechanism the DEA itself has described as a "legally compliant pathway" to evaluate whether to go further than that narrow order and reschedule marijuana broadly. It's worth keeping the hierarchy of evidence straight here: the original rescheduling recommendation came from a scientific and administrative review by the Department of Health and Human Services in 2023; the April 2026 order was an executive policy decision; and what's happening now in Arlington is a contested proceeding presenting testimony — not necessarily new scientific evidence — from parties with declared interests.

## The Treaty Argument Latin America Already Broke

The DEA's official rationale for caution rests partly on U.S. obligations under the 1961 Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs, which requires import and export permits and manufacturing quotas for cannabis even under Schedule III. It's an argument with an obvious tension: cannabis-focused legal commentators have pointed out that several signatories to that same treaty — Uruguay since 2013, Canada, and de facto decriminalization and home-cultivation regimes in Mexico and elsewhere in the region — already sit in open friction with it. That's not a settled legal question so much as an active debate among lawyers and scholars over how much flexibility Article 36 of the convention actually allows, but it illustrates that "treaty compliance" isn't a standard Washington applies to itself the way it expects others to.

That asymmetry isn't a minor detail for the region: Colombia, Uruguay and, to a lesser extent, Argentina and Paraguay already built medical cannabis regulatory frameworks with traceability, good manufacturing practices (GMP) and state-controlled quotas — exactly the kind of infrastructure the United States would have to build from scratch if it moves toward a broader Schedule III regime.

## What a Narrower Door Means for Latin American Exporters

This is where some enthusiasm needs tempering. Even if rescheduling expanded, only FDA-approved, prescription-grade formulations would qualify for the new import-export pathway — not the flower or extracts most of the Latin American industry currently exports, according to the legal analyses reviewed for this piece. Colombia, which exported $10.8 million in medical cannabis in 2023 per reporting by El Tiempo, also faces a separate headwind: a new U.S. hemp law (known as H.R. 5371) that redefines hemp and takes effect in November 2026, restricting full-spectrum products above 0.4 milligrams of THC per container. The same reporting notes this could dent demand for some Colombian products while opening space for high-purity CBD isolates, if the country strengthens its GMP certifications and negotiates regulatory-equivalency agreements with Washington.

Meanwhile, industry isn't waiting for the Arlington hearing to resolve. On July 9, Tilray Medical announced the commercial launch of a medical cannabis product in Panama — a CBD oral solution manufactured in Portugal under EU-GMP standards and distributed through the Farmacias Arrocha pharmacy chain — building on Panama's 2021 medical cannabis law and a May 2026 Health Ministry resolution, according to the company's own announcement carried by GlobeNewswire. That announcement is worth reading for what it is: a corporate press release, useful as a signal of where capital is moving, but not independent evidence of clinical or market impact.

What is clear is that the hearing closing on July 15 is, above all, a story about process: who gets a voice, what counts as evidence, and how narrow the door actually is that's being debated. For the region's medical exporters, who have spent years operating under standards Washington is only now beginning to argue about, the lesson isn't that the U.S. market has opened — it's that the door remains half-open, and the next move depends as much on a final DEA rule as on the legal fights already gathering around it.
