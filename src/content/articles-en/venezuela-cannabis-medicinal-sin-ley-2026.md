---
title: "In Venezuela, There Is No Medical Cannabis Program — Only the Threat of Prison"
excerpt: "While neighbors from Colombia to Argentina built medical cannabis frameworks, Venezuela has none. Patients with epilepsy, cancer, or chronic pain face a drug law that treats their medicine as contraband — with prison as the only guarantee."
tag: "Rights"
---

Across Latin America, medical cannabis has become a fraught but real legal category: patients register, doctors prescribe, courts intervene, and governments argue over the details. In Venezuela, there is no such argument to have, because there is no such category. The country's Organic Law of Drugs makes no provision for a patient, a prescription, or a registered product. A Venezuelan with epilepsy, cancer-related pain, or multiple sclerosis who uses cannabis to manage symptoms is, by the letter of the law, indistinguishable from a trafficker — and the government has said, as recently as September 2025, that it intends to keep it that way. The stakes are not abstract: they fall on patients who already live inside a health system that Human Rights Watch describes as unable to reliably supply ordinary medicine, let alone a controlled one.

## A law with no room for a patient

Venezuela's Organic Law of Drugs, in force since 2010, criminalizes cannabis cultivation, distribution, and possession above small thresholds, according to legal-status summaries compiled by the cannabis-law trackers Leafwell and MyCannabis.com. There is no licensing pathway for a doctor to prescribe cannabis, no patient registry, and no pharmacy authorized to dispense it — a stark contrast with the frameworks, however uneven, that Colombia, Argentina, Uruguay, Brazil, Mexico, and Peru have each built over the past decade. Possession above roughly 20 grams of cannabis (or 5 grams of a genetically modified variant) can bring one to two years in prison if a judge decides it was not for personal use; smaller amounts can be redirected into compulsory “security measures” — mandatory rehabilitation or detoxification — rather than prosecution, according to the same trackers. That diversion is not a legal accommodation for patients; it is a coercive alternative to prison, built on the premise that anyone found with cannabis is presumptively an addict or a criminal, never a patient.

The gap is not a matter of legislative neglect. In December 2020, when the United Nations Commission on Narcotic Drugs voted, following a World Health Organization recommendation, to remove cannabis from Schedule IV of the 1961 Single Convention — the category reserved for the most dangerous, least medically useful drugs — 27 member states voted in favor, according to UN News. Venezuela was among the minority that voted no, alongside Cuba, Russia, China, Pakistan and Hungary: a bloc of mostly prohibitionist states, several of them Venezuela's closest diplomatic partners. Bogotá, Buenos Aires, Brasília, Mexico City, Montevideo and Lima have each built at least a partial medical-cannabis framework since; Caracas has built none.

## The politics behind the prohibition

Venezuelan officials have been explicit that this is not an oversight but a choice. President Nicolás Maduro told international media on September 15, 2025, that cannabis “sigue siendo ilegal y va a seguir siendo ilegal” — “remains illegal and will remain illegal” — and that Venezuela “no vamos a caer en la moda insana de legalizar cualquier droga” (“will not fall into the insane fashion of legalizing any drug”), according to the outlet La Patilla, which covered the press conference. Maduro made the remarks after Brazilian authorities intercepted a plane originating in Venezuela carrying a marijuana shipment, using the episode to criticize United States cannabis and synthetic-drug policy rather than to engage with the medical-access debate underway elsewhere in the region.

Analysts who track the region's cannabis industry describe the government's posture as driven less by pharmacology than by foreign-policy alignment — an interpretation, not a proven fact about internal government deliberations. Alfredo Pascual, a cannabis-industry researcher, has attributed Venezuela's holdout to “a mixture of political, cultural and historical” factors that keep cannabis officially classified as dangerous and taboo, according to the outlet El Diario, and noted that Venezuela forgoes potential foreign-currency revenue from a legal cannabis sector — money the country badly needs — largely because investors avoid its political instability. Tito López, president of Venezuela's pharmaceutical industry chamber, told the same outlet that the government still treats cannabis as a “dangerous substance” domestically, in a political climate where any drug-related policy is entangled with trafficking accusations against the state itself. These are the assessments of industry-adjacent analysts and insiders, not independently verified claims about government motive — but they describe a real, observable outcome: no reform has moved in Venezuela, even as the rest of the region shifted around it.

## Prohibition layered on collapse

The absence of a cannabis program would matter less if Venezuela's health system otherwise functioned. It does not. Human Rights Watch's World Report 2026 found that emergency medical supply shortages reached 36 percent in 2024, according to a national hospital survey cited in the report, and that an estimated 86 percent of the population lived in poverty that year. As of November 2023, the United Nations estimated that 14.2 million people in Venezuela faced severe humanitarian needs. HRW documented that many Venezuelans have turned to self-medication and informal support networks simply to obtain ordinary medicines — a population already primed, by scarcity, to seek treatment outside formal channels. For patients who might otherwise ask a neurologist or oncologist about cannabis-based options that are now routine conversations in neighboring countries' clinics, there is neither a legal product to prescribe nor, in many cases, a functioning clinic offering alternatives.

That scarcity pushes patients toward the same clandestine cannabis markets that expose them to the criminal penalties described above: oil and flower of unknown potency, unknown contaminants, and no dosing guidance, obtained through informal networks rather than a pharmacy. It is a bind common to prohibition regimes generally, but acute in Venezuela's case: the less a state provides, the more patients depend on the market it refuses to regulate, and the more exposed they become to prosecution for that dependence.

## A closing door for advocacy, too

In other countries where medical cannabis access has expanded, the driving force has typically been organized patients — families of children with epilepsy in Brazil, patient collectives in Colombia and Mexico, cultivation cooperatives in Argentina and Uruguay — using courts, litigation, and public pressure to force reform. Venezuela's political environment makes that kind of organizing markedly harder. In November 2024, the government approved what human rights groups call the country's “anti-NGO law” — the Law on the Supervision, Regularization, Performance, and Financing of Non-Governmental and Related Organizations — which requires civil-society groups to register with the state, restricts foreign funding, and lets authorities deny authorization on vague political grounds. Amnesty International's Americas director, Ana Piquer, called it “a new attack by Nicolás Maduro's government against those fighting for human rights in Venezuela.”

The law was not written with cannabis patients in mind, but its effect reaches them: the associative model that has expanded access elsewhere in the region depends on precisely the kind of independent civil-society organizing that Venezuela's legal environment now constrains. No Venezuelan patient association has publicly emerged to press for cannabis reform the way Brazil's APEPI or Argentina's cultivation cooperatives have — not because Venezuelan patients' needs are different, but because the civic space, the legal opening, and the political will that made those movements possible elsewhere have no equivalent in Caracas. Until one of those conditions changes, Venezuelan patients are left exactly where the law has always put them: on the wrong side of it.
