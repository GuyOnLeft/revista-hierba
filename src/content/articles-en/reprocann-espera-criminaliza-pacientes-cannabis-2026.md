---
title: "REPROCANN: The Long Wait That Criminalizes Argentina's Medicinal Cannabis Patients"
excerpt: "More than 100,000 patients wait months or years to register with REPROCANN, as courts repeatedly fine the state for delays and the program moves under Argentina's anti-drug agency."
tag: "Criminalization"
---

In Argentina, the paperwork can outlast the patient. More than 100,000 people seeking legal authorization to grow or possess cannabis for medical use have piled up in the queue of REPROCANN, the national medicinal cannabis registry, some waiting more than a year for a government response that, under the country's own health law, should take weeks. For a region where Argentina's 2017 cannabis law was once held up as a regional model — cited by advocates in Peru, Colombia, and Brazil as proof that a health-first registry could work — the collapse of REPROCANN's processing system is now a cautionary tale about how administrative neglect can criminalize patients as effectively as a prohibition law, according to reporting from Infobae, Página/12, and the legal-affairs outlet El Heraldo.

## A Backlog Big Enough to Break a Law

REPROCANN was created under Law 27,350 to let patients with a doctor's recommendation register for personal or delegated cannabis cultivation, sparing them prosecution under Argentina's narcotics statute. By late 2025, according to figures the Health Ministry gave to Infobae, roughly 240,000 people held active authorizations — but some 100,000 more had been stuck in the application queue for up to 20 months. Nearly half of pending applicants were concentrated in Buenos Aires province, and the vast majority were adults between 27 and 59, according to the ministry's own breakdown.

Much of the jam traces to Resolution 1780/25, issued in May 2025, which required prescribing physicians to complete specific accredited training in cannabis-based medicine before they could certify patients. The rule shrank the pool of eligible doctors sharply: of roughly 5,800 physicians registered in the federal prescribers' network, only around 1,000 held the newly required credential, Infobae reported. Patients like Claudia, a 46-year-old REPROCANN user quoted by the outlet La Izquierda Diario while waiting on a permit renewal, described the effect bluntly: without a valid card, "me arrastran a la ilegalidad y la clandestinidad" — they drag me into illegality and clandestinity. Organizations working with patients, including the group Mamá Cultiva, said members with terminal diagnoses were left without medication because civil associations legally cannot hand over cannabis oil to anyone whose registration has lapsed.

## Courts Keep Ruling Against the State

Where the ministry stalled, Argentina's federal courts stepped in — repeatedly. In one case reported by El Heraldo, a Paraná resident submitted a complete REPROCANN application in January 2024 and received no answer after 424 business days. Federal Judge Daniel Alonso ultimately ordered the Health Ministry to resolve the case within ten days and, because the delay had forced the patient into litigation, assessed roughly 3.1 million pesos in legal costs against the state. The ruling was notable less for its outcome than for its familiarity: Argentine outlets including the legal-affairs site Diario Judicial and APFDigital have documented a running series of near-identical amparo rulings against the Health Ministry over REPROCANN delays dating back to at least 2024, with judges repeatedly finding, in Alonso's words, that "the sluggishness of the administration [was] amply demonstrated."

That judicial pressure, more than patient complaints alone, is widely credited — by Infobae's sourcing inside the ministry — with pushing the government to finally overhaul the registry's back end in the following months. It is a pattern regional legal-rights groups such as Dejusticia have flagged elsewhere in Latin American drug policy: courts functioning as the last line of defense for patients whose access rights exist on paper but not in administrative practice. The Washington Office on Latin America has made a similar point about the region more broadly, arguing that even where cannabis laws have been liberalized on paper, enforcement culture — police stops, administrative gatekeeping, selective prosecution — often continues to fall hardest on people who use the plant for health reasons rather than on large-scale traffickers.

It is worth being precise about what is established fact here and what is advocacy framing. That the ministry missed statutory deadlines and that courts repeatedly ruled against it are matters of judicial record. That the delays amount to a de facto human-rights violation, as patient organizations and some legal commentators argue, is an interpretation — a serious one, grounded in Argentina's constitutional right to health, but an argument rather than a court finding, since no ruling has yet declared the backlog itself unconstitutional as a systemic matter rather than case by case.

## From the Health Ministry to the Anti-Drug Agency

The politics around REPROCANN turned sharper in February 2025, when Security Minister Patricia Bullrich announced the government intended to cancel roughly 300,000 existing cannabis-cultivation permits outright, framing self-cultivation as a channel for diversion into the illegal market, according to Página/12. Patient organizations reacted immediately. Mamá Cultiva called medicinal cannabis "un derecho y una herramienta de salud pública" — a right and a public-health tool — and argued the security ministry had no business regulating it, according to Perfil's reporting on the response. Laura Caldentey, co-founder of Cannabis Medicinal Río Negro y Neuquén, went further, telling Perfil that stripping permits would "lanza[r] a todas estas personas a que sean perseguidas por la ley penal" — throw all these people back into criminal prosecution — and called the equation of home growers with traffickers "un error conceptual."

The mass cancellation was not ultimately carried out, but the structural shift Bullrich's announcement foreshadowed arrived anyway. Decree 27/2026 moved REPROCANN out of the Health Ministry's direct chain of command and into Sedronar, Argentina's national drug-policy secretariat — an agency historically built around drug-use prevention and control rather than patient care, as the outlet revistathc.com noted in its analysis of the decree. The change was framed by government sources as a technical integration of drug policy under one roof; patient organizations read it as a symbolic and practical demotion, placing people who use cannabis as medicine under an anti-narcotics lens rather than a public-health one.

## What "Automatic" Approval Actually Fixes

Facing both the litigation wave and public criticism, the ministry rolled out changes in the following months: a platform update in December 2025 letting stuck applicants cancel and refile, a dedicated module for the more than 1,400 registered patient associations, and, starting in late February 2026, automatic 24-hour approval for "low-risk" applications with complete paperwork from an accredited physician, according to Infobae and the outlet San Juan8. The backlog has since narrowed but not closed: San Juan8 reported more than 80,000 applications still pending nationally in February 2026, with mental-health cases and applicants under 21 still requiring manual review — the categories least able to absorb further delay.

For patients, the gap between authorization and cultivation still carries legal risk. Argentina's narcotics law continues to treat unregistered cultivation and possession as offenses, softened only by jurisprudence protecting personal use; a REPROCANN card, not the underlying medical need, is what separates a patient from a defendant. As Peru, Colombia, and Brazil each build out their own patient-registry systems for medicinal cannabis, Argentina's experience is becoming a specific, documented warning: a health-access law is only as strong as the bureaucracy — and the political will — behind the office that administers it.
