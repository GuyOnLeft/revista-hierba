---
title: "Argentina Keeps Its Medical Cannabis Agency Under Intervention for Another Year"
excerpt: "Decree 1147/2026 extends the intervention of ARICCAME, the body created in 2022 to regulate medical cannabis and industrial hemp. It has gone two years without its regular leadership, while Jujuy exports flower to Germany and the patient registry has changed hands."
tag: "Regulation"
---

On Monday, October 6, Argentina's Official Gazette published Decree 1147/2026, which keeps the Regulatory Agency for the Hemp and Medical Cannabis Industry (ARICCAME) under intervention for another year. Under an intervention, the executive branch sets aside an agency's regular leadership and appoints an administrator, the *interventor*, to run it. Ignacio Ferrari, a physician who has headed the agency since September 2024, will remain in that role with the powers granted by the original decree. The new term runs from the expiry of the previous one and can be extended for another year by a reasoned resolution of the Ministry of Economy's Secretariat of Industry, Commerce and Small and Medium-sized Enterprises.

Seen from northern Argentina, the news is less bureaucratic than it looks. ARICCAME is the body that Law 27,669, published in May 2022, created to regulate and authorize the entire medical cannabis and industrial hemp chain: import, export, cultivation, industrial production, manufacturing and sale. It has gone two years without the leadership that law provided for, and when this new term expires in 2027 it will have spent more time under intervention than under normal operation. All of this is happening while Jujuy exports tonnes of medical flower to Europe and the national patient registry changes hands.

## An intervention that was meant to last a year

The intervention began with Decree 833 of September 17, 2024. According to the recitals of a later resolution, that decree set a one-year term, "with the possibility of extension for an identical period and only once." Its stated goals were to reorganize the agency's internal workings, speed up procedures and issue licenses. The extension came with Resolution 464/2025 of the Secretariat of Industry and Commerce, which prolonged the intervention from September 3, 2025, citing a move to a "second phase" of hemp development focused on flower and biomass production.

In between, the government tried to shut the agency down. In July 2025, Decree 462/2025 ordered its dissolution along with that of INTA, INTI and other bodies in the economic sphere, and proposed moving medical cannabis to ANMAT, the national drug regulator, and hemp to the Secretariat of Industry and Commerce. The Chamber of Deputies (Resolution 95/2025) and the Senate (Resolution 56/2025) rejected that decree, and the executive restored the agency with Decree 627/2025 of September 2, 2025.

With the "only once" extension used up, a new decree was needed. The agricultural news site Bichos de Campo headlined it wryly: "It was for one year, but things happened."

## What the government says it has done

In the new decree's recitals, the government lists its achievements: the agency's "institutional reactivation," the creation of an interdisciplinary team, agricultural and industrial licenses for hemp seed, fiber and grain, and a special regime for propagation material of *Cannabis sativa* for medical use. According to the newspaper Primera Edición, that regime was approved in June 2026, and in September rules were set for horticultural hemp cultivation with traceability criteria.

The list of pending tasks is longer: adapting the agency's organizational and functional structure, continuing licensing processes, reviewing authorizations already granted, monitoring license holders, drafting the regulatory frameworks still missing, coordinating with the provinces and strengthening traceability. According to Economis, the Ministry of Economy will keep providing the agency with budget, accounting, procurement and human resources services until it can fully operate on its own.

Most of the reported progress concerns industrial hemp. On the medical side, the official account reported in the press mentions only the propagation-material regime. The specialist outlet Marimba noted that the extension highlights the regulatory gaps still affecting those waiting for complete frameworks and working licenses.

## Flower for Germany

On the same day as the decree, El Tribuno de Jujuy published a review of Cannava, Jujuy's state-owned medical cannabis company. The company exports between 85% and 90% of what it produces. In 2026 it shipped about three tonnes of medical cannabis products to Europe, mostly to Germany: THC flower for medical use, with GACP and GMP certifications. Its president, Ignacio Bruera, said that is "much more than double what Canada, Uruguay and Colombia export combined," a comparison Revista Hierba could not verify. CBD oil, by contrast, is "more oriented to the domestic market," according to the report.

Cannava plans to grow from three to fifteen tonnes in the coming years. It currently uses 8 of the 35 hectares available at El Pongo, has contracts signed through 2030 and aims to add the United Kingdom and Brazil as destinations.

The 2022 law also had the domestic market in mind. According to an analysis by the law firm Estudio O'Farrell, it amended Article 6 of Law 27,350 on medical cannabis so that health authorities could source medical-grade inputs from authorized domestic producers. That Jujuy's output goes mostly to export does not contradict the law, but it makes one question more visible: how much of what the country produces reaches patients here.

## On the other side, patients

The ARICCAME decree does not change access requirements or the Cannabis Program Registry (REPROCANN), as the news site Elonce pointed out: they are two separate tracks. But the registry changed this year too. Under Decree 27/2026, published on January 26, the program moved from the Ministry of Health to the Secretariat for Comprehensive Drug Policies (Sedronar). According to Infobae, the official aim is to strengthen the health, medical and scientific approach to therapeutic use under Law 27,350 and to tighten the program's traceability.

The transfer divided opinion. Marimba reported concern from groups that see Sedronar as an agency historically tied to the fight against drugs, as well as hopes from others for faster procedures and better coordination with harm-reduction programs. Specialists at the Cannabis Observatory of the National University of José C. Paz warned, according to La Bulla, that many people might not register for fear their data could be used in security policies.

The figures circulating in February were worrying. La Bulla reported that more than 80,000 registration and renewal applications were frozen, that response times had grown from about 21 days in 2022 to more than six months in 2025, and that the approval rate had fallen from 96% to under 50%. San Juan 8 described stricter requirements since 2025, such as requiring doctors to hold a diploma or master's degree in medical cannabis and requiring criminal record checks for people who grow for others. The same outlet cited a ruling in which a federal judge ordered the Ministry of Health to pay the legal fees of an *amparo* (a fast-track constitutional complaint) because, "in the face of the defendant's unjustified delay," the applicant "was forced to turn to the courts." In April, Revista THC reported that the program had introduced automated approvals for some profiles. Revista Hierba found no more recent official data on the status of those applications.

## What comes next

ARICCAME's new term expires in September 2027 and can be stretched to 2028. The government's own list of pending tasks, covering regulatory frameworks, coordination with the provinces, traceability and the review of licenses, offers a yardstick for that year. Primera Edición put it in concrete terms: regulation is proven by resolved applications and controls that work.

For people in Argentina who grow, research or are treated with cannabis, the underlying question is still the one from 2022: whether the industry that law envisioned will also supply the country's own patients. For now, the agency that should be shaping that answer is still without its regular leadership.
