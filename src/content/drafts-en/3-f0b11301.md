A study carried out at eleven pediatric neurology centers in Argentina and Chile, from the Garrahan hospital in Buenos Aires to public hospitals in Salta, Tucumán and Mendoza, brings local data to a question many families ask: whether cannabidiol (CBD) can help children whose epilepsy does not respond to medication. The paper, published on September 29 in the journal *Epilepsy & Behavior*, followed 79 children with drug-resistant focal epilepsies for an average of 30 months while they received purified CBD on top of their usual treatment.

According to the abstract, 43 of the 79 children (54.4%) cut the frequency of their seizures by 50% or more, and four (5.1%) became seizure-free. It is an encouraging result for patients with few options, but it comes from an analysis without a comparison group, and that shapes how it should be read.

Besides the Garrahan, hospitals and clinics in Salta, Tucumán, Mendoza, Rosario, Corrientes, Paraná, Comodoro Rivadavia, Neuquén and Gualeguaychú took part, along with Clínica Alemana in Santiago de Chile.

## What was studied

Focal epilepsies are those that start in one area of the brain. The International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) recognizes a group of childhood focal epilepsies called "self-limited," such as self-limited epilepsy with centrotemporal spikes, which tend to resolve on their own. This study dealt with the others, in children who had already tried at least four antiseizure medications without bringing their seizures under control.

It is a retrospective analysis: the team reviewed the medical records of patients who had already been treated. The dose was adjusted for each child according to response and tolerability, averaging 34 milligrams per kilogram of body weight per day (range 10 to 40). The response held steady at the 12-, 18-, 24- and 30-month check-ups. There were no significant differences between children with tuberous sclerosis complex, a genetic disorder in which focal seizures predominate, and the rest. Nor was response linked to seizure type, age or the other drugs the children were taking. Among responders, abnormalities in the EEG between seizures also improved.

Twenty-three children (29.1%) had adverse events, mostly mild, transient and manageable with dose adjustments; drowsiness was the most common. No serious treatment-related adverse events were recorded. The authors conclude, cautiously, that purified CBD was associated with sustained seizure reduction and good long-term tolerability, and that this supports further evaluation in this population.

## An Argentine line of research

The study continues a series by the same group, led by neurologist Roberto Caraballo of the Garrahan. In 2025, in the same journal, they published a follow-up of 551 children with developmental and epileptic encephalopathies, severe forms in which epilepsy and its cause affect development, treated between 2021 and 2024 at ten centers: 50.6% cut their seizures by 50% or more and 14.2% became seizure-free. All of them received Convupidiol, a purified CBD solution made by the Alef Medical laboratory and approved by Argentina's drug regulator, ANMAT, in 2020 for Lennox-Gastaut and Dravet syndromes and for tuberous sclerosis complex. The abstract of the new paper does not specify which product was used.

That study also exposed limits: 29% of the children stopped treatment because of lack of response, intolerance or difficulty keeping it up, and in some the effect wore off over time. The authors of both papers declared no conflicts of interest, and the 2025 paper states that it received no specific funding.

## What the study cannot say

Without a comparison group, it is impossible to separate the effect of CBD from other factors, such as the course of the disease, changes in medication or the placebo effect. The controlled trial closest to this type of seizure shows why. In GWPCARE6, published in *JAMA Neurology* in 2021, 224 patients with tuberous sclerosis complex received CBD or placebo for 16 weeks: at 25 mg/kg/day, seizures fell by 48.6%, but in the placebo group they also fell by 26.5%. Doubling the dose to 50 mg/kg/day added no benefit (47.5%) and increased diarrhea and drowsiness. Of those taking CBD, 18.9% had elevated liver enzymes, compared with none on placebo. The authors noted at the time that CBD's efficacy in conditions with primarily focal seizures did not yet appear to be established.

The dose also deserves attention. The Argentine study's average of 34 mg/kg/day exceeds the maximum recommended in the U.S. label for Epidiolex, the reference purified CBD: 20 mg/kg/day for Lennox-Gastaut and Dravet and 25 for tuberous sclerosis complex. According to that label, liver enzyme elevations are dose-related and more frequent with valproate, and drowsiness increases with clobazam, two drugs taken by 85% and 62% of the children, respectively, in the group's 2025 study. The label calls for checking liver enzymes before starting and at one, three and six months of treatment.

A Chilean study published on October 6 adds a nuance from the lab. Researchers at the Pontifical Catholic University of Chile and Arturo Prat University in Iquique induced prolonged seizures in mice with pilocarpine and gave them a single dose of CBD or THC. According to the paper, published in *Neuroscience*, CBD did not change seizure severity; THC reduced it, but with marked sedation, and neither reversed the changes in the brain's electrical activity. The authors conclude that an acute dose of cannabinoids is not enough to reduce seizures. It is an animal model and measures something else, an ongoing seizure; what the hospitals evaluated was a daily treatment over months, not a way to stop a seizure.

## Why it matters here

In Argentina, the approved pharmaceutical CBD has narrow indications, and in focal epilepsies other than tuberous sclerosis complex its use falls outside them. Coverage, meanwhile, is often decided in court. In March 2026, a federal court in San Martín ordered PAMI, the national health insurer for retirees and pensioners, to cover a cannabidiol medicine for a patient with severe intellectual disability and drug-resistant epilepsy. Earlier, Argentina's Supreme Court had ordered IOSPER, the health insurer of Entre Ríos province, to cover 100% of a cannabis oil for a minor with drug-resistant epilepsy, after the insurer argued that no scientific evidence supported the treatment. In those disputes, evidence is part of the argument, which is why data from the country's own hospitals matter, with their limits in plain view.

One more caveat is needed. The results apply to pharmaceutical-grade purified CBD, dosed in milligrams per kilogram, and do not carry over to oils of unknown composition. The laboratory of the School of Biochemical Sciences at the National University of Rosario reported in 2019 that seven out of ten samples it received contained less than 1 milligram per milliliter of CBD or THC. The chromatography laboratory at the PROBIEN institute (CONICET–National University of Comahue) has also found oils with no cannabinoids at all.

The next step was named by the authors themselves in their 2025 paper: large prospective studies to confirm these results and, eventually, expand the approved indications. Until then, the eleven-center series is a consistent signal, produced in hospitals in the region, that still needs to pass the test of a controlled trial.
