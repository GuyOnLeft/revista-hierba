On September 25, Chile's Investigations Police (PDI) reported seizing 16.5 kilos of coca leaves in Coihueco, in the Ñuble region. The man detained, a 19-year-old Bolivian, was remanded in custody by the Chillán Court of Guarantees. According to the newspaper La Discusión, the leaves were meant for Bolivians and Peruvians doing farm work in the area. Six days later, at dawn on October 1, soldiers posted on the Bolivian border at Colchane told the Carabineros police that a woman and her 17-year-old son were walking with two large bags. They were carrying 17.7 kilos of leaves in their natural state. Both were detained for breaking Law 20,000, Chile's drug law.

Read from Jujuy or Salta, where chewing coca is part of everyday life, the two stories show something that repeats all along the Andes: the same plant, *Erythroxylum coca*, changes legal status every time it crosses a border.

## One leaf, three laws

In Argentina, Article 15 of Law 23,737, passed in 1989, is explicit: "The possession and consumption of coca leaves in their natural state intended for the practice of coqueo or chewing, or for use as an infusion, shall not be considered possession or consumption of narcotics." But the law never settled how the leaf reaches the people who use it. Importing it is still banned under Decree 648/78, issued by the last military dictatorship, so supply depends on smuggling. On September 21, for example, the Gendarmería stopped a truck in Santiago del Estero that had left La Quiaca with 420 kilos of leaves, and the driver was charged under the Customs Code, according to TodoJujuy.

In Chile, the leaf is an illegal drug. A study by Claudia Levine for the Fundación Lobeliana, from May 2025, explains that the regulations of Law 20,000 place it among substances that do not cause "serious effects": the penalties are lighter, but it can still be prosecuted as trafficking. Only private personal use is allowed; buying, carrying or transporting it is not. Because the plant does not grow in northern Chile, the leaf comes from Bolivia or Peru. The Agriculture and Livestock Service (SAG) allows it into the country but sets no quantity, so at the border, the study says, whether to detain someone is up to the "police officer on duty." Anyone who fails to declare it can also end up detained. After the Coihueco operation, the PDI noted that the leaf is regulated by Law 20,000 "regardless of the fact that its consumption has a cultural and traditional dimension in countries such as Bolivia and Peru," Radio Contacto reported.

Bolivia, by contrast, protects the practice. In 2011 it withdrew from the 1961 Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs, which required traditional chewing to be eliminated within 25 years, according to the Transnational Institute (TNI). It rejoined with a reservation, ratified by Law 392 of 2013, that allows within its territory "the traditional chewing of the Coca Leaf, and the consumption and use of the Coca Leaf in its natural state, for cultural and medicinal purposes."

## What the WHO found

Coqueo, acullico or picchado, as it is called in northern Chile, is not simply chewing. The Lobeliana study describes it this way: dry leaves are gathered in the mouth into a wad, often with an alkaline "activator" such as baking soda or plant ash, and held in the cheek for a long time. The leaf is also used as medicine, at religious festivals and as an offering. It contains 1% or less of the cocaine alkaloid, according to The Guardian, and it takes about 1,000 kilos of leaves to obtain 1.4 kilos of cocaine base paste, according to ICEERS, a foundation that studies psychoactive plants.

The World Health Organization (WHO) put the leaf through a critical review, pushed for by Bolivia and Colombia. Its Expert Committee on Drug Dependence, meeting from October 20 to 23, 2025, recommended keeping it in Schedule I of the 1961 Convention, the most restrictive. According to an analysis by Martin Jelsma and John Walsh published by WOLA, the experts contracted by the WHO itself concluded that the review "revealed no evidence of clinically significant harm to public health associated with coca leaf use" and that the leaf "is not associated with significant dependence or abuse potential." The committee relied on the criterion of "convertibility": cocaine is made from the leaf. For Jelsma and Walsh, the decision "perpetuates injustices." Because the WHO proposed no change, there was no vote at the UN Commission on Narcotic Drugs in March 2026.

"We have been using coca without any problems since the time of our ancestors," Daynor Choque, leader of the commercial arm of the coca producers of Coripata, east of La Paz, told The Guardian. "Now, producers are being pushed into the illegal cocaine market just to survive."

## The kilos that decide

In Chilean courts, the line between custom and crime is usually drawn by the scale. Levine's study reviews several cases. In 2006, two Quechua-speaking women who had moved from the Bolivian altiplano to Calama spent almost six months in jail for carrying 5.44 kilos for a religious festival, before being acquitted. In Arica, a court acquitted a man carrying 16.85 kilos in 2015; in 2012 it had convicted another carrying 57. In 2022, a Quechua Bolivian prosecuted in Santiago spent ten months deprived of liberty over 500 kilos intended, as in Coihueco, for fellow farmworkers from his country. In 2023, two Aymara Bolivians spent about a year and five months in pretrial detention over 113.85 kilos meant for the market in Calama, and ended up accepting probation to avoid a trial in which they faced five to fifteen years in prison.

No law sets the quantity, but from those rulings, Levine concludes, it follows that around 15 kilos might still be presumed cultural use; above that, the leaf tends to be seen as raw material for cocaine. And that protection only reaches those who can prove they are Indigenous: for everyone else, the leaf is treated like any other illegal drug. The study speaks of "a double system of law enforcement." The loads seized in Coihueco and Colchane were just above that reference point.

## What comes next

In Chile, the only bill to decriminalize carrying and using dry leaf (bulletin 16381) was introduced in 2023 and, as of May 2025, was still at its first stage "without much prospect of approval," according to the study. In Argentina, Jujuy deputies Jorge Rizzotti and María Inés Zigarán introduced a bill in June to authorize the import and sale of leaf in its natural state for chewing and infusion, exclude it from Law 23,737 and repeal Decree 648/78. It provides for a registry of importers, entry points in Jujuy, Salta and Formosa, and packages of up to 200 grams, according to Parlamentario. Its authors estimate more than one and a half million users in the country and sum up the problem in one sentence: "The legal system allows the consumption of a product whose entry is prohibited." Revista Hierba found no record that the bill has advanced since.

Meanwhile, for the woman and the teenager detained in Colchane, and for the young man in pretrial detention in Ñuble, what the WHO found about the leaf weighs less than the kilos they were carrying.
